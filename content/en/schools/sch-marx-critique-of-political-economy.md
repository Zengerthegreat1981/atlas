# Marx's Critique of Political Economy

Karl Marx's principal work carries its subtitle precisely: *Capital: A Critique of Political Economy* (*Das Kapital: Kritik der politischen Ökonomie*). Marx does not write an alternative political economy to replace those of Smith and Ricardo so much as an **internal critique** of them. He takes their methodological tools, above all the labour theory of value, and pushes them to conclusions that the classical economists themselves did not intend.

## From Ricardo to Marx: Pushing the Theory of Value to Its Limit

Marx regarded Ricardo as the most profound and original representative of classical political economy, because Ricardo took the labour theory of value with full scientific rigour. But Marx saw that Ricardo stopped at analysing prices and distribution among fixed classes, without asking the deeper question: **where does profit come from at all, if goods are exchanged at their fair values?** Marx's answer is the concept of **surplus value**. The worker sells labour power at its value (the subsistence wage), but in a working day this power produces a value greater than its own. The difference, which the capitalist appropriates without equivalent, is the source of profit.

## From Surplus Value to Accumulation and Crisis

On this foundation Marx built a dynamic analysis of capitalism that the relatively static classical economics lacked: **the law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall**. Competition drives capitalists to replace living labour with machines continually in pursuit of a competitive advantage. But this very substitution shrinks the share of living labour, the sole source of surplus value for Marx, in the total capital invested. Over time it presses down the overall rate of profit, producing recurrent crises rather than a stable equilibrium.

## Primitive Accumulation: How Capitalism Began

In the final part of the first volume of *Capital*, Marx addresses a historical question that classical economics had neglected: how capitalism originated. Marx rejects the classical narrative in which some people saved wisely and so accumulated capital while others spent. In its place he proposes the concept of **primitive accumulation**: the separation of direct producers (peasants) from their means of production (land) by force and law. The clearest example is the enclosure movement (Enclosures) in England. This created a class of workers who owned nothing but their labour power and were compelled to sell it on the market.

## Critical Continuity, Not Rupture

It is important to note that Marx's critique was not a complete rejection of classical political economy, but a critical continuation of it. Marx uses Ricardo's own analytical method (the labour theory of value) to expose, from within that same method, contradictions that he argues classical economics could not see. This was because of its implicit assumption that capitalism is a natural, eternal system rather than a transient historical one. This entry addresses that angle specifically: the critique of value and accumulation, rather than Marx's general philosophy or historical materialism. The full intellectual biography of Marx and the development of philosophical Marxism are left to their own entries (Karl Marx and Classical Marxism).

## A Khaldunian Echo in the Labour Theory of Value

Some comparative scholars note a resemblance between Marx's idea of labour as the source of value and what Ibn Khaldun put forward in the *Muqaddimah*: that human labour is the source of gain and that accumulated wealth is itself rooted in earlier labour. This resemblance is usually presented in the literature on the history of economic thought as a parallel intellectual precedence, not as a documented direct historical influence.

## The Structure of the Full Project: Three Volumes, One Question

Marx planned *Capital* as a multi-part project, of which he himself completed only the first volume (1867). Friedrich Engels assembled and published the second (1885) and third (1894) volumes from Marx's manuscripts after his death. The first volume treats the direct process of capitalist production (the theory of value and surplus). The second treats the process of circulation and expanded reproduction, which Rosa Luxemburg later drew on in her analysis of imperialism. The third treats the "overall process of capitalist production," including the law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall.

## A Critique of "Vulgar" Economics, Not Classical Economics Alone

Marx carefully distinguishes between the "scientific" classical political economy of Smith and Ricardo, which he respected for its analytical seriousness despite disagreeing with it, and what he called "vulgar economy" (Vulgar Economy), which flourished after Ricardo. Marx held that vulgar economy abandoned analytical seriousness in favour of direct ideological justification of existing capitalist relations. It was content to describe surface phenomena, such as market prices, without seeking to uncover the deeper structural relations behind them. This distinction makes clear that Marx's critique targets a particular apologetic methodology, not all classical economic thought.

## The Dialectical Method of Presentation

In presenting *Capital*, Marx adopted a method that set out the economic categories (the commodity, money, capital) in a progressive logical-historical order. He started from the simplest form of economic relation (the exchange of two commodities) and proceeded gradually to the most complex forms (the full circuit of productive capital). This reflects his methodological debt to Hegel's philosophical dialectic, though Marx transformed it from an analysis of abstract ideas into an analysis of actual material social relations.
