# School of Mind-Heart (Xinxue / Lu-Wang)

Xinxue ("learning of the heart or mind") is the Neo-Confucian school that makes the heart-mind (xin) the locus of the moral principle and the source of good knowledge. It is ascribed to Lu Jiuyuan (1139–1193) and Wang Yangming (1472–1529), and so is also called the Lu-Wang school. It stands opposed to the school of Zhu Xi (Cheng-Zhu), which made principle (li) an object of external study, grasped by investigating things. For the adherents of Xinxue, morality is not acquired by pursuing things outside the self, but is drawn out from a person's innate conscience after it has been purified of selfishness.

## Lu Jiuyuan and the Beginning

Lu Jiuyuan was born in Jiangxi province and was known by the title Xiangshan, after a mountain where he lived and taught. He adopted Mencius's saying that every person has a heart that is good by nature, and said that "the universe is my heart and my heart is the universe" and that "the heart is principle". He held that the fundamental effort is to establish the moral root (establishing the will), and that much exposition and pursuit of texts scatters a person. In 1175 he met Zhu Xi at the Goose Lake Temple (Ehu) in Jiangxi, and they differed over the method of teaching: Zhu Xi begins with broad study and then arrives at the essence, whereas Lu begins with the essence and then broadens. Lu did not leave a large organized following in his own time; his teaching was revived and developed by Wang Yangming three centuries later, who acknowledged the affinity but gave the doctrine a more systematic form through the concepts of innate knowing and the unity of knowledge and action. The main difference is one of emphasis: Lu stresses recovering the original mind directly and establishing the will, while Wang adds a developed account of moral action and of the investigation of things as rectifying the mind.

## Wang Yangming

Wang Shouren (Yangming) was born in Zhejiang. He served as an official and military commander and won a victory over the rebellion of Prince Ning in 1519. He was exiled to Guizhou after offending the powerful eunuch Liu Jin, and at Longchang in 1508 he experienced what was called "enlightenment": that principle is not sought from external things but is in the heart-mind. His sayings were collected in the *Instructions for Practical Living* (Chuanxi lu). He formulated what was later called the "Four-Sentence Teaching": there is neither good nor evil in the substance of the mind; in the movement of intention there is good and evil; innate knowing knows good and evil; and the investigation of things means doing good and removing evil.

## Central Theses

- The heart-mind is principle (xin ji li): principle is not outside the heart-mind, and moral knowledge is not completed by transferring knowledge from outside.
- Innate knowing (liangzhi): an expression that appears in Mencius, meaning the moral capacity by which a person knows good and hates evil without learning. "Extending innate knowing" (zhi liangzhi) is the goal of effort.
- The unity of knowledge and action (zhi xing he yi): true knowledge includes action, and action is the completion of knowledge, so whoever knew the good and did not do it did not truly know it.
- The investigation of things (ge wu): he interpreted it as rectifying what is in the intention, not field investigation of things as Zhu Xi did. The story is told of his attempt to contemplate bamboo for seven days until he fell ill, whereupon he abandoned Zhu Xi's path.
- The unity of human beings and the world: the virtuous person sees heaven, earth and all beings as one body.

## Branches and Disagreement

After Wang's death his followers divided: within the "Zhejiang school", Qian Dehong and Wang Ji differed over the Four-Sentence Teaching: Qian accepted it as the master's doctrine, while Wang Ji doubted that it was the ultimate formulation and carried "neither good nor evil" further, in a direction closer to Buddhism; the "Jiangyou school" (Nie Bao and Luo Hongxian) stressed effort and discipline; and the "Taizhou school", founded by Wang Gen (1483–1541), spread among the common people and reached the furthest critique of the establishment with Li Zhi (1527–1602). Liu Zongzhou (1578–1645) then came and criticized the tendency to "wildness" among some of them, stressed "watchfulness in solitude" (shen du), and fasted until he died after the fall of the Ming. Critics in the seventeenth century, among them Gu Yanwu, said that "empty talk about the heart-mind and nature" weakened the state, and the "Kaozheng" school returned to textual research.

## Influence in East Asia and the Islamic World

The school passed to Japan, where it became known as "Yōmeigaku" through Nakae Tōju (1608–1648) and Kumazawa Banzan (1619–1691), and inspired Ōshio Heihachirō in the Osaka uprising of 1837 and figures of the late Tokugawa movement that led to the Meiji Restoration, such as Yoshida Shōin (1830–1859). In Korea Yi Hwang (Toegye) criticized it in the sixteenth century, while Jeong Jedu (1649–1736) adopted it. In the twentieth century New Confucian thinkers such as Mou Zongsan and Tang Junyi revived it. Muslim China produced an attempt to connect Islam and Neo-Confucianism: Wang Daiyu and Liu Zhi (c. 1660–1730) wrote in Chinese, drawing on Persian Islamic Sufi works such as those of Najm al-Dīn Rāzī and Jāmī and comparing them with the ideas of heart-mind and principle in Neo-Confucianism, as Sachiko Murata shows in her study.

## Limits

Adherents of Zhu Xi criticized the school for lowering the value of study and acquisition, and because its phrasing is close to Zen Buddhism, especially "neither good nor evil". Others held that reliance on moral intuition opens the door to subjectivity and to rationalization, and it is hard to distinguish the innate disposition from caprice. There is no stability in the attributions: some of Wang's sayings are ascribed to his disciples, and the books of the *Instructions* were collected by his followers.
