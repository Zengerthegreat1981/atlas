# Kyoto School

A Japanese philosophical current that took shape around Kyoto Imperial University from the first decade of the twentieth century. It worked to build a philosophy in dialogue with the Western heritage (Kant, Hegel, Nietzsche, Husserl, and Heidegger) on the basis of Buddhist experience, especially Zen and the Pure Land, and it made "nothingness", not "being", the source of truth and the center of thought. The first to use the name "Kyoto School" was the philosopher Tosaka Jun in 1932, as a criticism, and it was then accepted by those it described. It was the first philosophical school of worldwide standing in modern Japan, and it tried to build a Japanese philosophical language with terms coined to correspond to Western concepts.

## The Founder and the First Generation

- **Nishida Kitarō** (1870–1945): taught at Kyoto University from 1910 (full professor in 1913), practiced Zen at the Daitokuji monastery, and was a lifelong friend of D. T. Suzuki. His first book, *An Inquiry into the Good* (Zen no kenkyū), appeared in 1911 and contains the idea of "pure experience", which precedes the split between subject and object. He then published *Intuition and Reflection in Self-Consciousness* (1917) and *From the Acting to the Seeing* (1927), which contains the concept of "place" (basho), then *The Self-Determination of Nothingness* (1932), and, the last thing he wrote, "The Logic of Place and the Religious Worldview" (1945).
- **Tanabe Hajime** (1885–1962): joined Kyoto in 1919 and studied under Husserl and Heidegger in Germany. In the 1930s he criticized Nishida's theory for making realization immediate and unmediated, and formulated the "logic of species" (Shu no ronri, 1934–1941), which makes society and the state a mediator between the individual and the universal. After the war he wrote *Philosophy as Metanoetics* (1946) on repentance and salvation by "other-power", influenced by Shinran.
- **Watsuji Tetsurō** (1889–1960): an associate who taught at Kyoto (1925–1934) before moving to Tokyo; author of *Climate and Culture* (Fūdo, 1935) and *Ethics* (Rinrigaku, 1937–1949), which defined the human being as a being "in between" (aida), that is, in relations.
- **Miki Kiyoshi** (1897–1945): a student of Heidegger who tried to combine Marxism, existentialism, and history, and died in prison in September 1945 after the end of the war.
- **Tosaka Jun** (1900–1945): a materialist critic of the school, who died in prison in August 1945.

## The Second Generation and Postwar

- **Nishitani Keiji** (1900–1990): wrote *Religion and Nothingness* (1961), which contains an analysis of Western nihilism and its relation to the Buddhist "emptiness" (sunyata).
- **Kosaka Masaaki, Koyama Iwao, Nishitani Keiji, and the historian Suzuki Shigetaka**: took part in the round-table discussions published in the journal *Chūō Kōron* (1941–1943); Nishitani and Suzuki also took part in the "Overcoming Modernity" symposium (1942).
- **Hisamatsu Shin'ichi** (1889–1980) and Masao Abe (1915–2006): carried the philosophy of Zen into dialogue with Christianity and Western philosophy.
- **Ueda Shizuteru** (1926–2019): one of the most prominent students of the second generation, who interpreted Zen through Heidegger and Eckhart.

## Central Concepts

- **Pure experience**: direct experience before any split between knower and known, which is the basis of reality.
- **Place (basho) and absolute nothingness**: beings manifest in a "place" that is absolute nothingness, which is not an absence but a horizon in which being and relative nothingness appear.
- **Logic of species**: the role of society and history in the realization of the universal.
- **Metanoetics**: a philosophy based on the "bankruptcy of reason" and then its rising through faith grounded in other-power.
- **Emptiness (sunyata)**: the way out of nihilism by passing through nothingness itself, not by overcoming it.

## The Political Controversy

Some figures of the school were involved in wartime discourse: Nishida wrote "The Problem of Japanese Culture" (1940), Tanabe lectured on the logic of species and the state (his postwar *Metanoetics* was written partly as self-criticism), and Kosaka and Nishitani took part in symposia that later critics regarded as support for the ideology of Japanese expansion (the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere). David Williams, in *Defending Japan's Pacific War* (2005), re-examined these positions as an attempt to criticize the West from within nationalist discourse. Others have argued that their texts resisted military censorship.

## Influence

- The school attracted growing interest in the West from the later twentieth century through the work of Masao Abe, James Heisig, Christopher Ives, and others, and it contributed to the dialogue among Buddhism, philosophy, and Christianity.
- A generation of the "Post-Kyoto School" later appeared, which rereads its heritage critically and raises the question of the relation between Japanese philosophy and the West in a language that does not reduce Japan to "nothingness" and "the East". The school became a subject of study in departments of Japanese and comparative philosophy in Europe, America, and Asia.

## Limits and Criticism

- Critics from the Marxist current (Tosaka) held that the conception of absolute nothingness evades the social and historical structure.
- Buddhist thinkers, such as the followers of "Critical Buddhism" (Hakamaya and Matsumoto), objected that the idea of a single origin or "place" is closer to a substantialist philosophy.
- Some historians held that the school relied on generalizations about "East" and "West" that reproduced the Orientalist division.
