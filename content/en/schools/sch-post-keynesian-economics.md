# Post-Keynesian Economics

**Post-Keynesian economics** is a critical school of macroeconomics that arose from the conviction of a group of economists, most prominently Joan Robinson at Cambridge, that the neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis that became academically dominant after the Second World War had drained the original ideas of John Maynard Keynes of their radicalism. In their view, that synthesis reduced Keynes to a special case within the general neoclassical framework, rather than presenting his work as a radical theoretical alternative to it.

## Genuine Uncertainty, Not Calculable Risk

Post-Keynesians hold that the central concept neglected in the dominant reading of Keynes is **uncertainty** in its fundamental sense. Capital investment decisions are taken in the face of a future whose probabilities cannot be calculated statistically in any objective sense. This differs from the "risk" that neoclassical economics treats as statistically insurable. This fundamental uncertainty is what makes the expectations of businesspeople, and what Keynes called "animal spirits," a basic driver of investment fluctuations, rather than a mere rational and calculated response to interest rates.

## Michał Kalecki: A Parallel, Marx-Influenced Route

The Polish economist Michał Kalecki developed results similar to Keynes's on effective demand, independently of Keynes and shortly before the publication of *The General Theory*. His approach, however, integrated class analysis and the distribution of income between profits and wages, and linked the capitalist business cycle to the collective investment decisions of capitalists. The Kaleckian reading deeply influenced the most radical branch of the post-Keynesian school and offered a theoretical bridge between Keynes and Marx that has remained a subject of ongoing academic interest.

## Hyman Minsky: Structural Financial Instability

Hyman Minsky made one of the school's most important original contributions: the **financial instability hypothesis**. It holds that long periods of economic stability generate their own rising financial fragility. As the apparent stability persists, borrowers and lenders gradually accept higher risks, until the financial structure reaches a critical point at which a relatively small shock causes it to collapse. The idea was widely rediscovered after the global financial crisis of 2008.

## The Cambridge Capital Controversy

Joan Robinson, Piero Sraffa (whose *Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities*, 1960, was a key text) and Nicholas Kaldor formed the British wing of the **Cambridge capital controversy** against economists at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow). They criticized the neoclassical concept of "capital" as a single homogeneous quantity that can be measured independently of the prices of distribution. Their argument was that this concept logically undermines the marginal productivity theory of capital, on which the entire neoclassical theory of distribution is built.

## Growth and Distribution in Kaldor and Robinson

Nicholas Kaldor and Joan Robinson developed growth models as alternatives to the standard neoclassical model (Solow's). These models link the growth rate directly to the shares of profits and wages in national income, rather than relying only on individual saving and an assumed external technological progress. They argued that investment decisions and the distribution of income among classes are fundamental determinants of economic growth and cannot be separated from its analysis.

## A Marginal Yet Influential Position After 2008

The post-Keynesian school remained relatively marginal in mainstream academic economics throughout the dominance of monetarism and new classical economics, from the 1970s to the turn of the millennium. The global financial crisis of 2008 renewed interest in its ideas, particularly Minsky's financial instability hypothesis. These ideas appeared better able to explain a financial crisis arising from internal fragility within the financial sector itself than the dominant models, which had largely neglected the financial sector in their macroeconomic analysis before the crisis.

## Internal Diversity: Not One Homogeneous School

Historians of economic thought note that "post-Keynesian economics" is an umbrella covering distinct branches rather than a single homogeneous intellectual bloc. There is a British Cambridge branch (Robinson and Kaldor) focused on growth and distribution; a Sraffian branch that rebuilds price theory on classical foundations; a Kaleckian branch that integrates class analysis; and an American monetary branch (Minsky) focused on financial fragility. Despite sharing a critique of the neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis, this internal diversity made the school less methodologically coherent than its more unified rivals, such as monetarism or new classical economics.

## Specialized Institutions and Journals

Representatives of the school established their own research institutions and specialized academic journals (the most prominent being the *Journal of Post Keynesian Economics*, founded in 1978). These provided publication outlets independent of the main economics journals, which were gradually dominated by new classical methodology. The step reflected an awareness of the need for separate academic infrastructure to sustain this critical tradition within a field increasingly dominated by competing schools.
