# Early Buddhism

The first Buddhist teachings before the sectarian divisions, that is, during the life of Gautama Buddha and the few generations after him up to the Council of Vaishali about a century after his death. It was distinguished by its focus on direct experience and a practical treatment of suffering, and by its turning away from metaphysical questions that do not lead to liberation. It is the origin from which the later Buddhist schools arose, from Theravada to Mahayana and Vajrayana.

## The Founder and Context

Siddhartha Gautama was born of the Shakya clan in Kapilavastu on the borders of present-day Nepal. Traditionally his life is dated between 563 and 483 BCE, but modern research, including the Göttingen conference (1988) published by Heinz Bechert, tends to place his death in the fifth or early fourth century BCE. Tradition relates that he left palace life at twenty-nine, attained enlightenment at thirty-five under the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya, and taught for about forty-five years until his death at Kushinagar. His teaching came in the climate of the Shramanas, the wandering ascetics of the Ganges valley, who denied the authority of the Vedas and offered different solutions for salvation, as did the Jains, the Ajivikas, and the materialists.

## Central Ideas

- **The Four Noble Truths**: dukkha (suffering), samudaya (its cause, which is attachment, desire, and ignorance), nirodha (its cessation through nirvana), and magga (the path to that).
- **The Eightfold Path**: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration, grouped under three trainings: ethics, meditation, and wisdom.
- **The Three Marks**: anicca (impermanence), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), and anatta (no substantial self). On them rests the analysis of the human being into five aggregates (skandhas): body, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness, none of which contains a fixed self.
- **Dependent origination (paticca samuppada)**: a chain of twelve links explaining the arising of suffering from ignorance to old age and death, bearing a general rule: if this exists, that exists.
- **Karma and samsara**: intentional actions produce a moral effect, and births are renewed as long as there is ignorance and attachment.
- **The Middle Way**: between indulgence in pleasures and excessive asceticism.
- **The unanswered questions (avyakata)**: ten questions that the Buddha refrained from answering, among them whether the world is eternal, whether it is finite, whether the self is the body, and whether the Tathagata exists after death. The reason for refraining, in the *Malunkyaputta* discourse, is that they do not help on the path to liberation; it is like a man struck by a poisoned arrow who refused to have it removed before he learned the name of the archer and the type of his bow.

## Texts and Compilation

The teachings were transmitted orally in the community of monks. The first council was held at Rajagriha after the death, and the second at Vaishali, in which a dispute over monastic rules arose; according to some traditions this led to the first major split, between the Sthaviras and the Mahasanghikas, though the causes and dating of the schism are debated. The "Tipitaka" became three baskets: the Sutta (discourses), the Vinaya (monastic rules), and the Abhidhamma (systematic analysis, which is late). The Pali collection was written down in Sri Lanka in the first century BCE, and no written texts remain from the time of the Buddha, so textual study rests on comparison between the Pali and Chinese (Agama) collections.

## Disciples and Community

Among his direct disciples were Mahakassapa, who presided over the first council; Ananda, who memorized the discourses and, through the intercession of Prajapati, secured the admission of nuns; Sariputta, in wisdom; Moggallana, in meditative powers; Anuruddha; and Upali, who recited the Vinaya. The monks and nuns established a community (sangha) that relied on alms and wandering, then residence during the rainy season. In the third century BCE the Emperor Ashoka spread the teaching and sent missions to Sri Lanka and the northwest, a step that is generally seen as important in Buddhism's spread beyond its region of origin.

## Influence and the Connection to Arab Heritage

Early Buddhism gave rise to all the later schools, and within it there remains a debate between those who see in it an empirical ethical philosophy and those who see in it a complete religion. The Arab-Islamic heritage has had an old knowledge of it: Ibn al-Nadim mentioned the "budd" of India in *Al-Fihrist*; the life of the Buddha was transmitted in the form of the Ismaili story of "Bilawhar and Budhasaf," edited by Daniel Gimaret, and al-Shahrastani recounted it in *Al-Milal wa-l-Nihal* when speaking of the "people of the budds"; and al-Biruni in *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind* (c. 1030) devoted pages to transmigration and to Buddhism, which he called among the people of Khurasan the "Sumaniyya." These descriptions agree in highlighting the ascetic negation of the world, but they may confuse books and doctrines.

## Limits and Criticism

- The texts were not written down during the Buddha's life, so what has reached us is the product of a long oral tradition and later editing, and it is difficult to separate the words of the founder from the later layers of the community.
- Scholars debate the meaning of anatta: whether it is an ontological denial of the self or a warning against attachment to it, and Steven Collins in *Selfless Persons* (1982) offered a detailed reading of this.
- Some hold that presenting Buddhism as a purely rational philosophy is a modern simplification that ignores the cosmic and ritual side.
- The image of the Buddha varies among the Pali and Sanskrit sources.
