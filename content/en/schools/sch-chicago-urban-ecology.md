# First Chicago School / Urban Ecology

The current founded by Robert Ezra Park and Ernest Burgess in the Department of Sociology at the University of Chicago, often described as the first sociology department in the United States (founded 1892), which applied concepts from biological ecology to the study of the modern city.

## The Central Idea

Park and Burgess borrowed from plant ecology concepts such as "competition," "invasion," and "succession" to explain how population groups and economic functions are distributed geographically within the large industrial city. In 1925 Burgess presented the "concentric zone model" to describe the growth of Chicago from the central business district to the deteriorating zone of transition, then the workers' neighborhoods, then the affluent suburbs. The researchers adopted a field method that was innovative at the time: combining social statistics with direct ethnographic observation and in-depth interviews.

## Historical Context: Chicago as a Living Laboratory

This school arose in a unique historical setting: between 1890 and 1930 Chicago was one of the fastest-growing cities in the world, its population swelling from about one million to more than three million over four decades through waves of internal migration (especially Black people coming from the American South in the "Great Migration") and heavy immigration from Eastern and Southern Europe. This immense and rapid transformation gave the scholars of the neighboring university a "living social laboratory" for studying processes of urbanization, integration, and deviance in real time. Robert Park, the former journalist who had attended Georg Simmel's lectures in Berlin and took his doctorate in Germany, exploited it to establish, together with Burgess, a systematic field research program of wide scope.

## Method and Pioneering Studies

Alongside the theoretical model of concentric zones, the school produced a series of influential detailed field studies by the students of Park and Burgess: Nels Anderson studied "the hobo" (The Hobo, 1923); Louis Wirth studied the Jewish ghetto in Chicago (The Ghetto, 1928); Clifford Shaw studied juvenile delinquency geographically through "rate maps" that linked crime to the decaying "transition" zones rather than to ethnic characteristics of the inhabitants themselves; and Harvey Zorbaugh studied the area of "the Gold Coast and the slum" (The Gold Coast and the Slum, 1929), showing the sharp class contrast within a narrow geographic distance. All these studies adopted a field methodology that combined precise statistical maps, direct ethnographic observation, in-depth interviews, and the analysis of individual "life history," in what later became known as the "Chicago method."

## Impact and Critique

This research program produced dozens of pioneering field studies on deviance, migration, urban crime, and slums, and established a methodological research tradition (participant observation, life history) that continued to guide American urban sociology throughout the twentieth century; symbolic interactionism, associated with Herbert Blumer and others trained at Chicago, grew out of the same milieu. However, in the 1970s Manuel Castells leveled a sharp Marxist critique at this legacy, holding that the borrowing of biological "ecological" concepts (competition, invasion, succession) from biology conceals the actual economic and political structures (real estate investment decisions, policies of residential discrimination, real estate capital) behind a false "naturalistic" veil that portrays the distribution of poverty and wealth in the city as the product of inevitable environmental laws rather than of human and power-based decisions that can be changed.

## Key Works and Representatives

Alongside Park and Burgess, Louis Wirth is considered one of the foremost figures of the second generation of this school, offering in his famous article "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938) a deeper theorization of the effect of urban size, density, and population heterogeneity on social relations, moving from purely spatial ecological description to a psychosocial analysis of the experience of urban alienation. Likewise, Frederic Thrasher, with his study *The Gang* (1927), contributed to applying the school's method to the phenomenon of youth gangs in "deteriorating" areas, while the study of St. Clair Drake and Horace Cayton, *Black Metropolis* (1945), represented a later extension that applied the same framework to the experience of Black Americans on Chicago's South Side.

## Concluding Note

The legacy of this school remains twofold: on the one hand, a pioneering field research methodology that redefined how to study the city empirically; on the other, a relatively simple ecological theoretical model that was soon surpassed by later generations of urban sociologists with frameworks more attentive to power and the political economy of the city.

## Additional Note

It is worth noting that the sociology department at the University of Chicago was for decades a leading center for doctoral training in the field in America, and that the *American Journal of Sociology*, founded by Albion Small in 1895, remained the main venue in which most of the school's early field studies were published.
