# Neoplatonism

The last great current of ancient Greek philosophy, founded by **Plotinus** (204/5-270 CE) in Rome and systematized by later thinkers up to **Proclus** (412-485 CE) in Athens. Its adherents regarded themselves simply as Platonists; they read Plato, with Aristotelian, Pythagorean, and Stoic elements, as a unified system in which everything derives from a single transcendent first principle. It strongly influenced Christian, Islamic, and Jewish thought, and Renaissance Platonism.

## Context

In the third century CE philosophy in the Greek world was dominated by Middle Platonism, Stoicism, and Peripatetic and skeptical currents. Plotinus studied for about eleven years in Alexandria under Ammonius Saccas, then came to Rome around 244 and taught there until his death in 270. He understood philosophy as a way of life aimed at the soul's return to its source, and he drew on Plato, Aristotle, and the Stoics to formulate it.

## Phases

- **Plotinus (c. 244-270 CE)**: in Rome. His treatises, written c. 253-270, were edited by his student Porphyry into six groups of nine, the *Enneads* (54 treatises).
- **Porphyry (c. 234-c. 305) and Iamblichus (c. 245-c. 325)**: Porphyry systematized Plotinus and wrote on logic and against Christianity; Iamblichus, working in Syria, introduced a stronger emphasis on ritual practice (theurgy) and a more elaborate hierarchy of divine beings.
- **Athens (c. 400-529)**: Plutarch of Athens, Syrianus, Proclus, Marinus, Isidore, and Damascius developed a systematic metaphysics and a vast commentary tradition on Plato.
- **Alexandria (5th-6th centuries)**: Hierocles, Hermias, Ammonius son of Hermias, Olympiodorus, and the Christian John Philoponus taught and commented on Plato and Aristotle.

## Key Figures

- **Plotinus** (204/5-270 CE): founder of Neoplatonism.
- **Porphyry** (c. 234-c. 305 CE): author of the *Isagoge* (an introduction to Aristotle's *Categories*), the *Life of Plotinus*, and *Against the Christians*, now surviving only in fragments.
- **Iamblichus** (c. 245-c. 325 CE): author of *On the Mysteries* (attributed to him in the later tradition) and a major reorganizer of Platonic curricula and metaphysics, who gave theurgy an important place.
- **Plutarch of Athens** (d. c. 432 CE) and **Syrianus** (d. c. 437 CE): heads of the Athenian school; Syrianus was Proclus's teacher.
- **Proclus** (412-485 CE): head of the Academy at Athens and the greatest systematizer of Neoplatonism, author of the *Elements of Theology*, the *Platonic Theology*, and commentaries on the *Timaeus*, *Republic*, *Parmenides*, and other dialogues.
- **Marinus of Neapolis**: Proclus's successor, who wrote the *Life of Proclus*.
- **Damascius** (c. 462-after 538 CE): last head of the Athenian school, author of *Problems and Solutions concerning First Principles*.

## Central Concepts

- **The One (to hen)**: the first principle, beyond being and thought, and the source of everything.
- **Emanation (proodos)**: from the One proceeds Intellect (nous), from Intellect Soul (psyche), and from Soul the physical world. Emanation is not a temporal act, and the One is not diminished by it.
- **Intellect (nous)**: the realm of the Forms and of divine thinking.
- **Soul (psyche)**: a mediator between Intellect and the body; it includes the World Soul and individual souls.
- **Matter (hyle)**: the lowest level of reality, identified by Plotinus with privation and the source of evil, though evil is not a positive principle.
- **Return (epistrophe)**: the soul ascends back toward the One through virtue, contemplation, and in Plotinus's case a union (henosis) that transcends thought; for Iamblichus and Proclus, theurgic ritual also plays an essential role.
- **The three hypostases**: the One, Intellect, and Soul, in Plotinus.
- **Theurgy (theourgia)**: ritual practice aimed at uniting the soul with the divine, emphasized by Iamblichus and his successors, not by Plotinus.

## Commentary and Metaphysics in Late Antiquity

The Athenian school produced a large body of commentary on Plato and Aristotle. Proclus's commentaries (on the *Timaeus*, *Republic*, *Parmenides*, and others) and his systematic works are the main sources for late Platonism. Damascius's *First Principles* is one of the last original metaphysical works of the Platonic school and pushes the doctrine of the ineffable principle to its limit.

## Relation to Christianity

Neoplatonism and Christianity emerged in the same period, and their relation is complex and debated.
- **Third century**: Eusebius reports that Origen of Alexandria studied under Ammonius Saccas, the teacher of Plotinus (some scholars distinguish a Neoplatonist Origen from the Christian one).
- **Fourth century**: the Cappadocian Fathers and Augustine used Platonic concepts; Augustine says in the *Confessions* (book 7) that he read "books of the Platonists," which scholars identify as writings of Plotinus and Porphyry in Latin translation.
- **Porphyry's *Against the Christians***: a critique of Christianity in fifteen books, preserved only in fragments, quoted or answered by Eusebius, Jerome, Augustine, and others.
- **529 CE**: a decree of Justinian is traditionally linked to the closing of the Platonic school at Athens; historians debate how abruptly the teaching ended.

## Influence

- **Christian theology**: Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, and Boethius transmitted Neoplatonic ideas to the Latin West; John Scotus Eriugena (9th century) translated Pseudo-Dionysius.
- **Islamic philosophy**: Neoplatonic texts circulated in Arabic, notably the *Theology of Aristotle* (a paraphrase of parts of Plotinus's *Enneads*) and the *Liber de causis* (drawn from Proclus's *Elements of Theology*). Al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Avicenna, and the Brethren of Purity drew on them.
- **Avicenna** (980-1037) developed an emanationist metaphysics in the *Metaphysics* of *al-Shifa'* (*al-Ilahiyyat*).
- **Averroes** (1126-1198) criticized the emanationist elements in favour of a purer Aristotelianism.
- **Renaissance**: Marsilio Ficino (1433-1499) translated Plato (1484) and Plotinus (1492) into Latin and led the Platonic circle in Florence.
- **Modern philosophy**: Hegel and Schelling engaged with Plotinus and Proclus; Cambridge Platonists in the seventeenth century drew on Neoplatonic sources.

## The End

- **529 CE**: Justinian issued measures against pagan teaching, traditionally associated with the end of the Platonic school at Athens.
- **After 529**: Damascius, Simplicius, Priscian, and others are reported to have gone to the court of the Persian king Khosrow I, and to have returned after the peace of 532.
- **Later**: Neoplatonic commentary continued in Alexandria (Ammonius, Olympiodorus, Philoponus) and, through Simplicius, elsewhere; Neoplatonic ideas continued in Christian and Islamic thought.

## Limits

- "Neoplatonism" is a modern term; ancient philosophers called themselves Platonists.
- The boundary between Middle Platonism and Neoplatonism is a matter of scholarly convention.
- Neoplatonists were not a unified school: Plotinus, Iamblichus, and Proclus differ, for example, on the descent of the soul and the role of ritual.
- Plotinus himself did not stress theurgy and criticized Gnostic views of the cosmos in *Enneads* II.9.
