# Keynesian Economics

**Keynesian economics** is a school of political economy founded on the ideas of the British economist **John Maynard Keynes**, above all in his pivotal book *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936). It rests on a premise that broke with the teaching then dominant in Britain: the economy can settle at an equilibrium marked by long-lasting **involuntary unemployment**, without market forces automatically pushing it toward full employment. State intervention through fiscal and monetary policy is therefore not a departure from efficiency but, at times, a necessary condition for achieving it.

## The Context of Birth: The Great Depression and the Failure of Classical Theory

The school arose as a direct response to the **Great Depression** of the 1930s, when the classical expectation that wages and interest rates would fall automatically until the economy returned to full employment failed to materialise. Keynes observed that massive unemployment persisted for many years without correcting itself. He concluded that the classical assumption formulated by Jean-Baptiste Say, namely that supply creates its own demand so that a general and persistent overproduction cannot occur, was structurally wrong, not merely a temporary imbalance.

## Effective Demand Instead of Automatic Equilibrium

Keynes replaced the classical logic of supply with the concept of **effective demand**: the actual level of total spending in the economy (consumption, investment and government expenditure) determines the level of output and employment, and not the reverse. When this aggregate demand falls, as it did during the Depression, no automatic mechanism guarantees its return to a level of full employment; the economy may remain at an equilibrium short of full employment for a long period.

## Fiscal Policy as a Tool for Recovery

The direct political conclusion of this analysis is that the government can, and sometimes should, intervene through public spending or tax cuts to raise aggregate demand when the private sector fails to do so, even if this produces a budget deficit in the short run. Although the American "New Deal" of the 1930s preceded the *General Theory* and was not derived from it, the idea provided a theoretical justification for deficit-financed stabilisation, and it shaped postwar full-employment commitments such as the British 1944 White Paper on Employment Policy. It became a standard reference for economic policymakers in the Western world for decades.

## Liquidity Preference and the Interest Rate

Keynes also developed an alternative theory of how the interest rate is determined, based on **liquidity preference**. In this view the interest rate is not the price of waiting (as the classical economists held) but the price of parting with liquid cash. It is determined by the money supply relative to individuals' desire to hold money in liquid form. This allows the explanation of situations in which lowering the interest rate fails to stimulate sufficient investment, which his followers later called the "liquidity trap".

## Academic Reception and Global Spread

Keynesian ideas spread rapidly through American and British universities after the war and became the dominant framework for teaching macroeconomics until the mid-1970s. The main teaching device was already in place before the war: in 1937 John Hicks set out the IS-LM diagram as a simplified reading of Keynes's argument. Paul Samuelson then built that reading into the "neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis" that came to dominate the textbooks. The synthesis is treated in its own entry.

## The Crisis and the Challenge: Stagflation in the 1970s

The traditional Keynesian school suffered a severe credibility crisis with the oil price shock of 1973 and the "stagflation" that followed, namely the simultaneous rise of inflation and unemployment. The standard Keynesian analytical tools, in particular the simple Phillips curve, were not readily equipped to explain this phenomenon. This opened the way for the rise of monetarism and the New Classical school as competing alternatives.

## The Divided Heritage: A Reconciling Synthesis and a Radical Post-Keynesian Path

After Keynes's death in 1946, his legacy split into two distinct paths. One was a reconciling path that merged his partial ideas into the general neoclassical framework (the neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis). The other held that this synthesis had stripped Keynes's original ideas of their radicalism, and it called for recovering a reading more faithful to his text (the post-Keynesian school). This reflects a genuine interpretive dispute over what Keynes actually meant, a question that remains a continuing academic debate.

## Applications in the Arab World

Some economic planning programmes in Egypt during the era of President Gamal Abdel Nasser, particularly the Five-Year Plans that began in 1960, drew on a general principle close to the Keynesian spirit in adopting an active, directive role for the state in investment and the allocation of resources. However, these programmes were closer to a model of comprehensive central planning than to the Western Keynesian recipe, which rests on demand management in an essentially free market economy.

## Variation within the Keynesian Recipe by Country

Applications of the Keynesian recipe varied in practice according to the institutional context of each country. The United States focused on tax cuts and temporary spending programmes to counter economic cycles, while European countries (Britain and the Scandinavian countries) moved toward building more durable welfare states that combined demand management with permanent social and health insurance programmes. This variation shows that "Keynesianism" was not a single, uniform political prescription so much as a general analytical framework that was deployed in different ways according to each country's political priorities.
