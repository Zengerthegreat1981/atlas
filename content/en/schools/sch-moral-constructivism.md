# Moral Constructivism

## Meaning and Definition

Moral constructivism (Moral Constructivism) is a metaethical position holding that moral truths are not objective, independent facts that are "discovered," as moral realism claims. Rather, they are "constructed" (Constructed) through a rational procedure agreed upon by rational agents under specified fair conditions. A moral proposition is true because it results from this correct procedure, not because it corresponds to a moral reality that exists prior to the procedure itself.

## Historical Context

The roots of this trend go back to Kantian philosophy, which held that the moral law is legislated by practical reason for itself (rational autonomy) and is not discovered outside of it. Its mature contemporary form, however, emerged with John Rawls in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), who derived principles of justice through the procedure of the "original position" behind a "veil of ignorance." Rawls labelled his approach "Kantian constructivism" in his 1980 Dewey Lectures ("Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory") and later recast it as "political constructivism" in *Political Liberalism* (1993). Christine Korsgaard developed the trend philosophically in *The Sources of Normativity* (1996), linking constructivism to the concept of the agent's practical identity.

## A Comparison with Islamic Legal Theory (Usul al-Fiqh)

This trend differs fundamentally from the principles of Islamic jurisprudence (*usul al-fiqh*), which, on the dominant view, do not treat consensus (*ijma'*) or rational reasoning (*ijtihad*) as "creating" the legal ruling, but as "discovering" it from a prior source, namely the divine revelation. Even analogical reasoning (*qiyas*), though it relies on reason to extract the effective cause ('illa), remains a tool for discovering an objective and fixed ruling that exists in the knowledge of God, not a construction of it. Constructivism, by contrast, makes the rational procedure itself the source of truth rather than a means of uncovering a truth that already exists. Nevertheless, there is a partial convergence in the idea of consensus, which gives epistemic legitimacy to the agreement of scholars, although the difference in usul remains: for jurists, consensus is evidence of a text or proof that has not reached us, not an independent source of legislation.

## Central Ideas and Thinkers

Constructivism is distinguished from outright non-realism (such as emotivism) by its insistence that there is a genuine standard of correctness for moral judgments, but this standard is procedural (the soundness of practical reasoning) rather than a correspondence to an independent metaphysical reality. Contemporary constructivists divide into a "Kantian constructivism" (Rawls, Korsgaard, O'Neill), which ties the procedure to the conditions of practical reason as such, and a "Humean constructivism" (defended, for example, by Sharon Street in "Constructivism about Reasons," 2008), which takes the agent's own evaluative attitudes as the starting point. The trend has drawn objections from realists such as Derek Parfit in *On What Matters* (2011), who argued that Kantian constructivism either collapses into a form of realism or fails to deliver substantive moral conclusions, since the procedure presupposes standards of correct reasoning that need a justification not itself produced by the procedure.

## Application and Impact

The constructivist method is used in practice to formulate principles of political justice and human rights through deliberative committees that simulate the requirement of "impartiality behind a veil of ignorance." It is also used to justify modern democratic constitutions as the product of a fair rational agreement among free and equal citizens, rather than as a reflection of a prior moral order imposed upon them.

## Constructivism in Habermas's Discourse Ethics

Jürgen Habermas developed a procedural theory known as "Discourse Ethics" (*Moral Consciousness and Communicative Action*, 1983/1990), sometimes classified alongside constructivism, which ties the validity of a moral norm to its capacity to be agreed upon by all those affected by it in a free, rational dialogue free from coercion. For Habermas, the criterion of validity is procedural and communicative (the test is actual or idealized public dialogue), rather than the solitary reflection of the individual rational agent in Kant. This proposal reflects the spirit of the contemporary democratic age, which ties the legitimacy of decisions to the process of public deliberation rather than to their predetermined content.

## The Difference between Constructivism and Ethical Relativism

Many people mistakenly confuse constructivism with explicit ethical relativism, and the difference is fundamental. Relativism makes the truth of a moral judgment depend on what each community actually believes (a social description). Constructivism, by contrast, requires a specific normative rational procedure (such as Rawls's veil of ignorance or Habermas's conditions of discourse) by which the actual belief of any community can be judged and shown to be mistaken. Not every socially agreed outcome is therefore necessarily a correctly constructed one, since actual agreement may fail to satisfy the conditions of procedural justice.

## Constructivism in International Relations

A broadly similar logic of procedural legitimacy is sometimes invoked to justify international law and multilateral treaties (compare Rawls's *The Law of Peoples*, 1999); this is distinct from "constructivism" in international-relations theory, associated with Alexander Wendt, which is a sociological claim about how norms and identities are socially made. The legitimacy of an international rule (such as the prohibition on the use of chemical weapons) is regarded as arising from the agreement of states to it through a relatively fair negotiating procedure, not from its being discovered in the "nature of things." This explains why some international rules are regarded as capable of amendment over time whenever the conditions of global agreement change, in contrast to a moral-realist view on which such standards hold independently of any agreement.

The amendability of international rules is at once an advantage and a drawback. It allows adaptation to changing circumstances, but it sometimes weakens the stability needed to protect the victims of violations from the later withdrawal of political will by powerful states.
