# Lockeanism (Empirical Lockeanism)

A philosophical tradition deriving from **John Locke** (1632-1704), English philosopher and political theorist, often called the "father of liberalism" and a founder of modern empiricism. Locke's *Essay concerning Human Understanding* (1689) explains knowledge by the "ideas" the mind receives from sensation and reflection, rejecting innate ideas, and his *Two Treatises of Government* grounds legitimate political authority in consent, natural rights, and the right to resist tyranny.

## Founding Context

Locke was born in Wrington, Somerset, into a Puritan-leaning family of modest gentry; his father fought on the Parliamentary side in the Civil War. He was educated at Westminster School and Christ Church, Oxford, where he read Descartes and was influenced by the experimental natural philosophy of Robert Boyle and by his medical work with Thomas Sydenham. He met Anthony Ashley Cooper, later the first Earl of Shaftesbury (1621-1683), in 1666 and became his physician and secretary, entering politics. Suspected of involvement in the opposition to the crown, he fled to the Netherlands (1683-1689) and returned to England after the Glorious Revolution of 1688, publishing his major works in 1689-1690.

## Prominent Figures

- **John Locke** (1632-1704): the central figure.
- **Isaac Newton** (1642-1727): his friend and correspondent.
- **Anthony Ashley Cooper, first Earl of Shaftesbury** (1621-1683): his patron.
- **Damaris Cudworth Masham** (1659-1708): philosopher, daughter of Ralph Cudworth, and his close friend, in whose household he spent his last years.
- **William Molyneux** (1656-1698): correspondent, who posed the "Molyneux problem" about a man born blind who gains sight.
- **Étienne Bonnot de Condillac** (1714-1780): *Essai sur l'origine des connaissances humaines* (1746), carrying Lockean empiricism into France.
- **Voltaire** (1694-1778): helped popularize Locke and Newton in France in his *Letters concerning the English Nation* (1733).
- **George Berkeley** (1685-1753) and **David Hume** (1711-1776): developed empiricism critically from Locke's starting point.
- **Thomas Jefferson** (1743-1826): the Declaration of Independence (1776) draws on the natural-rights tradition to which Locke contributed.

## Central Concepts

### 1. No Innate Ideas

In the *Essay* (1689) Locke rejects innate principles and ideas. The mind at birth is like "white paper" on which experience writes. (The Latin phrase *tabula rasa* is not Locke's own.)

### 2. Sensation and Reflection

Two sources of ideas:
- **Sensation**: ideas derived from external objects through the senses.
- **Reflection**: ideas derived from the mind's observation of its own operations (perceiving, thinking, doubting, believing, reasoning, knowing, willing).

### 3. Simple and Complex Ideas

- **Simple ideas**: the basic, unanalysable ideas received passively, such as whiteness, sweetness, or solidity.
- **Complex ideas**: ideas the mind builds from simple ones by combining, relating, and abstracting, such as modes, relations, and substances.

### 4. Substance

Locke holds that we have no clear idea of substance in general, "something I know not what" that supports qualities, though we know collections of qualities. This became a target of criticism for Berkeley and Hume.

### 5. Primary and Secondary Qualities

- **Primary qualities**: qualities inseparable from bodies in every state, such as solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, and number; our ideas of them resemble the qualities.
- **Secondary qualities**: powers in bodies, grounded in their primary qualities, to produce sensations such as colour, taste, smell, and sound in us; our ideas of them do not resemble anything in the bodies.

### 6. Personal Identity

In the chapter added in the second edition (1694), Locke identifies the person with continuity of consciousness, which extends backward through memory, rather than with identity of soul or body.

### 7. Government

In the *Two Treatises of Government* (published 1689, dated 1690):
- **The state of nature**: a state of freedom and equality governed by natural law, in which people have rights to life, liberty, and property.
- **Consent and the social contract**: legitimate government arises from the consent of the governed to secure those rights.
- **Resistance**: the people may remove a government that violates its trust.

### 8. Religious Toleration

In the *Letter concerning Toleration* (written in Latin as *Epistola de tolerantia*, 1689, with an English translation by William Popple), Locke argues that the civil magistrate's concern is civil interests, not the salvation of souls. His toleration did not extend to atheists or to Catholics owing allegiance to a foreign prince.

### 9. Property

Locke argues in the *Second Treatise* (chapter 5) that each person owns his own person and labour, and acquires property by mixing his labour with common resources, subject to limits such as leaving "enough and as good" for others. Later economists and socialists, including Adam Smith and Karl Marx, discussed labour as a source of value in different ways.

## Reception and Developments

- **French reception**: Condillac, Voltaire, and the Encyclopedists took up Locke's empiricism, with Condillac carrying it further toward a "sensationalist" account in which all mental life derives from sensation.
- **Scottish response**: Thomas Reid and the common-sense school rejected the "way of ideas" shared by Locke and Hume.
- **Political reception**: Locke's political thought influenced Whig and later American revolutionary rhetoric, though the extent of his direct influence on the American founders is debated.
- **Associationist psychology**: David Hartley, James Mill, John Stuart Mill, and Alexander Bain built psychological theories on empiricist foundations.
- **Contemporary uses**: debates about personal identity (psychological continuity theories), and Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), which develops a Lockean theory of rights and property.

## Relation to Other Schools

- **Continental rationalism**: opposed on innate ideas; Leibniz replied to Locke in the *New Essays* (written c. 1704, published 1765).
- **British empiricism**: Locke is among its principal systematic figures.
- **Berkeleianism**: Berkeley criticized Locke's abstract ideas and the primary/secondary quality distinction.
- **Humeanism**: Hume took over the way of ideas but rejected Locke's notion of substance.
- **Scottish Common Sense**: a response to the skeptical consequences drawn from the way of ideas.
- **Classical liberalism**: Locke's political writings are a major source.

## The Philosophical Predicament

- **Qualities and substance**: if all we know are ideas and qualities, what is the unknown substance that supports them? Berkeley and Hume pressed this.
- **Resemblance**: how can ideas of primary qualities resemble bodies if we never compare ideas with things outside the mind?
- **Personal identity**: critics such as Joseph Butler and Thomas Reid argued that Locke's memory-based account is circular or generates paradoxes.
- **Religion**: Locke's theological writings, including *The Reasonableness of Christianity*, were attacked by critics such as Bishop Stillingfleet as leaning toward Socinianism, while Locke himself remained a Christian.
- **Political consent**: critics questioned how real consent could be shown by those born into an existing society, which Locke addresses through the idea of tacit consent.
