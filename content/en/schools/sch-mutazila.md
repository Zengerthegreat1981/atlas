# The Mu'tazila

The Mu'tazila were a rationalist Islamic school of kalam that arose in Basra in the Umayyad period and then flourished in Baghdad and elsewhere in the Abbasid period. They called themselves "the people of justice and unity" (*ahl al-adl wa-l-tawhid*). They rest on giving priority to reason in establishing knowledge of God and the judgments of divine justice, and on declaring God to be beyond any resemblance to created things. In the second and third centuries AH they were the most prominent current in kalam; they then weakened before the Ash'ari school and the people of hadith, but their influence remained in jurisprudence and its principles, Qur'anic exegesis and rhetoric.

## Origins

The sources report that Wasil ibn Ata (80–131 AH) was in the circle of al-Hasan al-Basri when he was asked about the perpetrator of a grave sin. He said that he is in "a position between the two positions", neither a believer nor an unbeliever, and withdrew from the circle, whereupon al-Hasan said: "Wasil has withdrawn from us" (*i'tazalana*). There are other accounts of the matter that link the name to abstention and political withdrawal, and some contemporary scholars doubt the account. It appears that the roots of the ideas go back to the Qadariyya, who affirmed human freedom, and to the Jahmiyya in the denial of attributes. Amr ibn Ubayd (d. 144 AH) joined Wasil, and the school then became independent in the Abbasid period and two schools formed: Basra and Baghdad.

## The five principles

As formulated by Judge Abd al-Jabbar in *Sharh al-Usul al-Khamsa*:

1. **Unity (tawhid)**: God is one and does not resemble His creation; body, direction and vision with the eye are denied of Him. They said that He knows by His essence and not through a knowledge additional to the essence, in order to avoid a multiplicity of eternal things, and their opponents called them "*mu'attila*" (those who divest God of attributes), which is a term of reproach.
2. **Justice (adl)**: God does not do what is ugly and does not create the unjust acts of human beings; the human being is the real agent of his acts and responsible for them. In some accounts they hold that God is obliged to provide "grace" (*lutf*) and what is best (*al-aslah*).
3. **The promise and the threat**: God carries out His promise of reward to the obedient and His threat of punishment to the disobedient, and so does not pardon the perpetrator of a grave sin who died without repentance.
4. **The position between the two positions**: the perpetrator of a grave sin is a transgressor (*fasiq*), neither a believer nor an unbeliever.
5. **Commanding the right and forbidding the wrong**: obligatory on everyone; they disagreed on whether fighting is permitted in carrying it out.

## Method and ideas

They gave priority to reason in primary knowledge, holding that rational inquiry makes knowledge of God obligatory before the arrival of revelation, and that good and bad are rationally known; this was studied by George Hourani in "Islamic Rationalism: The Ethics of Abd al-Jabbar" (1971). They interpreted verses that suggest anthropomorphism figuratively. They held that the Qur'an is created, because speech is an attribute of action. In natural philosophy some of them, such as Abu al-Hudhayl al-Allaf (d. about 235 AH), held to the indivisible part (the atom) and to the finiteness of the world, while Ibrahim al-Nazzam (d. about 231 AH) held to infinite divisibility and the leap (*tafra*), and to the Qur'an's inimitability lying in "*sarfa*", that is, that God turned the Arabs away from contesting it. Al-Jahiz (d. 255 AH) wrote on animals, on eloquence, and in defense of the Mu'tazila. On the imamate they held to selection, and some of them preferred Ali, without being Shi'a.

## Leading figures of the two schools

- **Basra**: Abu Ali al-Jubba'i (d. 303 AH) and his son Abu Hashim (d. 321 AH), then Judge Abd al-Jabbar al-Hamadhani (d. 415 AH), author of *al-Mughni fi Abwab al-Tawhid wa-l-Adl*, and Abu al-Husayn al-Basri (d. 436 AH).
- **Baghdad**: Bishr ibn al-Mu'tamir (d. 210 AH), Abu Musa al-Mirdar, Abu al-Husayn al-Khayyat, and Abu al-Qasim al-Balkhi al-Ka'bi.
- **Exegesis**: al-Zamakhshari (d. 538 AH) in *al-Kashshaf*, which is regarded as the best-known Mu'tazili exegesis still in circulation.

## The inquisition and the decline of power

The caliph al-Ma'mun adopted in 212 AH the doctrine of the createdness of the Qur'an, and the "inquisition" (*mihna*) began in 218 AH; judges and jurists were interrogated, and Ahmad ibn Hanbal endured it patiently and was imprisoned. It continued into the time of al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq, then al-Mutawakkil halted it in 234 AH and in 237 AH sided with the people of hadith. Thus the Mu'tazila had official power only for a limited number of decades, and the state did not at that time adopt Ash'arism: Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 324 AH) was a Mu'tazili and then left them about 300 AH, and Ash'arism did not spread officially until later, under the Seljuks.

## Influence and continuity

The Zaydis in Yemen preserved the Mu'tazila's books, which is why most volumes of *al-Mughni* were found in their libraries in the twentieth century, and researchers benefited from it. Mu'tazili thought remained present in Hanafi jurisprudence, among the Imami Shi'a and among the Ibadis. Modern reformers such as Muhammad Abduh praised it, Ahmad Amin treated it in *Duha al-Islam* (volume three), and Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd in *al-Ittijah al-Aqli fi al-Tafsir* (1982). In the West it was studied by van Ess in *Theologie und Gesellschaft im 2. und 3. Jahrhundert Hidschra*.

## Limits and criticism

Its opponents among the people of hadith criticized its interpretation of texts and its placing of reason ahead of transmitted tradition, and the Ash'aris criticized it on the question of obliging God to do what is best and on absolutely free action. Many early Mu'tazili works are lost; apart from the surviving volumes of Abd al-Jabbar and al-Zamakhshari's commentary, our knowledge comes largely from the bibliographical *Fihrist* of Ibn al-Nadim and from the reports of opponents. Researchers note that what we know about it often comes from the books of its opponents such as al-Shahrastani, al-Ash'ari and al-Baghdadi, and that one individual's view should not be generalized to all, since its leading figures differed on many questions. The school has also been accused of authoritarianism in the inquisition, so that repression became part of its memory.
