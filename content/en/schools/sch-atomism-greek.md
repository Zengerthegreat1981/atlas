# Greek Atomism

A philosophical movement founded by Leucippus (mid-5th century BCE) and developed by Democritus (c. 460–370 BCE) in the city of Abdera on the coast of Thrace. It offered an early and comprehensive mechanical-materialist account of the world: being consists of tiny indivisible particles (atoms) moving in the void, and all the diversity we see arises from the shapes, arrangement and position of these atoms. Epicurus (341–270 BCE) then revived it within an ethical school, Lucretius carried it into Latin, and it returned to the foreground of natural philosophy in the seventeenth century.

## Context

Atomism arose as an answer to the impasse produced by Parmenides: if being is one and unchanging and non-being does not exist, then there is neither motion nor plurality. Before Democritus, Empedocles had recourse to four roots that mix and separate through the two forces of Love and Strife, and Anaxagoras to infinite seeds set in motion by Mind (nous). The atomists answered that non-being, that is, the void, "exists no less than what exists": the void has an existence that makes motion and plurality possible, and indivisible bodies have an existence that makes permanence possible. Abdera was an open commercial city, and the sophist Protagoras also came from it.

## The Two Founders

- **Leucippus**: Aristotle and Theophrastus mention him as the founder of atomism, and he is credited with the book "The Great World-Order" (Megas Diakosmos), which Theophrastus attributed to him although it was also listed among the works of Democritus, and the book "On Mind." According to Diogenes Laertius, Epicurus denied that Leucippus had existed; since Aristotle and Theophrastus name him, most contemporary scholars accept that he did.
- **Democritus**: tradition attributes to him dozens of works on physics, ethics, mathematics, music and literary criticism, of which only fragments survive, transmitted by Aristotle, Sextus Empiricus, Stobaeus and others. He was called "the Laughing Philosopher" in tradition for his calm view of human folly.

## Central Concepts

- **The atom (atomos)**: "that which cannot be divided," a hard, solid, eternal, unchanging particle, infinite in number, differing in shape, arrangement and position; Aristotle illustrated this with the letters of the alphabet, which differ in shape (A and N), arrangement (AN and NA) and position (Z and N).
- **The void (kenon)**: the empty space necessary for the motion of atoms; without it there is neither differentiation nor motion.
- **Necessity**: the world forms through the rotation of a vortex (dine) that sorts like atoms together, and everything that happens occurs by mechanical necessity, not by purpose or a guiding intellect; hence the attack of Aristotle and Plato on it for lacking final causation. "Chance" for Democritus is not a cause but ignorance of causes.
- **Knowledge**: Democritus distinguished between "legitimate" knowledge (gnesie), which is the intellect's knowledge of atoms and void, and "obscure" knowledge (skotie), which is the knowledge of the senses. In his famous fragment he said that sweet, bitter, hot, cold and colour exist by convention (nomos), while in truth there are atoms and void. He then brought out the tension that the senses themselves are the source of rational knowledge, reporting the senses as saying to the intellect: "Wretched mind, from us you took your proofs, and do you wish to overthrow us?"
- **Ethics (euthymia)**: the goal is tranquillity, serenity of soul and moderation of pleasure; this is the root of Epicurus's later ethics, although they differed in many details.

## Influence

- **Epicurus and Lucretius**: Epicurus modified atomism by introducing the "swerve" to explain freedom, and Lucretius wrote his poem "On the Nature of Things" (De rerum natura, c. 55 BCE), which was the bridge to the Renaissance.
- **Arab-Islamic thought**: the Arabs did not receive Democritus's books in complete translation, but atomism entered through Islamic kalam: most of the Mu'tazila and the Ash'aris held to the "indivisible part" (al-jawhar al-fard), and al-Nazzam opposed it by affirming the "leap" (tafra) and the infinite divisibility of bodies; Moses Maimonides summarized the premises of the mutakallimun in the "Guide of the Perplexed" (1/73); Ibn Sina rejected atomism in the physics of the "Shifa'," and Ibn Rushd rejected it. Shlomo Pines argued in "Beiträge zur islamischen Atomenlehre" (1936; English translation *Studies in Islamic Atomism*, 1997) that the kalam theory was not transmitted directly from the Greeks and that it was influenced by Indian conceptions. The physician al-Razi (d. 925) held a materialist corpuscular theory related to it.
- **Modern physics**: Pierre Gassendi revived atomism in "Animadversiones in decimum librum Diogenis Laertii" (1649) and "Syntagma philosophicum" (1658), and Boyle and Newton were influenced by it in their corpuscular conceptions, down to Dalton's atomic theory in 1808.

## Limits and Criticism

- Ancient atomism is a natural metaphysics that was not built on experiment or calculation; it shares the name, but not the method, with scientific atomism.
- Democritus's books have not survived, so most of what we know comes from his opponents and commentators, foremost among them Aristotle, making it impossible to be certain of many details.
- The tension between strict determinism and an ethics of responsibility remained unresolved.
- Plato and Aristotle criticized it for neglecting final causation and for being unable to explain life and mind through mechanical motion.
- The Muslim philosophers of the Peripatetic orientation faulted it for denying the substantial conception and forms, and for making natural continuity impossible in geometry.
