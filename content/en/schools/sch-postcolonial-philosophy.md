# Postcolonial Philosophy

Postcolonial philosophy is a critical current that arose at the intersection of continental philosophy, especially poststructuralism, with the critique of colonialism. It is concerned with analyzing the continuing discursive, epistemic and psychological effects of European colonialism after the end of its direct political control, and asks how colonialism produced the image of the colonized and knowledge about them. Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) is regarded as the founding work of the field, and Kamal Abu Deeb translated it into Arabic (1981).

## Arab and Maghrebi Roots

Arab and Maghrebi thinkers anticipated or contributed to many of this current's concerns. Malek Bennabi wrote *Conditions of Renaissance* (1948), which contains the idea of "colonizability" (al-qabiliyya lil-isti'mar): that colonialism has internal conditions within society. In 1963 Anouar Abdel-Malek published his essay "Orientalism in Crisis," an early and influential critique of Orientalism from within the social sciences. Abdallah Laroui published *L'idéologie arabe contemporaine* (1967), a critique of Arab ideological discourse. Hassan Hanafi, in *An Introduction to the Science of Occidentalism* (1991), called for studying the West as an object rather than as a studying subject. Sadiq Jalal al-Azm, in "Orientalism and Orientalism in Reverse" (1981), criticized Said's book, holding that Said falls into an essentialism that treats "the West" as a single bloc, and that some Arab writers practise a reverse Orientalism. Albert Memmi wrote *Portrait du colonisé* (1957), an analysis of the psychology of the colonized.

## Fanonian Roots

The current rests on Frantz Fanon (1925-1961), especially *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) and *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), which analyzed the psychological alienation and existential violence resulting from colonialism, and regarded the "Other" as a psychological and social construction. Fanon worked as a psychiatrist in Algeria (at Blida-Joinville) and joined the National Liberation Front.

## The Three Pillars

Alongside Said, Gayatri Spivak and Homi Bhabha are regarded as the other two pillars of what Robert Young called the "holy trinity" of postcolonial theory.

- **Said**: showed in *Orientalism* that European Orientalism produced "the Orient" as an imagined, static, mysterious object that justifies dominating it, drawing on Foucault's analysis of knowledge and power and on Gramsci's concept of hegemony.
- **Spivak**: borrowed "the subaltern" from Gramsci and from the Indian Subaltern Studies group (Guha, 1982). In "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) she asked whether its voice can be heard outside colonial representation, and about "epistemic violence."
- **Bhabha**: in *The Location of Culture* (1994) he introduced "hybridity," "mimicry" and the "third space": when the colonized adopt the discourse of the colonizer they do not repeat it exactly but produce a version "almost the same, but not quite," which exposes the fragility of colonial discourse.

## Concepts and Tools

The current uses a set of intersecting concepts: "Othering," that is, constructing the other as different and inferior; cultural "hegemony," which leads the colonized to adopt the colonizer's perspective willingly; and "epistemic violence," which erases local knowledges in favor of a knowledge regarded as universal. With these tools novels, travel writings and colonial archives are read, revealing the sexualized and decadent images of the Orient and tracing their echoes in contemporary policies.

## Difference from Latin American Decoloniality

Unlike the Latin American decolonial current (Quijano, Dussel and Mignolo), which roots colonialism in the conquest of the Americas in 1492 and seeks to build an alternative knowledge, postcolonialism often starts from the British and French context in Asia, Africa and the Middle East, and uses the tools of Foucault, Derrida and Lacan to question colonial discourse from within.

## Influence

- It changed literary and cultural studies, and introduced the concepts of Orientalism, representation and hybridity into history, anthropology and international relations.
- It contributed to the revision of curricula and to decentering Europe in history and the philosophy of history.
- In the Arab world it stimulated studies critiquing Orientalism and discussions about the limits of borrowing.

## Limits and Criticisms

- **Aijaz Ahmad**: in *In Theory* (1992) he offered a Marxist critique, holding that the reliance of Said, Bhabha and Spivak on poststructuralism distances them from a material analysis of colonialism as an exploitative economic system, and turns critique into an academic practice detached from liberation struggles.
- **Al-Azm** and others: accused "Orientalism" of essentialism and of ignoring the diversity of the Orientalists.
- **Decoloniality**: held that this current is insufficient for understanding early settler colonialism.
- **Language and position**: critics complain that writing in English in Western universities reproduces the center, and that some concepts derived from the Indian context do not apply easily to other contexts.
