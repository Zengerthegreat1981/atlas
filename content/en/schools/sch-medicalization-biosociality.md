# Sociology of Medicalization & Biosociality: Conrad & Rose

A current that studies how problems formerly handled through moral or social frameworks become "medical conditions" managed through medical diagnosis and treatment, and the effect of this on social identity.

## Central Idea

Peter Conrad, building on earlier critiques such as Irving Zola's (1972), defined "medicalization" as the process by which a human problem, one not previously defined in medical terms (such as hyperactive childhood, alcoholism, childbirth, grief), becomes a "medical condition" that is diagnosed and treated, often under the influence of medical professional interests or a pharmaceutical industry that benefits from expanding the range of treatable "disease." He argued that this shift is not purely scientifically neutral but carries social consequences: it turns the "morally guilty" or "socially deviant" person into a "patient" who deserves treatment rather than blame, which may ease moral stigma but transfers the power to define the problem from society and the family to the medical institution. The concept of "biosociality" was coined by the anthropologist Paul Rabinow (1992) and developed by Nikolas Rose, notably in *The Politics of Life Itself* (2007): with the development of genomic science, entirely new social identities and support groups form around precise biological-genetic classifications (such as carriers of a particular gene for a hereditary disease), producing new forms of collective belonging and a biopolitics based on fine-grained biology itself rather than traditional social identity.

## Historical Context

The field of "sociology of medicalization" arose in the 1970s within a broader critical wave directed at the medical institution, which included Ivan Illich's work on "iatrogenesis" and Michel Foucault's work on "clinical medicine" and "biopolitics." The concept was developed more systematically in the work of Peter Conrad and Joseph Schneider in their joint book *Deviance and Medicalization: From Badness to Sickness* (1980). It traced historically how phenomena such as alcoholism, delinquency and behavioral disorders moved from being "sins" or "moral deviations" handled by the church or the criminal justice system to being "pathological conditions" managed by the medical and pharmaceutical institutions. Nikolas Rose, directly influenced by Foucault, later developed in the 1990s and 2000s a more sophisticated analysis of how human subjectivity is reshaped through modern neuroscience and genomics.

## Key Ideas and Method

Conrad distinguished between different levels of medicalization: the conceptual level (defining a problem in medical terms in public discourse without actual institutional intervention), the institutional level (formal organizations adopting a particular medical classification), and the level of the actual interaction between doctor and patient. He observed how multiple interests converge to drive this process: the pharmaceutical industry, which benefits from broadening the definition of "disease" treatable with drugs; the medical profession, which expands the scope of its authority and social legitimacy; and sometimes the patients themselves, who prefer a "neutral" medical explanation of their suffering to a moral explanation that makes them personally to blame. Nikolas Rose, for his part, took up Rabinow's notion of biosociality in *The Politics of Life Itself* (2007) and, with Carlos Novas, in the idea of "biological citizenship" (2005), a thesis broader than traditional medicalization: with the development of precise genetic testing, individuals are no longer classified only as "patients" but as bearers of potential future biological risks (such as carriers of a particular genetic mutation). Around them form support groups and entirely new social identities based on this precise biological classification ("biological citizenship"), in a profound transformation of the very meaning of identity and social belonging.

## Prominent Works and Figures

Peter Conrad (1945–2020), an American sociologist at Brandeis University, was known for his pioneering study of the medicalization of hyperactivity in children (*Identifying Hyperactive Children*, 1976) and later *The Medicalization of Society* (2007). Nikolas Rose (born 1947), a British sociologist and professor at King's College London, developed a sophisticated Foucauldian analysis of psychology, psychiatry and the genome within his broader project on "Governing the Soul" and contemporary "biopower."

## Impact and Critique

This current provided an influential critical analytic tool for understanding the expansion of medical power in contemporary everyday life, intersecting with Foucault's analysis of biopolitics, and it deeply influenced disability studies, mental-health studies and critical gender studies. It was criticized by physicians and researchers who held that the concept of "medicalization" sometimes exaggerates the portrayal of medical diagnosis as a purely social-control instrument, ignoring the real suffering that effective medical treatment relieves for patients themselves, and that some supposed cases of "medicalization" in fact reflect genuine scientific progress in understanding previously unknown biological causes, not merely an arbitrary social construction. Despite this criticism, the frameworks of medicalization and biosociality remained essential tools in the contemporary sociology of health and illness, regularly used to analyze current controversies such as the diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder, depression and obesity, as conditions in which biological and social dimensions intersect.
