# Buddhist Ethics

## Historical Context

Buddhist ethics arose in northern India with Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha, meaning "the awakened one"), traditionally dated to the sixth century BCE, though many modern scholars place his death in the fifth or early fourth century BCE. According to tradition, he left a life of princely luxury in search of an answer to the problem of human suffering. After a long period of severe asceticism, which he then rejected, Gautama attained "enlightenment" (*bodhi*) under the bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya, and delivered his first sermon at Sarnath, traditionally called the "Setting in Motion of the Wheel of Dhamma," which sets out the Four Noble Truths. Buddhism was originally not a speculative metaphysical system so much as a practical "path" (*marga*) to liberation from the cycle of repeated birth and death (*samsara*). This practical character is what made ethics, rather than theology, the core of the Buddhist teaching. Buddhism spread across Asia in several schools: Theravada (the "way of the elders") in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia; Mahayana (the "great vehicle") in China and Japan; and Vajrayana in Tibet. Each developed its own interpretation of the original ethics while preserving its essence.

## The Four Noble Truths and the Foundations of Ethics

Buddhist ethics rests on a diagnosis of the human condition, formulated in four truths. First, life is suffering (*dukkha*), in the broad sense that includes dissatisfaction and constant change. Second, the cause of suffering is craving and attachment (*tanha*) and ignorance of the true nature of things. Third, suffering can come to an end (*nirvana*) through the extinction of attachment. Fourth, the way to this extinction is the "Noble Eightfold Path." What matters ethically here is that Buddhism does not issue commands from a legislating god. It offers a causal analysis: the ethical act is the one that weakens attachment and ignorance and reduces suffering, both for the agent and for others. The unwholesome act, by contrast, is one that feeds the three roots of evil: greed (*lobha*), hatred (*dosa*), and delusion (*moha*).

## The Noble Eightfold Path and Sila

The Eightfold Path consists of right understanding, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. Traditionally, it is divided into three branches: wisdom (*panna*), ethical conduct (*sila*), and meditative concentration (*samadhi*). *Sila* lies at the heart of everyday Buddhist ethics. Its most prominent expression is the "Five Precepts" that lay followers undertake: abstaining from killing living beings, from stealing, from sexual misconduct, from lying, and from intoxicants. Monks take on additional, stricter rules within the *Vinaya* (the monastic code of conduct). It is worth noting that these precepts are not absolute commands in the Kantian sense. They are voluntary trainings (*sikkhapada*) that the individual adopts out of concern for reducing his or her own suffering and the suffering of others. Scholars interpret Buddhist ethics in different ways: Damien Keown reads it as a form of virtue ethics, while others, such as Charles Goodman, have argued for a consequentialist reading.

## Karma and Moral Intention

Every moral act is linked to the principle of *karma* (action), a law of moral causation that connects actions to results that return to the agent, either in the present life or in later rebirths. Most importantly, the Buddha located the concept of karma in *cetana*, meaning the intention or mental motivation behind an action, rather than in the external act alone. In the *Anguttara Nikaya* (6.63) he is reported to say that it is intention that he calls karma, which gives Buddhist ethics a strong psychological dimension: the moral quality of an act depends heavily on the mental motive from which it arises.

## Key Thinkers and Schools

In the fifth century CE, Buddhaghosa, in his *Visuddhimagga* (The Path of Purification), produced a comprehensive systematic arrangement of *sila*, *samadhi*, and *panna* in the Theravada tradition. In the Mahayana tradition, the eighth-century Indian author Shantideva, in the *Bodhicaryavatara* (A Guide to the Bodhisattva's Way of Life), presented the ideal of the "bodhisattva," who vows to work for the liberation of all sentient beings, often described as postponing final nirvana for their sake. Compassion (*karuna*) and loving-kindness (*metta*) thus become central virtues. In the modern era, the Vietnamese monk Thich Nhat Hanh helped shape "Engaged Buddhism," which applies nonviolence and compassion to questions of conflict, the environment and social justice, and the Dalai Lama has promoted a "secular ethics" grounded in compassion but addressed to people of any belief, notably in *Beyond Religion* (2011).

## Objections and Comparison with Islamic Ethics

Buddhist ethics has been criticized on the grounds that, lacking a divine legislator, it may lack a firm foundation for obligation, and that the criterion of "reducing suffering" may sometimes conflict with other duties, such as justice. A question has also been raised about whether the doctrine of "no-self" (*anatta*) is compatible with the idea of a moral agent who bears responsibility for his or her karma across rebirths. Comparing Buddhist ethics with Islamic ethics (*al-akhlaq*), there is a notable intersection. The concept of intention (*cetana* in Buddhism) parallels the Prophetic principle "Actions are judged by intentions" (*innama al-a'mal bil-niyyat*). The purification of the soul from greed, anger, and ignorance in Islamic Sufism resembles the uprooting of the "three roots" in Buddhism, although the metaphysical framework differs fundamentally. Islamic ethics rests on monotheism, revelation, and divine accountability in the afterlife, whereas Buddhist ethics is built on an analysis of the nature of the self and suffering without assuming a creator god.

Illustrative example (hypothetical): a monk is faced with a person who has grievously harmed him. Instead of seeking revenge, he practices "meditation on loving-kindness" (*metta bhavana*), directing a wish for well-being even toward the one who harmed him. He does this not because an external law commands him to, but because anger itself, according to Buddhist analysis, keeps its bearer captive to attachment and suffering. Freeing the mind from resentment is therefore both an ethical act and an act of self-liberation.
