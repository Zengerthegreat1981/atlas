# Utilitarianism

Utilitarianism is a doctrine in ethics and legislation holding that the rightness of an act or rule is measured by the good that results for those affected by it, and that the good in question is happiness, or pleasure and the absence of pain. It was formulated in its classical form in Britain by Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), and Henry Sidgwick (1838–1900), and was preceded by ideas in Francis Hutcheson, David Hume, Claude Adrien Helvétius, and Cesare Beccaria. This page treats the classical formulation; the debates since the middle of the twentieth century are treated on the page on contemporary utilitarianism.

## Roots and Context

The doctrine emerged in industrial Britain alongside the movement for political and legal reform. Bentham, a London lawyer, wanted law to rest on a rational basis in place of custom and privilege, and its usefulness to be measured by its effect on people's welfare. He first formulated the principle of utility in *A Fragment on Government* (1776), and expounded it most fully in *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (printed 1780, published 1789), where he said that nature has placed mankind under two sovereign masters, pleasure and pain. Around him gathered the group of "Philosophical Radicals", among them James Mill (1773–1836), Bentham's close associate and the father of John Stuart, who educated his son in these ideas and helped to make utilitarianism the basis of a programme of political and legal reform.

## Bentham

Bentham devised what he called the "felicific calculus" for measuring the value of a pleasure or pain according to its intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent (the number of people affected). Each individual counts as one and none as more than one, so that no one has an advantage in the calculation. He applied utilitarianism to prison reform (the Panopticon, 1791), to the defence of freedom of interest (*Defence of Usury*, 1787), and to a critique of natural and imprescriptible rights as "nonsense upon stilts". He called for extending moral consideration to animals, saying that the question is not whether they can reason but whether they can suffer.

## Mill

In *Utilitarianism* (published in 1861 and as a book in 1863), Mill sought to modify Bentham's doctrine and to answer the objection against it. He said that pleasures differ in kind and not merely in quantity, that it is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied, and that anyone who has experienced both intellectual and sensual pleasures prefers the former. He tried to prove the principle by arguing that happiness is desirable and that each person desires his own happiness, so that general happiness is a good for all; many critics have judged this proof weak. He applied the doctrine in *On Liberty* (1859), making harm to others the only justification for society's interference with the individual's freedom, and in *The Subjection of Women* (1869), in defence of equality between the sexes.

## Sidgwick

Sidgwick wrote *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), the most rigorous exposition of the doctrine. He grounded utilitarianism on rational axioms that make the general good an end, and concluded that practical reason is torn between two principles he could not reconcile, self-interest and the general good, which he called the "dualism of practical reason." His book remains a model of precise philosophical analysis of moral doctrines.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Studies compare utilitarianism with Islamic thought on the concept of *maṣlaḥa* (public interest). Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH) made the preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny, and property the foundations of interests; al-Shatibi (d. 790 AH) built in *al-Muwafaqat* a theory of the objectives of the Sharia (*maqāṣid*) on securing benefits and averting harms; and Najm al-Din al-Tufi (d. 716 AH) held that interest takes precedence over speculative texts in matters of transactions. But this *maṣlaḥa* is bound by the texts and by fixed objectives, and is not an open calculation of pleasure and pain; hence Wael Hallaq in *A History of Islamic Legal Theories* (1997) stresses that the comparison requires caution. In the modern era, Rifa'a al-Tahtawi (1801–1873) transmitted ideas of "public utility" from the French, and Lutfi al-Sayyid (1872–1963) was influenced by Mill and English liberalism, according to Albert Hourani's account in *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age*.

## Criticism

Thomas Carlyle derided the doctrine as a "pig philosophy", and Mill replied by distinguishing among pleasures. Critics have objected that it sacrifices the individual for the sake of the collective, gives no weight to justice and rights, and that happiness is hard to measure and to aggregate across persons. The question of act utilitarianism versus rule utilitarianism will remain a matter of dispute; this is a distinction the classical authors did not use under those names, but which was formulated later.

## Influence on Reform

The doctrine left a practical mark on British legislation in the nineteenth century: Edwin Chadwick was associated with it in public health reform, as were the Poor Law reforms (1834) and the organization of public administration, and campaigns to extend the franchise and reform the criminal law. Mill himself took up the call for the representation of minorities and for women's right to vote while a Member of Parliament (1865–1868).
