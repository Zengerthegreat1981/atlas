# Empirical Legal Studies (ELS)

A relatively recent methodological field. Its name and institutional identity crystallized from the turn of the millennium, with the founding of the *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies* in 2004. It applies rigorous empirical social research tools (statistics, quantitative models, controlled experiments) to legal questions, going beyond the traditional reliance on the analysis of judicial texts and theoretical reflection alone.

## Difference from earlier legal realism

The field shares with **American legal realism** a focus on "what actually happens" rather than "what the rule formally says". The difference is methodologically fundamental. The early realists (such as Llewellyn and Frank, with Holmes as a precursor) relied mainly on **observation and unstructured professional intuition**, whereas empirical legal studies requires a **rigorous research design** (representative samples, controlled variables, statistical tests of significance) before accepting any claim about judicial behavior or the effect of a given law.

## Examples of its typical questions

Researchers in this field ask questions directly amenable to quantitative measurement. Do conviction rulings statistically vary by the race of the defendant or the victim when other factors are controlled? Does a particular law actually reduce crime rates compared with states that did not adopt it (using quasi-experimental designs such as difference-in-differences)? Does a judge's partisan background statistically predict the pattern of rulings in particular cases?

## The intersection with law and economics

The field intersects heavily with **law and economics**, since econometric tools supply many of the statistical techniques it uses. Empirical legal studies is broader methodologically, however, as it also incorporates sociological and experimental psychological methods that are not limited to the economic assumptions of the rational actor.

## The use of quasi-experimental designs to evaluate the effects of laws

Researchers in this field rely on a family of **quasi-experimental designs** to approximate causal inference when a rigorous randomized trial cannot be conducted directly on an actual law:

- The **difference-in-differences** design compares the change in a variable (such as the crime rate) before and after a law is applied in a state that adopted it, against a similar state that did not adopt it in the same period.
- The **regression discontinuity** design exploits sharp legal thresholds (such as a particular legal age or an income ceiling for eligibility for a program) to compare those who fall just on either side of the threshold directly.
- The **instrumental variables** design exploits external random variation (such as the random assignment of judges to cases) to isolate the effect of one variable from other confounding factors.

## Critique of the field and its methodological limits

Several methodological criticisms have been directed at the field. Many legal questions, such as interpreting an ambiguous constitutional text, cannot easily be reduced to quantitatively measurable variables. The availability of structured judicial data, such as databases of American judgments, biases empirical research toward data-rich common law systems at the expense of other systems that are less digitally documented. Causal inference in law, that is, whether law X actually causes change Y in behavior or whether other confounding variables are at work, remains difficult even with the most advanced quasi-experimental designs. Nevertheless, the field continues to expand with the growing availability of digital judicial and administrative data and the development of machine learning tools for analyzing large legal texts (legal text mining). This opens new prospects for testing hypotheses that could not previously be tested quantitatively.

## Expansion beyond the United States

The field arose primarily in American universities but gradually spread to Europe and Asia, with the founding of journals and conferences dedicated to it outside the American context. Researchers have increasingly studied non-Anglo-Saxon legal systems statistically, for example by examining court performance in European civil law systems or the relationship between legal reform and economic growth in developing economies. This expansion remains relatively limited compared with the depth of data accumulation in the American context. It reflects, however, a growing recognition that general theoretical claims about the behavior of legal institutions need testing across multiple legal and cultural contexts before being generalized.
