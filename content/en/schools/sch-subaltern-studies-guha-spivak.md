# Subaltern Studies and Sociological History from Below

An Indian research group founded by Ranajit Guha to rewrite the history of colonial India from the perspective of the "Subaltern": the peasants, workers and marginalized people whom both colonial and elite nationalist historical writing had erased.

## The Central Idea

In *Subaltern Studies* (a series begun in 1982), Guha criticized both British colonial historiography (which portrayed Indian resistance as irrational disorder lacking political consciousness) and elite Indian nationalist historiography (which reduced the history of independence to the leadership of the educated elite, marginalizing the role of the ordinary peasant masses). Both, he argued, ignored the subaltern's real political consciousness and independent resistance. Gayatri Spivak took from this project her famous question "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988). She argued that the subaltern, especially the subaltern woman in the colonies, has in practice no audible voice in either discourse (the colonial, or the elite local nationalist one opposed to it). Even the attempt by a researcher to "represent" her, including the postcolonial researcher's own attempt, risks silencing her again by speaking "on her behalf" instead of enabling her to express herself directly on her own terms.

## Historical Context: The Subaltern Studies Collective

The "Subaltern Studies Collective" was founded in the early 1980s by Ranajit Guha, an Indian historian (1923–2023) who taught in Britain (University of Sussex) and later in Australia (Australian National University). He worked with a group of young Indian historians influenced by a mixture of unorthodox Marxism (especially Gramsci's concept of hegemony, which Guha borrowed directly) and the post-structuralist critique of colonial historical texts. The group's work appeared in a series of collective volumes titled *Subaltern Studies* beginning in 1982, with contributions from historians such as Dipesh Chakrabarty and Partha Chatterjee alongside Guha himself.

## Borrowing the Concept of the "Subaltern" from Gramsci

Guha borrowed the term "subaltern" (in British military usage, a junior officer) from Antonio Gramsci, who used it to refer to subordinate social classes that lack unified, independent political and cultural representation within the existing hegemony. Guha extended the concept to include all the marginalized groups in Indian colonial society (the peasants above all, but also workers, women and the socially lower classes). These groups produced their own forms of political and cultural resistance (peasant uprisings, symbolic rituals of rebellion), which neither colonial historiography nor the later elite nationalist historiography recognized as conscious political action with its own logic.

## Spivak's Critique: Double Representation

Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, the Indian-born literary theorist working in the United States, engaged with the group, criticizing and extending its project through deconstruction, Marxism and feminism. She warned of the dangers of what she calls "representation" (Representation), which has a double meaning, drawing on Marx's distinction between *Vertretung* and *Darstellung*: political representation (speaking on behalf of) and artistic or epistemic representation (depicting or portraying). These two meanings overlap in a way that conceals a real process of silencing, even in discourses that claim in good faith to "represent" the subaltern's interest. Her famous analysis focused specifically on the case of the Indian subaltern woman (in the context of the practice of sati, the widow's self-immolation). It showed how layers of silencing intersect in her case: British colonialism, which supposedly "saved" her from a barbaric local tradition (in Spivak's phrase, white men saving brown women from brown men), and patriarchal indigenous discourse, which defended the tradition in the name of "authenticity." The subaltern woman herself thus disappears between the two competing discourses, without her direct voice being heard in either.

## Impact

This current established "Subaltern Studies" as a sociological-historical method with worldwide influence in writing the history of the Global South from below. Its influence spread from India to Latin America (through the Latin American Subaltern Studies Group formed in the early 1990s, which acknowledged the Indian model) and to other regions, and it influenced postcolonial theory and postcolonial feminism deeply, as well as the reassessment of the methods of writing social history in general.

## Additional Note

Some of the group's founding members, especially Dipesh Chakrabarty, later developed an independent intellectual path closer to a philosophical critique of Eurocentrism in the writing of history (*Provincializing Europe*, 2000, which draws on but is not limited to the group's concerns), while others distanced themselves from the original group as internal methodological disagreements grew over whether the subaltern's "voice" can be recovered historically at all.

Edward Said, although the project of *Orientalism* (1978) differed from Subaltern Studies in focusing on European colonial discourse rather than on recovering the colonized's voice, wrote the foreword to *Selected Subaltern Studies* (1988), edited by Guha and Spivak, which helped bring the group to a wider international readership.
