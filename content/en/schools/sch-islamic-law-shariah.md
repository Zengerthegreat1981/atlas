# Islamic Law and Sharia

A field of comparative legal theory that examines the structure of Sharia as a comprehensive normative system combining a religious-ethical dimension with an organized legal dimension, and how its practical rulings (fiqh) are derived from its foundational sources through the methodology of usul al-fiqh.

## Conceptual Structure

- **Sharia versus fiqh**: A fundamental distinction in Islamic thought between "Sharia," the fixed and universal divine principles derived directly from revelation, and "fiqh," the human, interpretive understanding of these principles and their application to changing circumstances. Fiqh varies with the number of schools and jurists, and like any human understanding it can be right or wrong, unlike Sharia itself.
- **The overarching objectives (maqasid) of Sharia**: An interpretive framework that developed gradually in medieval Islamic jurisprudence, reaching its theoretical peak with al-Shatibi in the 8th century AH. It holds that the detailed rulings of Sharia serve five overarching objectives: the preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny and property. This opens room for reading particular rulings in light of their greater purposes, not merely their literal wording.
- **Plurality of sources and schools**: As in usul al-fiqh generally, Sharia rests on the Qur'an, the Sunna, consensus (ijma') and analogy (qiyas) as agreed sources, with additional tools disputed among the schools (istihsan, public interest or maslaha mursala). This produces legitimate internal juristic plurality, not necessarily a contradiction between one school and another.
- **Contemporary application**: Muslim-majority states today differ widely in the extent to which Sharia is adopted as an official source of legislation, ranging from its exclusive application in some areas of personal status law to partial integration with positive legal systems derived from European legal traditions (civil law or common law).

## Historical Development

Islamic legal reasoning took systematic form in the second and third centuries AH (eighth and ninth centuries CE). Al-Shafi'i's *al-Risala* is traditionally regarded as the first sustained treatise on legal methodology. Over time the Sunni jurists consolidated into four enduring schools (madhahib), the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali, alongside Shi'i traditions such as the Ja'fari school and the Ibadi school. Classical jurists also developed a body of legal maxims (qawa'id fiqhiyya) and a literature on legal purposes. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, codification and state legislation, notably the Ottoman Majalla (1870s) and later civil codes in Arab states, reshaped the relationship between fiqh and state law.

## Limitations

- The term "Sharia" is sometimes used in public (media and political) discourse in a simplified or reduced sense that does not reflect the actual juristic and historical complexity of the concept within the Islamic tradition itself.
- The debate over the relationship of Sharia to modern state concepts (constitutionalism, human rights, separation of powers) is live and unresolved, and juristic and political positions on it vary widely.
