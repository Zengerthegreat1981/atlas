# Evolutionary Psychology

Evolutionary psychology applies the principles of evolution and natural selection to understand human behavior, cognition, and emotion. It assumes that psychological mechanisms evolved to solve adaptive problems faced by ancestors in ancestral environments, enabling survival and reproduction.

## Historical Development

**Foundations (1859 to the 1970s):**
- Charles Darwin: evolution and the descent of man (1859, 1871)
- William James: functionalism; habits and instincts as adaptations
- Edward Thorndike and B.F. Skinner: the evolution of behavior through learning

**Modern emergence (1970s-1990s):**
- Jerome Barkow, Leda Cosmides and John Tooby (eds.): *The Adapted Mind* (1992), the computational theory of mind
- David Buss: sexual selection, the psychology of mating
- Martin Daly and Margo Wilson: homicide and domestic violence from an evolutionary perspective
- Randy Thornhill and Craig Palmer: *A Natural History of Rape* (2000) and the controversy over rape as an adaptation
- Steven Pinker: *How the Mind Works* (1997), the evolutionary synthesis

**Contemporary (2000s to the present):**
- Evolutionary clinical psychology: dysfunction as malfunction or by-product
- Gene-culture co-evolution approaches
- Evolutionary developmental psychology: individual development and evolution
- Cross-cultural testing of evolutionary predictions

## Core Theoretical Concepts

### Natural Selection and Adaptation

**Selection pressures:**
- Survival (predators, disease, famine, climate)
- Reproduction (mate attraction, fertility, parental investment)
- Kin selection (helping relatives who share genes)
- Reciprocal altruism (cooperation with non-kin)

**Adaptive mechanisms:**
- Psychological traits that increased fitness in the ancestral environment
- They may no longer be optimal in the modern environment (evolutionary mismatch)
- Domain-specific modules versus general-purpose learning

### The Environment of Evolutionary Adaptedness (EEA)

**The ancestral context:**
- The Pleistocene: African savanna, small groups, high mortality
- No antibiotics, modern medicine, technology, or institutional structures
- Scarcity of food, predation, disease, challenges of social hierarchy
- Reproductive competition, pair-bonding dilemmas, parental uncertainty

**Implications of the EEA:**
- We possess mechanisms adapted to the ancestral environment, not the modern one
- Technology and culture have outpaced evolutionary change by a wide margin
- This explains why modern stimuli trigger ancient responses (craving fast food, status anxiety)

### Reproductive Success and Inclusive Fitness

**The measure of natural selection:**
- Inclusive fitness: genes passed on to offspring plus genes shared with relatives
- Sexual selection: traits that increase mating success (the peacock's tail, human status)
- Parental investment theory: different selection pressures on males and females
- Intrasexual competition: competition for mates
- Intersexual choice: mate-selection strategies

**Sex differences in mating psychology:**
- Females: higher parental investment, greater selectivity about partners, a pursuit of long-term commitment
- Males: lower investment, a drive to compete, a tendency toward quantity in mating
- However: human mating is highly flexible, biparental, and culturally variable

## Main Areas of Study

### The Psychology of Mating and Sex

**Core mechanisms:**
- Sexual jealousy (mate guarding and paternity uncertainty in males; emotional infidelity in females)
- Criteria of attractiveness (indicators of health, status, fertility cues)
- Disparities in parental investment
- Mate attraction and same-sex competition
- Provisioning of offspring and childcare

**Adaptive problems solved:**
- Identifying partners of reproductive value
- Assessing a partner's commitment and quality
- Ensuring genetic paternity
- Competing with same-sex rivals
- Maintaining pair bonds

### Aggression and Violence

**Evolutionary triggers:**
- Same-sex competition for mates and resources (male violence peaks in the reproductive years)
- The pursuit of status and dominance hierarchies
- Kin coalitions and group conflict
- Acquiring and defending resources
- Female aggression: coalition-based, defense of resources, mate guarding

**Maladaptive violence:**
- Modern mismatch: dominance violence in low-violence societies
- Impulsive homicide is often low in benefit (violating the evolutionary cost/benefit model)
- The controversy over sexual coercion: an adaptation, a by-product, or a social construction?

### Fear and Anxiety

**Adaptive fears:**
- Predators, snakes, heights, darkness, strangers (ancient threats)
- Social anxiety (status, rejection in group contexts)
- Fear of contamination (avoidance of pathogens)
- Separation anxiety (protecting the young, maintaining proximity)

**Modern phobias:**
- Fear of modern dangers (cars, electricity) is learned less readily
- Preparedness theory: evolutionary history shapes learning biases
- Social anxiety persists despite the absence of physical danger
- Anxiety disorders as hyperactive threat-detection systems

### Cooperation and Altruism

**Kin selection:**
- Hamilton's rule: helping relatives in proportion to shared genes
- Nepotism is universal, but its extent varies culturally
- Parental care is obligatory; helping relatives is conditional

**Reciprocal altruism:**
- Trivers's theory: helping others in expectation of return
- It requires memory, cheater detection, and delayed reciprocity
- Reputation tracking; gossip as a cheater detector
- It explains human friendship, alliance, and morality

**Group selection (controversial):**
- Multilevel selection: cooperation within groups, competition between groups
- Parochial altruism: favoring the in-group and discriminating against the out-group
- Moral systems: coalition psychology, enforcement of justice

### Attachment and the Family

**Parent-child bonding:**
- Infant signals (crying, cuteness) elicit caregiving
- Separation distress (ensuring that proximity is maintained)
- The secure base for exploration (balancing safety and learning)
- Critical periods are debatable; plasticity is greater than previously thought

**Sibling dynamics:**
- Conflict over parental investment (divergence of individual reproductive interests)
- Alliance (warmth and closeness are often reported as greater between same-sex siblings; Buhrmester & Furman, 1990)
- Birth-order effects: the firstborn's privilege or the flexibility of later-borns

**Family violence:**
- Stepparent risk: the Cinderella hypothesis (a higher risk of child abuse with a non-kin parent)
- Triggers of infanticide by parents: severe burden, paternity uncertainty, disability
- Incest avoidance: the Westermarck effect (childhood co-rearing reduces sexual attraction)

### Social Status and Dominance

**The pursuit of hierarchy:**
- Coalition politics: male coalitions compete for status
- Gaining status through competence, generosity, and toughness
- Cultural variation: egalitarian versus authoritarian hierarchies
- Female status through other coalitions, caregiving, and prestige

**Conspicuous consumption:**
- Signaling fitness through costly displays (resources, health, intelligence)
- Fashion, luxury goods, and physical training as status signals
- Flaunting: exaggerated traits that exceed practical function

### Moral Psychology and Justice

**Moral intuitions (using Haidt's moral foundations framework, which is evolutionarily inspired but not part of evolutionary psychology proper):**
- Detecting fairness and reciprocity (cheater detection)
- The harm/care foundation of morality
- Loyalty/betrayal (in-group cohesion)
- Authority/subversion (respect for hierarchy)
- Sanctity/degradation (contamination and purity)

**Punishment and revenge:**
- A preference for proportional justice (an eye for an eye)
- Enforcing cooperation (punishing free riders)
- Restoring reputation (cycles of escalating violence)
- Forgiveness is conditional on remorse

## Therapeutic Applications

These applications are largely conceptual: evolutionary clinical psychology offers hypotheses and ways of framing problems, and has limited controlled trial evidence of its own.

### Evolutionary Clinical Psychology

**Reframing disorder:**
- Psychopathology as malfunction (a broken mechanism) versus by-product (a structural parasitism)
- Some disorders: hyperactive ancestral mechanisms (anxiety, disgust, jealousy)
- Others: problems of modern mismatch (internet addiction, body image)
- Evolutionary psychotherapy: normalizing the adaptive roots and managing the maladaptive expression

**Therapeutic implications:**
- Anxiety: acknowledging that ancestral fears are valid but hyperactive; exposure therapy as disconfirmation of expectations
- Depression: social rank theory (depressive responses as yielding in a costly contest for status); reaffirming agency
- Sexual dysfunction: sex roles and cultural expectations versus evolved preferences
- Personality disorders: adaptive strategies in the wrong context (harshness is adaptive in a harsh environment, pathological elsewhere)

### Couple and Family Therapy

**Mating conflicts:**
- Intervening in sexual jealousy: understanding the evolutionary triggers and building trust
- Parental-investment conflicts: partners' expectations about child-rearing and the division of labor
- Marital infidelity: a biological drive and cultural rules together; context matters
- Communication: understanding the divergent reproductive interests of males and females

**Parent-child therapy:**
- Sibling rivalry: normative competition for parental investment
- Discipline: balancing affection (attachment) with limits (behavioral shaping)
- Adolescent rebellion: coalition politics with peers versus family authority

## Research Methods

**The comparative approach:**
- Behavior across species: primates, chimpanzees, bonobos, other mammals
- Cross-cultural human studies: testing predictions across societies
- Historical analysis: rates of polygyny, war, mating systems

**Experimental approaches:**
- Hypothetical scenarios: jealousy triggers, criteria of attractiveness
- Behavioral measures: time allocation, resource sharing, aggression
- Neuroimaging: brain activity during morally significant scenarios
- Hormonal correlates: testosterone and dominance, oxytocin and bonding

**Computational modeling:**
- Evolutionary game theory: strategies, arms races, stable equilibria
- Agent-based models: simulating selection pressures across generations
- Ideal Bayesian agents: what a rational decision-maker should do

## Strengths

- **A unifying framework:** it explains diverse behaviors through a single principle
- **Cross-cultural prediction:** hypotheses that can be tested and falsified across populations
- **Integration:** it links psychology to biology, anthropology, and ecology
- **Clinical relevance:** it offers hypotheses about the origins of dysfunction
- **Explanatory power:** it accounts for biases and behaviors that would otherwise be puzzling

## Limitations and Criticisms

**Philosophical:**
- "Just-so" stories: unfalsifiable retrospective explanations (the adaptationist assumption)
- Genetic determinism: it overstates genetic influence and understates learning and culture
- Ethical implications: the naturalistic fallacy (what is, is what ought to be)

**Methodological:**
- The EEA is unknown: actual ancestral pressures are only imperfectly reconstructed
- The assumption of modularity: domain-specific modules have not been empirically verified
- The reverse-engineering problem: observing a trait does not prove a claim of adaptation
- Alternative explanations: by-products, cultural evolution, individual development

**Empirical:**
- Cross-cultural variation sometimes contradicts universal predictions
- Mating theory: it simplifies human pair bonds and overlooks love and commitment
- Sex differences: many predictions are weak or contradicted by research
- Modern behavior: not a clear ancestral adaptation (internet use, accumulation of wealth)

**Political:**
- Misuse to justify gender inequality
- Racism: evolutionary arguments have been used to defend ethnocentrism
- Reductionism: it flattens cultural meaning and historical contingency

## Contemporary Integration

**Modern evolutionary psychology emphasizes:**
- Gene-culture co-evolution: genetic and cultural evolution interact
- Developmental plasticity: ancestral mechanisms are flexible and sensitive to input
- Evoked versus transmitted culture: behavior responds to local conditions
- Pluralism: evolution is one lens among several (not the only explanation)

**Interdisciplinary links:**
- Behavioral ecology: applying evolutionary logic across species
- Anthropology: testing predictions in diverse societies
- Neuroscience: mapping ancient mechanisms onto the modern brain
- Medicine: evolutionary psychiatry, treating dysfunction as malfunction
