# Cognitive Anthropology

Cognitive anthropology is the branch of anthropology that studies how people think — how they classify the world, remember, reason, and solve problems — and how those mental processes are shaped by culture. Its central question is whether the mind works the same way everywhere or whether different cultures produce different minds. Where psychologists study thinking in the laboratory, cognitive anthropologists study it in the field, asking how a farmer classifies plants, how a navigator finds his way without instruments, or how a child learns the colour words of her language. The field's founding claim is that culture is not only out there, in rituals and institutions, but in here, in the mind: it is a system of knowledge, stored in people's heads and shared between them, and the anthropologist's job is to describe that system as precisely as a linguist describes a grammar.

## Origins and History

Cognitive anthropology emerged in the United States in the 1950s and 1960s out of the meeting of anthropology and linguistics. Ward Goodenough argued in 1957 that culture should be defined as the knowledge people need in order to behave in ways acceptable to the members of their society, and that the ethnographer's task was to write the rules of that knowledge. Charles Frake developed the method of ethnographic semantics, eliciting the categories people use to classify disease, kin, and the natural world. The early work was formal and linguistic: the anthropologist collected terms, worked out how they were organized into taxonomies, and wrote the rules by which people made judgements.

The field's most famous finding came from the study of colour. Brent Berlin and Paul Kay's Basic Color Terms (1969) showed that, although languages divide the spectrum differently, the basic colour words of the world's languages follow a predictable order of emergence, from black and white through red to green, yellow, and blue. The result suggested that human perception constrains culture: the eye is the same everywhere, and languages build on its structure. The finding was controversial — critics argued it underplayed cultural difference — and the debate it launched shaped the field for decades. In the 1970s and 1980s the field broadened from classification to cognition in the round: how people reason, how they remember, how they use models of the world to act. Roy D'Andrade's The Development of Cognitive Anthropology (1995) gave the field its history and its synthesis, distinguishing the study of shared cultural models — the taken-for-granted understandings that guide everyday life — from the study of individual minds.

## Key Figures and Works

Ward Goodenough's "Cultural Anthropology and Linguistics" (1957) founded the field's program. Brent Berlin and Paul Kay's Basic Color Terms (1969) provided its most famous empirical result. Charles Frake's studies of disease classification among the Subanun of the Philippines showed how a folk taxonomy could be described with the rigour of a grammar. Harold Conklin's work on Hanunóo plant classification demonstrated that indigenous knowledge of the natural world was systematic and detailed, challenging the idea that folk knowledge was vague or primitive. Roy D'Andrade's The Development of Cognitive Anthropology (1995) remains the standard account of the field's history. Stephen Levinson's Space in Language and Cognition (2003) showed that the way a language talks about space — whether it uses left and right, or north and south, or uphill and downhill — shapes how its speakers remember and navigate, an influential argument that language and thought are linked. More recently, cognitive anthropologists have studied distributed cognition: the idea, developed by Edwin Hutchins in Cognition in the Wild (1995), that thinking does not happen only inside heads but across people and tools, as when a ship's crew navigates together.

## Method

The field's methods are among the most formal in anthropology. Ethnographic semantics elicits the terms people use for a domain and works out their structure through sorting tasks, pile sorts, and triad tests, in which informants group items or judge which two of three are most alike. Free listing asks people to name all the members of a category — all the illnesses, all the foods — and measures which items are most salient. Consensus analysis, developed by A. Kimball Romney with Susan Weller and William Batchelder, tests whether a group shares a single cultural model by measuring agreement across informants. These methods are combined with classic ethnography: the formal models are checked against what people actually do. The field's methods have been adopted far beyond anthropology, in market research, in the study of expertise, and in the design of artificial intelligence systems that must model human categories.

## Influence and Legacies

Cognitive anthropology changed anthropology's idea of culture, making it a mental as well as a social phenomenon, and it changed psychology, showing that the laboratory's picture of the mind was incomplete without cultural variation. Its findings on colour shaped the debate about linguistic relativity — the question of whether language shapes thought — and Levinson's work on space revived that debate in a new form. The concept of the cultural model has become a standard tool in the social sciences, used to study how people understand illness, risk, and the economy. The field's methods have influenced cognitive science, where the study of folk categories informs research on concepts and categorization. Its current frontier is the meeting with neuroscience and with the study of religion: how the mind produces religious experience, how rituals are remembered, and how cultural models of the self shape mental health. The question Goodenough posed in 1957 — what must a person know to be a member of a culture — remains the field's guiding question.
