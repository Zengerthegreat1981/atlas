# Schumpeterian Evolutionary Economics

**Schumpeterian evolutionary economics** is a current in political economy that views the capitalist economy as a **dynamic system that is inherently out of balance**, driven by successive waves of technological and organizational innovation that destroy existing sectors and technologies and create new ones. It does not regard the economy as a system that tends automatically toward static stability, as the neoclassical theory of perfect competition assumes. The origin of this current lies with the Austrian economist **Joseph Schumpeter**. From the early 1980s it developed into a formal research programme known as "evolutionary economics," through the work of Richard Nelson and Sidney Winter.

## Schumpeter: The Innovating Entrepreneur and the Business Cycle

Schumpeter was trained in Vienna under Austrian-school economists, notably Friedrich von Wieser and Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, and engaged with the German Historical School's debates, but he belonged exclusively to neither and developed an independent dynamic method.

In his early book *The Theory of Economic Development* (*Theorie der wirtschaftlichen Entwicklung*, 1911), Schumpeter distinguished between the "static circular flow," an economy that repeats itself without fundamental change, and genuine **"development,"** driven by the **entrepreneur** who carries out "new combinations" of the factors of production: a new product, a new method of production, a new market, a new source of raw materials, or a new form of industrial organization (such as a monopoly, or its breaking up). This innovation gives the entrepreneur a temporary extraordinary profit before competitors imitate it and dissolve it. The resulting waves of innovation, rather than any equilibrating mechanism, drive the cyclical movement of the economy.

## Creative Destruction

In his later American book *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942), Schumpeter formulated his most famous concept: **"creative destruction."** The essence of capitalist dynamism, he argued, is not calm price competition within a stable industrial structure, but successive waves of radical technological innovation (the railway, electricity, the automobile). These destroy existing industries, skills and firms entirely, as they create new ones. Consequently, even the **temporary monopoly** produced by a successful innovation is not necessarily an obstacle to long-term dynamic efficiency, because it is precisely what motivates investment in the next innovation, which will later destroy it.

In the same book Schumpeter controversially predicted that capitalism might erode itself through its very success. The bureaucracy of large corporations weakens the spirit of individual enterprise, and a critical intellectual class arises from within the system. This would gradually open the way for forms of administrative socialism.

## Nelson and Winter: Organizational Routines in Place of Maximal Rationality

**Richard Nelson** and **Sidney Winter** restated the Schumpeterian intuition in a rigorous analytical framework in their joint book *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change* (1982), borrowing an explicit biological analogy. Instead of assuming that firms maximize profits rationally, with complete information at every moment, they proposed that firms operate through **"routines"**: patterns of behaviour and decision rules accumulated historically, which the firm repeats almost automatically, much as genes are inherited. **Innovation** appears as random or deliberate variations of these routines, which then undergo a process of **"selection"** in the market. This keeps the most successful routines and eliminates the least profitable, resembling Darwinian natural selection without presupposing extreme rationality in advance.

## Influence on the Economics of Innovation and Industrial Policy

This current established an entire subfield in the economics of innovation and technological change. It has influenced the study of "national innovation systems" and policies supporting research and development. It offers an explanatory alternative for the growth of firms and industries that does not presuppose a static equilibrium, but rather branching historical trajectories that depend on the accumulation of earlier organizational capabilities. This connects it later to the concept of path dependence in new institutional economics.

## An Economics Distant from the Equilibrium Assumption

This current shares a unifying methodological trait that distinguishes it from most branches of mainstream economics. It rejects the assumption that the economy tends automatically toward a stable equilibrium state that can be described mathematically as a fixed point. It insists instead that **disequilibrium** is the normal and productive state of a living capitalist economy. It holds that attempting to understand growth and innovation through static equilibrium tools (such as supply-and-demand analysis at a single fixed point) necessarily overlooks the real source of most of the great economic gains in history.

This methodological stance brings the current close to a shared intuition with the German Historical School, which likewise rejected static abstraction. The two differed radically in their later analytical tools, however: the German Historical School moved toward detailed empirical description, while the evolutionary Schumpeterians moved toward rigorous mathematical modelling and computer simulation of the change process itself.

## From an Individual Insight to a Collective Research Programme

Schumpeter's original ideas remained for decades a penetrating theoretical intuition, but they were not formulated with rigorous analytical tools amenable to systematic testing. Thanks to the work of Nelson and Winter, they became an organized collective research programme. This enabled a whole generation of later researchers to build mathematical models and computer simulations that test specific Schumpeterian hypotheses (such as the effect of firm size on the rate of innovation, or the effect of industry structure on the speed of diffusion of new technologies) with an empirical precision not available in Schumpeter's own time. This explains the field's steady growth and expansion since the 1980s up to the present.
