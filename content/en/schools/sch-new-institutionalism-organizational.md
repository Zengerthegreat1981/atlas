# New Institutionalism in Organizational Sociology

A theory developed by Paul DiMaggio and Walter Powell to explain why organizations in a given field become similar over time, even when this similarity does not necessarily serve economic efficiency.

## The Central Idea

In their seminal article "The Iron Cage Revisited" (1983, with an explicit reference to Weber's metaphor), DiMaggio and Powell formulated the concept of "institutional isomorphism": organizations operating in the same field tend toward structural similarity through three mechanisms. **Coercive isomorphism** arises from formal legal or regulatory pressures that impose uniform standards. **Mimetic isomorphism** is the imitation of successful or prestigious organizations under conditions of uncertainty, even without evidence of their actual effectiveness. **Normative isomorphism** is the spread of uniform professional standards through shared training and professional networks (such as accountants or lawyers trained according to uniform standard curricula). Unlike economic theory, which assumes that organizations become alike because the most efficient solution wins out competitively, this current argued that similarity often arises from the need for social "legitimacy" and not from technical efficiency alone.

## Context: The Critique of Rational Efficiency Theory

This school emerged as a response to an earlier current in the sociology of organizations that assumed that the structure of organizations is shaped essentially as a rational response to the demands of technical efficiency and adaptation to a competitive market environment. DiMaggio and Powell drew on an earlier theoretical root laid down by John Meyer and Brian Rowan in their seminal article "Institutionalized Organizations: Formal Structure as Myth and Ceremony" (1977). That article argued that many of the formal structures of modern organizations (particular administrative departments, particular procedures, particular job titles) are adopted because they carry a socially recognized institutional symbolic "legitimacy," not because they are necessarily the most technically efficient, and that organizations sometimes maintain a kind of "decoupling" between this formal structure and their actual day-to-day operational activity.

## Organizational Fields and World Society

DiMaggio and Powell developed the concept of the "organizational field": the totality of organizations (competing and cooperating, suppliers, customers and regulatory bodies) that together constitute a recognized area of institutional life, and within which institutional isomorphism tends to appear after an initial founding stage that often sees greater diversity and innovation. John Meyer later extended this framework in "World Society Theory," holding that certain organizational models (such as the modern bureaucratic nation-state, the university and human rights) spread globally through international organizations and shared normative standards, regardless of the deep cultural or economic differences among the states that adopt them. Meyer and colleagues point to similarities in the formal shape of states and school systems across very different contexts as evidence of this process.

## A Later Extension: Fligstein's Conceptions of Control

Neil Fligstein, a leading figure in the new economic sociology, which overlaps with this current, developed the concept of "conceptions of control" (in *The Transformation of Corporate Control*, 1990) to explain how, within each organizational field (such as a particular industry), a shared, unwritten understanding forms among the major actors about the acceptable "rules of the game" for competition and cooperation, and how these conceptions change historically in response to crises or new governmental regulatory interventions. In this way he linked new institutionalism to a deeper analysis of power and stability within capitalist markets.

## Impact

This theory became a fundamental reference framework in contemporary organizational sociology. It explains widespread phenomena such as the adoption by entirely different firms of similar management or marketing standards (such as total quality management systems or corporate social responsibility standards) despite their radically different operational and cultural contexts, and it influenced neighboring fields from the sociology of education to studies of institutional globalization.

## Concluding Note

Scholars usually distinguish between this current ("sociological new institutionalism") and a parallel current bearing the same name but originating in economics (the new institutional economics of Douglass North). The sociological current focuses on symbolic legitimacy and shared cultural norms as the main explanatory factor, while the economic current focuses on reducing transaction costs. This is a fundamental methodological difference that should not be confused despite the similarity of the names.

## Additional Clarification on Normative Isomorphism

Normative isomorphism is clearly evident in regulated professions with uniform certifications and licenses (accounting, medicine, law, engineering). There, uniform professional training programs and the networks of professional associations that cut across organizations produce employees who carry the same organizational and cognitive assumptions regardless of the organization in which they actually work, and they carry this normative isomorphism with them as they move between different organizations within the same organizational field.
