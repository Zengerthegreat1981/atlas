# Classical Marxism

Classical Marxism is a philosophical, economic and political movement founded by Karl Marx (1818–1883) and Friedrich Engels (1820–1895). It set out from a critique of Hegelian philosophy, from a critique of the classical political economy of Adam Smith and David Ricardo, and from French socialism. Among its most prominent texts are the "Communist Manifesto" (1848) and the first volume of "Capital" (1867). "Classical" here means what the two founders themselves wrote, not the varied schools built upon it after them.

## Intellectual Formation

Marx studied law and philosophy in Bonn and Berlin and joined the "Young Hegelians," who made the Hegelian dialectic an instrument for criticizing religion and the state. He was influenced by Feuerbach in his critique of religion as man's alienation from his essence, then went beyond him in the "Theses on Feuerbach" (1845), where he said that philosophers have interpreted the world and that the point is to change it. In Paris (1844) he began his lasting collaboration with Engels, who had written "The Condition of the Working Class in England" (1845), and they collaborated on "The German Ideology" (written 1845–1846 and published in full in 1932). After the revolutions of 1848 Marx settled in London and spent decades studying economics at the British Museum.

## Foundations

- **Historical materialism**: the development of societies is determined by their mode of production, that is, by the forces of production and the relations of production, upon which rests a superstructure of law, the state and ideas. Marx summarized this conception in the preface to "A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy" (1859).
- **Class struggle**: the "Communist Manifesto" says that the history of societies to date is the history of class struggles, and that the bourgeoisie has created the proletariat that will be its gravedigger.
- **Critique of political economy**: in "Capital" Marx analyzed the commodity and value and distinguished between abstract labour and concrete labour, and explained profit through "surplus value," arising from the difference between the value of labour power and the value of what it produces. He also dealt with "commodity fetishism," that is, the appearance of social relations between people in the form of relations between things.
- **Alienation**: in the "Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts" (written 1844, published 1932) he described the worker's alienation from his product, from his activity, from his "species-being," and from others.
- **State and revolution**: the state is explained as the instrument of a ruling class, and in the "Critique of the Gotha Programme" (1875) Marx envisages a transitional stage of the "dictatorship of the proletariat" preceding communist society.

The term "dialectical materialism" was not used by Marx himself; it was popularized by Plekhanov (the German worker-philosopher Joseph Dietzgen had used it earlier) and then by Soviet Marxism on the basis of Engels's writings ("Anti-Dühring," 1878, and the unfinished "Dialectics of Nature," published in 1925), and this is an important difference between Marx and the later tradition.

## Religion, Colonialism and the East

Marx dealt with religion in "A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right" (1843–1844), describing it as "the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people," a phrase often cut from its context, which combines condemnation and sympathy. In 1853 he wrote articles in the "New York Tribune" on British rule in India, in which he saw it as destroying the old structures but also spreading the conditions for capitalist transformation, and he spoke of the "Asiatic mode of production." These writings have drawn later criticism, including what Edward Said said in "Orientalism" about the European view of the East, and for this reason Third World scholars re-read Marx's position on colonialism.

## Stages of Intellectual Production

Many scholars distinguish between the "young Marx" (until 1845), who is preoccupied with alienation and man, and the "mature Marx" of the "Grundrisse" (1857–1858, published 1939–1941) and "Capital." Althusser made this difference an "epistemological break," while others saw continuity between the two. After Marx's death Engels published the second (1885) and third (1894) volumes of "Capital" from his drafts, and wrote "The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State" (1884).

## Influence and Reception

Marxism influenced social democracy (Bernstein and Kautsky), then Leninism ("What Is to Be Done?" 1902 and "The State and Revolution" 1917), "Western Marxism" in Lukács, Gramsci and the Frankfurt School, sociology through the concept of class, and economic history. In the Arab world, Marxist readings of the heritage of thought and history appeared, among them Husayn Muruwwa's book "Materialist Tendencies in Arab-Islamic Philosophy," which tried to trace materialist currents in the heritage, and the studies of Mahdi Amil, which proposed the concept of the "colonial mode of production" to understand the social structure in Arab countries. The debate has continued in Arab thought over the suitability of the method for societies with religious and tribal structures.

## Limits of Marxism and Critique

- The labour theory of value has proved to be a matter of dispute within economics; Böhm-Bawerk criticized the "transformation" problem between values and prices in the third volume, and the marginalist school put forward an alternative theory based on utility.
- The economic interpretation of history is criticized for minimizing the role of ideas, culture and religion, and Engels in his late letters tried to soften the deterministic tendency.
- The expectations of the spread of revolutions in the most industrialized countries were not realized, and regimes claiming Marxism produced authoritarian results, which leads scholars to disagree over how far the text is responsible for them.
- Scholars differ in defining the limits of the classical, since the reading of alienation in the young Marx contrasts with the reading of economics in the mature Marx.
