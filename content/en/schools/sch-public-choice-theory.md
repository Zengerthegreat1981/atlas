# Public Choice Theory

**Public choice theory** is a field of political economy that applies the standard tools of microeconomic analysis, the rational individual seeking to maximize his or her own self-interest, to a domain that was traditionally assumed to be governed by a fundamentally different logic: politics, collective decision-making and bureaucracy. The field was given its landmark statement in the book *The Calculus of Consent* (1962) by **James Buchanan** and **Gordon Tullock**, which assumed that politicians, voters and bureaucrats, just like consumers and producers in the market, act from self-interest rather than from the disinterested public service traditionally assumed.

## Challenging the assumption of the benevolent planner

This analysis targeted a common implicit assumption in traditional welfare economics: that government, unlike the market, is a neutral apparatus that by its nature seeks to correct market failures and maximize general social welfare. Buchanan and Tullock argued that this assumption is methodologically inconsistent. If individuals pursue their self-interest in the market, there is no methodological reason to assume that they suddenly turn into purely disinterested beings when they occupy a political or bureaucratic office.

## Rent-seeking

Gordon Tullock's 1967 analysis of the welfare costs of tariffs, monopolies and theft laid the groundwork for the concept of **rent-seeking** (the term itself was coined by Anne Krueger in 1974): the resources that individuals and companies spend on lobbying and political pressure to win exclusive government privileges (licences, tariff protection, monopolistic contracts), instead of competing in the market through productive activity. From the standpoint of social welfare, this activity represents a net waste of resources. It creates no new value but redistributes existing value at an additional social cost.

## Olson: the logic of collective action

In *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965), Mancur Olson offered an explanation of why small groups with concentrated interests, such as a particular industry, are better able to organize politically than large, diffuse constituencies such as consumers and taxpayers, even when the collective interest of the latter may exceed that of the former. The reason is the **free-rider problem**, which discourages individuals from bearing the cost of organizing when they can benefit from the results of others' organizing without participating.

## Downs: the economics of democracy

In *An Economic Theory of Democracy* (1957), Anthony Downs applied a similar logic to the behaviour of voters and parties. He proposed that political parties in a competitive democratic system position themselves ideologically as producers competing for voters' support. An individual voter, knowing that his or her single vote will not practically change the outcome of an election, faces a weak rational incentive to gather enough information about complex political issues. Downs called this the voter's "rational ignorance".

## Government failure versus market failure

The school formulated the concept of **government failure** in parallel with the traditional concept of market failure in welfare economics. Just as markets may fail to achieve social efficiency because of monopolies or externalities, governments may also fail, because of structural internal incentives (re-election, the expansion of bureaucratic budgets, regulatory capture by interest groups) that steer public policy away from serving the general interest, even when it is presented as a remedy for market failure.

## Constitutional economics and the limits of power

Buchanan later developed what he called "constitutional economics": the view that constitutional rules, rather than individual policies, are the appropriate level at which to contain the self-interested tendencies of political actors. This is achieved through lasting institutional constraints, such as spending limits or balanced-budget rules, that are difficult for a transient political majority to revise easily. This work earned him the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1986.

## Criticism from several political perspectives

Some political scientists have criticized the school for applying reductive economic assumptions to a more complex political phenomenon that includes genuine ideological and value-based motives not fully reducible to material self-interest. Others, from the left, have regarded it as an ideological tool used to justify reducing the role of the state in general under the pretext of "government failure", without a comparably rigorous critical analysis of market failure.

## The Virginia school and its institutions

The field was institutionally centred on what became known as the "Virginia School", particularly the research centre associated with Buchanan and Tullock, which began at the University of Virginia, moved to Virginia Polytechnic Institute in 1969 and then to George Mason University in 1983. There, new analytical tools were developed, including applied game theory on voting behaviour and models of competitive bidding for rents. These tools became a standard part of political economy teaching on the behaviour of public institutions.

## Practical influence on policy design

The school's influence extended beyond academic circles into arguments about institutional design. Many American states already had balanced-budget rules, some dating from the nineteenth century. Public-choice writing later supplied a theoretical case for keeping such rules hard to amend, and for narrowing licences and trade barriers that create rents. William Niskanen's *Bureaucracy and Representative Government* (1971) added a model in which bureaus seek larger budgets. How far these ideas changed policy, as distinct from how they were used in policy argument, remains disputed.
