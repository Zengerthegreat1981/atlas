# Islamic Ethics and Political Thought

An umbrella that brings together the ethical and political theories that Islamic thought developed on the nature of legitimate authority, the foundations of government and the moral objectives (maqasid) of the Sharia, from the early Qur'anic foundation to the classical juristic theories of the imamate and the caliphate.

## Intellectual Foundations

- **Qur'anic ethics**: The Qur'an presents a central value system (justice, trustworthiness, mercy and excellence/benevolence, *ihsan*) that is treated as a normative basis for both individual and political action. It forms the first textual reference on which later, more detailed juristic and theological theories were built.
- **Al-Mawardi's *Ahkam al-Sultaniyya***: The best-known classical theorization of the foundations of government, by the jurist Abu al-Hasan al-Mawardi (974–1058 CE) in his book *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* (The Ordinances of Government). He held that the imamate, the supreme political authority, exists to preserve religion and to govern the world by means of it, and that the legitimacy of the ruler is conditional on preserving justice and attending to the welfare of the subjects. He set out the qualifications of the imam and of the electors (*ahl al-ikhtiyar*) who choose him, and the duties that office entails.
- **The concept of shura**: The principle of collective consultation in public decisions, derived from a direct Qur'anic text (Surat al-Shura), has become the focus of a modern debate about its relationship to democracy and contemporary political participation. Some see it as a consensual basis for representative government; others see it as a non-binding consultation that the ruler may simply take or leave.
- **The Sharia as a moral rather than only a punitive system**: A modern interpretive current reads the Sharia at its core as a purposive system aimed at realizing universal moral objectives (preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny and property), rather than a mere code of penalties. This opens the way to a contemporary discussion of human rights and their place within this framework.

## Philosophical Ethics and the Refinement of Character

Ethics was not confined to jurisprudence and theology. Miskawayh (d. 421 AH/1030 CE) wrote *Tahdhib al-akhlaq wa-tathir al-a'raq* (The Refinement of Character and the Purification of Dispositions), combining Platonic psychology, the idea of virtue as a mean between two vices in the manner of Aristotle, and a division of the soul into three powers: rational, irascible and appetitive. Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH/1111 CE) formulated a devotional ethics in *Ihya' 'ulum al-din*, and to him is attributed *al-Tibr al-masbuk fi nasihat al-muluk* (The Cast Ingots, Counsel for Kings; its authorship is debated) on the ethics and statecraft of the ruler. In al-Tusi's *Akhlaq-i Nasiri* (d. 672 AH/1274 CE), ethics, household management (*tadbir*) and politics became three divisions of practical wisdom.

## Political Governance and Historical Experience

Ibn al-Muqaffa' wrote *Risala fi al-Sahaba* (Epistle on the Caliph's Entourage), and the scribes formulated the courtly literature of advice to princes (*al-adab al-sultani*). Then al-Mawardi wrote *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* in Abbasid Baghdad, where the authority of the caliph had weakened before the Buyids and the Seljuks. His book codified a reality: an imamate established by choice (*ikhtiyar*) or by designation (*ahd*), a vizierate of delegation and execution, and an emirate of seizure (*imarat al-istila'*) that maintains order. The work shows that political jurisprudence often sought to justify the actual state of affairs as much as to constrain it. Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728 AH/1328 CE), in *al-Siyasa al-shar'iyya fi islah al-ra'i wa-l-ra'iyya* (Governance According to the Sharia, on Reforming the Shepherd and the Flock), joined the holding of authority to justice and trustworthiness. Ibn Khaldun (d. 808 AH/1406 CE), in the *Muqaddima*, linked the rise of states to *'asabiyya* (group solidarity) and to the cycle of civilization (*dawra hadariyya*). This diversity shows that Islamic political thought was not a single theory.

## Philosophers and the Imamate

Al-Farabi (d. 950 CE), in *Mabadi' Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila* (The Principles of the Views of the Citizens of the Virtuous City), treated the qualities of the First Ruler (*al-ra'is al-awwal*), requiring in him perfection of intellect and character, and making human association a necessity for perfection. He made the ignorant, the wicked and the misguided cities the opposites of the virtuous city. Abu al-Hasan al-Amiri (d. 381 AH/992 CE), in *al-Sa'ada wa-l-is'ad* (Happiness and Making Happy), approached the relation between religion and politics, and this line continued with Ibn Bajja (d. 533 AH/1138 CE) in *Tadbir al-mutawahhid* (The Governance of the Solitary), who held that the philosopher might save himself in a corrupt society. This explains why the theory of the imamate differed between the jurists, who required Qurashi descent, knowledge and justice, and the philosophical model that made leadership depend on competence.

## The Dispute over the Imamate

The dispute over the imamate was the first great disagreement. The Imami Shi'a regarded it as one of the foundations of religion and as a designation (*nass*) to 'Ali and his descendants. The Sunnis regarded it as a matter of jurisprudence, contracted by allegiance (*bay'a*) and consultation (*shura*). The Kharijites held that any just Muslim might assume it. Al-Ash'ari recorded these views in *Maqalat al-Islamiyyin* (The Doctrines of the Muslims).

## Limitations

- Reconciling the classical concept of shura with modern representative democracy remains a sharp and unresolved disagreement, both juristic and political, between an expansive reading and a restrictive one.
- This school is an umbrella that gathers many subfields, from the early Qur'anic foundation to modern political jurisprudence. It is therefore not a single historically homogeneous intellectual school.
