# Islamic Ethics and Political Thought

An umbrella that brings together the ethical and political theories that Islamic thought developed on the nature of legitimate authority, the foundations of government and the moral objectives (maqasid) of the Sharia, from the early Qur'anic foundation to the classical juristic theories of the imamate and the caliphate.

## Intellectual Foundations

- **Qur'anic ethics**: The Qur'an presents a central value system (justice, trustworthiness, mercy and excellence/benevolence, *ihsan*) that is treated as a normative basis for both individual and political action. It forms the first textual reference on which later, more detailed juristic and theological theories were built.
- **Al-Mawardi's *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya***: The best-known classical juristic account of government, by Abu al-Hasan al-Mawardi (364–450 AH / 974–1058 CE). He defined the imamate as instituted to succeed prophethood in guarding religion and governing the world (*hirasat al-din wa-siyasat al-dunya*). He set out the qualifications of the imam and of the electors (*ahl al-ikhtiyar*), and the duties of the office, including justice and care for the subjects. The same book also describes offices that recognize power already seized.
- **The concept of shura**: The principle of collective consultation in public decisions, derived from a direct Qur'anic text (Surat al-Shura), has become the focus of a modern debate about its relationship to democracy and contemporary political participation. Some see it as a consensual basis for representative government; others see it as a non-binding consultation that the ruler may simply take or leave.
- **The Sharia as a moral rather than only a punitive system**: Classical writers, notably al-Ghazali and later Abu Ishaq al-Shatibi (d. 790 AH/1388 CE), described necessary aims of the law (*maqasid*): preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny and property. A modern current uses that purposive reading in arguments about human rights. The step from the classical list to a rights doctrine is disputed.

## Philosophical Ethics and the Refinement of Character

Ethics was not confined to jurisprudence and theology. Miskawayh (d. 421 AH/1030 CE) wrote *Tahdhib al-akhlaq wa-tathir al-a'raq* (The Refinement of Character), combining a Platonic division of the soul into rational, irascible and appetitive powers with the Aristotelian idea of virtue as a mean between two vices. Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH/1111 CE) set out a devotional ethics in *Ihya' 'ulum al-din*. A Persian mirror for princes, *Nasihat al-muluk*, circulated under his name. In the usual printed form the text falls into two parts: the shorter first part is widely accepted as his, and Patricia Crone argued that the longer second part is not. The Arabic *al-Tibr al-masbuk fi nasihat al-muluk* is a translation of the Persian. Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (d. 672 AH/1274 CE), in the Persian *Akhlaq-i Nasiri*, divided practical philosophy into ethics, household management (*tadbir al-manzil*) and politics.

## Political Governance and Historical Experience

Ibn al-Muqaffa' wrote *Risala fi al-Sahaba* (Epistle on the Caliph's Entourage), and the scribes formulated the courtly literature of advice to princes (*al-adab al-sultani*). Al-Mawardi wrote *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* in Abbasid Baghdad while Buyid amirs held effective power and the caliph's authority had narrowed. The Seljuks took Baghdad in 1055, three years before his death. The book sets out an imamate established by choice (*ikhtiyar*) or by designation (*ahd*), a vizierate of delegation and of execution, and an emirate of seizure (*imarat al-istila'*) that keeps order when power has already been taken. It seeks both to justify the state as it stood and to set conditions on it. Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728 AH/1328 CE), in *al-Siyasa al-shar'iyya fi islah al-ra'i wa-l-ra'iyya* (Governance According to the Sharia, on Reforming the Shepherd and the Flock), joined the holding of authority to justice and trustworthiness. Ibn Khaldun (d. 808 AH/1406 CE), in the *Muqaddima*, linked the rise and decline of dynasties to *'asabiyya* (group solidarity) and to a passage from hardship to luxury. This diversity shows that Islamic political thought was not a single theory.

## Philosophers and the Imamate

Al-Farabi (d. 950 CE), in *Mabadi' Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila* (The Principles of the Views of the Citizens of the Virtuous City), treated the qualities of the First Ruler (*al-ra'is al-awwal*), requiring in him perfection of intellect and character, and making human association a necessity for perfection. He made the ignorant, the wicked and the misguided cities the opposites of the virtuous city. Abu al-Hasan al-Amiri (d. 381 AH/992 CE), in *al-Sa'ada wa-l-is'ad* (Happiness and Making Happy), approached the relation between religion and politics, and this line continued with Ibn Bajja (d. 533 AH/1139 CE) in *Tadbir al-mutawahhid* (The Governance of the Solitary), who held that the philosopher might preserve himself in a corrupt society. The jurists' theory of the imamate commonly required Qurashi descent, knowledge and justice, among other conditions. The philosophical model made leadership depend on intellectual and moral competence. The two lists are not the same project.

## The Dispute over the Imamate

The dispute over the imamate was the earliest major disagreement in the community. The Imami Shi'a regarded it as one of the foundations of religion and as a designation (*nass*) to 'Ali and his descendants. The Sunnis regarded it as a matter of jurisprudence, contracted by allegiance (*bay'a*) and consultation (*shura*). The Kharijites held that any just Muslim might assume it. Al-Ash'ari recorded these views in *Maqalat al-Islamiyyin* (The Doctrines of the Muslims).

## Limitations

- Reconciling the classical concept of shura with modern representative democracy remains a sharp and unresolved disagreement, both juristic and political, between an expansive reading and a restrictive one.
- This school is an umbrella that gathers many subfields, from the early Qur'anic foundation to modern political jurisprudence. It is therefore not a single historically homogeneous intellectual school.
