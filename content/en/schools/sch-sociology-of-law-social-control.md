# Sociology of Law & Social Control

Donald Black's abstract theory of the "quantity of law" actually applied to a given dispute, which he explains by purely social-structural variables rather than by the content of the written law itself.

## The Central Idea

In *The Behavior of Law* (1976), Black argued that the actual "quantity" of law applied to any social dispute (the severity of punishment, or the likelihood of prosecution in the first place) is determined not only by the content of the abstract written law, but by structural characteristics of the disputing parties themselves: the social distance between them (strangers or kin), the difference in social status (class, race), and the degree of their integration into traditional social networks. He formulated general empirical propositions drawn from cross-cultural comparative studies. Law is applied more strictly between strangers than between close relatives (who prefer to resolve disputes outside the court), and less strictly against those of high status (who receive statistically more lenient legal treatment). This is a controversial thesis suggesting that "law" is far less neutral and general than official legal discourse itself assumes, and that actual justice is distributed in a systematically socially unequal way.

## Historical Context

Donald Black developed his thesis within a broader project of founding the sociology of law as a rigorous, explanatory, empirical field. His approach drew on comparative legal anthropology (for example E. Adamson Hoebel's *The Law of Primitive Man*, 1954, and Bronisław Malinowski's *Crime and Custom in Savage Society*, 1926), and its taste for abstract structural forms is often compared with Georg Simmel's formal sociology. Black sought to separate the sociological study of law from normative legal philosophy (what law ought to be) in favor of a purely descriptive and predictive account of how law behaves, regardless of any moral judgment on the justice of that behavior.

## Main Ideas and Method

In *The Behavior of Law* (1976), Black treated law as "governmental social control" and presented a series of formal propositions linking its quantity to social-structural variables such as stratification, morphology (the distribution of people in social space), culture, organization and other forms of social control. Among the best known: law varies with relational distance (it is used less between intimates, who tend to handle conflict by other means, and more between strangers), and law varies with vertical status, so that it is generally applied more strongly downward (against lower-status parties) than upward (against higher-status parties). Black drew on comparative data from many societies and periods, from tribal societies to contemporary American courts, to illustrate these propositions. He stressed that he did not seek to explain law by its declared moral or political content but by its location and direction in social space.

## Major Works and Representatives

Black's name became associated with a number of students and colleagues who applied his formal method to the study of law, crime and conflict; this work is sometimes called "pure sociology" and is distinguished from interpretive or critical approaches such as Marxist sociology of law or approaches focused on actors' subjective meanings. Donald Black (born 1941), long a professor of sociology at the University of Virginia, edited the two-volume *Toward a General Theory of Social Control* (1984), which extended the framework to informal social control (such as gossip, ostracism and vigilante violence) alongside formal law, treating social control as a whole rather than law alone.

## Impact and Critique

This work offered a bold, predictive, quantitative framework for the sociological study of law. It influenced the sociology of law and comparative criminal justice, and it provided analytical tools later used in studying racial and class disparities in the actual application of criminal law. It was criticized by jurists and researchers who considered its theoretical reductionism excessive, ignoring individual agency and the subjective legal consciousness of the disputing parties in favor of abstract structural variables alone. Some of its formal "propositions" were also regarded as open to many exceptions that weaken their claimed "general law" character. Despite this criticism, Black's work remained an original methodological contribution to developing an empirical, comparative sociology of law that goes beyond the limits of traditional descriptive legal study. Empirical tests of his propositions have produced mixed results, supporting some and casting doubt on the generality of others.
