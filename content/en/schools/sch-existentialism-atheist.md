# Atheist Existentialism

A current within existentialism that rejects the idea of a metaphysical God and, with it, the idea of an "objective meaning" given from outside. This current takes Sartre's slogans seriously: **man is condemned to be free**, and there is no refuge except in action. It differs from absurdism (Camus) in holding that **freedom is possible** despite the absence of God and that meaning is made through commitment, whereas absurdism holds that the struggle with meaninglessness ends in "revolt" without any promise of victory.

## Historical Course

Its roots go back to Nietzsche and his proclamation of the "death of God" in *The Gay Science* (1882), and to Dostoevsky in *Notes from Underground* (1864), where the narrator rebels against rationality. But the systematic formulation came in occupied Paris and after the Liberation: Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* appeared in 1943, and he delivered his lecture "Existentialism Is a Humanism" in October 1945, whereupon existentialism became a cultural fashion; Sartre, Beauvoir, and Merleau-Ponty founded the journal *Les Temps modernes* in the same year. Heidegger rejected the label of existentialist and replied to Sartre in his "Letter on Humanism" (1947).

## Key Figures

- **Jean-Paul Sartre** (1905–1980): the movement's leading public figure. *Being and Nothingness* (1943), *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1946), *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960); he declined the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1964.
- **Simone de Beauvoir** (1908–1986): the ethics of ambiguity and the analysis of the oppression of women. *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947), *The Second Sex* (1949), which contains her phrase "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."
- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** (1908–1961): the phenomenology of the body in *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945); he drew away from Sartre in the 1950s.
- **Albert Camus** (1913–1960): *The Stranger* and *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), then *The Rebel* (1951); he broke with Sartre in 1952 after a controversy over *The Rebel* in *Les Temps modernes*, and he himself rejected the label of existentialist.

## Founding Principles

- **Nothingness is what makes consciousness possible**: for Sartre, consciousness is not a "thing" but a gap at the heart of being. He distinguishes being-in-itself (l'en-soi) of things from being-for-itself (le pour-soi) of consciousness, and this gap is what makes free action possible.
- **Unconditional freedom**: every decision, even the decision not to decide, is a responsible act, and hence human beings have no excuse.
- **Bad faith (mauvaise foi)**: self-deception by denying freedom, like the waiter who plays his role as if he were a "thing," or by denying facts; authenticity is its overcoming.
- **The look (le regard)**: in *Being and Nothingness*, a person becomes an object before the look of the other and feels shame; in the play *No Exit* (Huis clos, 1944) occurs the line "Hell is other people."
- **Commitment (engagement)**: in the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) Sartre tried to combine individual freedom with the Marxist analysis of material conditions and the collective.
- **Beauvoir's ethics of ambiguity**: freedom is realized only by willing the freedom of others, so for her ethics rests on mutual recognition.

## Sartre and Camus

The two were friends from 1943-44, when Camus's *The Stranger* had drawn Sartre's admiring review. The rupture came in 1952, after Francis Jeanson, writing in *Les Temps modernes*, reviewed *The Rebel* critically from a Marxist standpoint. Camus answered in an open letter addressed to the editor, and Sartre replied sharply; the exchange divided their Parisian readers. The disagreement concerned the status of history and violence: Camus refused to justify present killing by a promised future, while Sartre held that abstaining from revolutionary commitment was itself a political choice.

## Positions on Marxism and Politics

- **Sartre**: he drew close to the French Communist Party in the 1950s without joining it, then criticized the Soviet intervention in Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968), defended the Algerian liberation movement, and wrote the preface to Frantz Fanon's *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961).
- **Beauvoir**: she linked feminism to a structural analysis of oppression, and kept her independence from party discipline.
- **Merleau-Ponty**: in *Humanism and Terror* (1947) he discussed the logic of revolution and violence among the Communists, then moved away from Marxism in *Adventures of the Dialectic* (1955).
- **Camus**: he defended a revolt limited in its means that refuses killing in the name of history.

## Atheist Existentialism and Philosophical Anarchism

Atheist existentialism shares significant theoretical ground with philosophical anarchism, particularly in its radical rejection of external moral authority, sovereign hierarchy, and institutional coercion. Sartre’s ontological claim that the human being is "condemned to be free" locates moral responsibility entirely in the individual agent, denying the legitimacy of state dictates, naturalized social roles, or bourgeois legalism. While Sartre engaged closely with Marxism from the 1950s onward, he consistently rejected bureaucratic party authoritarianism and Soviet statism. In his later years (in interviews of the 1970s), Sartre described his political outlook in libertarian terms, stressing his mistrust of state power and hierarchy, though he never formulated a systematic anarchist theory. Camus's defense of limited rebellion against state violence and ideological terror is also often read as close to libertarian socialist traditions, but these affinities are interpretive and the thinkers themselves did not adopt the label of anarchism as a school.

## Arab Reception

Arab intellectuals became acquainted with Sartre early. The journal *al-Adab*, founded by Suhayl Idris in Beirut in 1953, was a platform for literary commitment inspired by *What Is Literature?*, and Idris contributed to translating Sartre into Arabic. Abdel Rahman Badawi wrote *Existential Time* (1943) on German existentialism, an early and influential Arabic work on the subject. The Arab debate over atheist existentialism came mostly from the angle of its relation to faith and national commitment.

## Limits and Criticism

- **The charge of excessive subjectivity**: Lukács, in *Existentialism or Marxism?* (1948), saw existentialism as an expression of the crisis of the bourgeoisie, and orthodox Marxists attacked it.
- **Practical contradiction**: Sartre was accused of vacillating positions toward Communist regimes, and hence of a gap between principle and position.
- **The criterion**: if meaning is made by choice, by what criterion is a wrong choice judged? Sartre replied that freedom itself is a universal value, and this did not convince many critics.
- **Analytic critique**: Carnap, in "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932), argued that passages in Heidegger's writing, such as "the Nothing nothings," were meaningless pseudo-statements; logical empiricists extended this suspicion to existentialist discourse generally.
