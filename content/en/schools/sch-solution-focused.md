# Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT)

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT) is a short-term therapeutic model that concentrates on building possible solutions rather than analyzing problems. It rests on a fundamental assumption: the client already has, or has within reach, the resources needed for a solution, and the therapist's role is not to diagnose or interpret but to guide the client toward exploring and using these resources.

## Intellectual and Historical Context

SFBT emerged in the late 1970s and 1980s at the Brief Family Therapy Center (BFTC), founded in 1978 in Milwaukee, Wisconsin. It was founded by **Steve de Shazer** and **Insoo Kim Berg**, who were influenced by several currents:

- **The Palo Alto Group**: the work of Gregory Bateson, John Weakland, Richard Fisch, and others, with their emphasis on brief therapy and on communication.
- **Milton Erickson**: his utilization approach, his focus on clients' resources and competence, and his use of future-oriented suggestion influenced the model, mediated through the Palo Alto group and Jay Haley.

De Shazer and Berg developed the model at the center, and de Shazer published his founding book *Patterns of Brief Family Therapy* (1982) and then *Keys to Solution in Brief Therapy* (1985). After de Shazer's death in 2005, colleagues completed *More Than Miracles* (2007); Berg died in 2007.

## The School's Core Contributions

SFBT's contributions go beyond techniques to touch the entire philosophy of therapy:

1. **Focus on the solution, not the problem**: the founding principle. The therapist does not ask "What is the problem?" but "What do you want to be different?" and "How will you know that things have improved?"

2. **Brevity of therapy**: as its name says. The number of sessions is not fixed in advance; SFBT is typically completed in a small number of sessions, and ending when the client's goals are met is part of the approach.

3. **The client as expert on their own life**: the therapist trusts that the client knows what they need, and helps them discover it.

4. **Dismantling pathologizing language**: using the client's language ("problem behavior" rather than "illness") to avoid the stigma of labels.

5. **Framing exceptions**: looking for the times when the problem did not occur, or occurred to a lesser degree, and exploring what was different in them.

## Core Concepts and Techniques

### The Miracle Question

The most famous question in SFBT: "If a miracle happened tonight and the problem you came for disappeared completely, but you were not awake, how would you know tomorrow morning that the miracle had happened? What would be different?" It aims to move the client from focusing on pain toward imagining a different future.

### Scaling Questions

"On a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 means the worst possible and 10 means your goal, where are you now?" The therapist uses these questions to measure progress and to identify small achievable steps.

### Exception Seeking

Searching the client's life for moments when the problem did not occur. "Are there times when the problem was less severe? What were you doing at those times?"

### The Formula First Session Task

Between the first and second sessions the client is asked to notice what happens in their life that they want to continue, to direct attention toward what is already working.

### Compliments and Reinforcement

At the end of each session, the therapist summarizes the strengths and abilities the client has shown. This constructive reinforcement is one of the pillars of the model.

## Its Position Within the Therapeutic Field

SFBT historically belongs to the family of brief family therapy, but it extends to other fields:

- **Education**: application in schools under the name "solution-focused counseling."
- **Social services**: use in case management and in crisis situations.
- **Organizations**: coaching and consulting based on SFBT principles (for example Berg and Szabo, 2005).

SFBT differs from cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) in not targeting distorted thoughts and in building on solutions and exceptions rather than analyzing the problem, and it differs from psychodynamic therapy in not seeking the causes of problems.

## Evidence and Effectiveness

The evidence base for SFBT consists mainly of small and moderate-sized studies. A review of controlled outcome studies (Gingerich & Eisengart, 2000) found the evidence promising but limited, and a later meta-analysis (Kim, 2008) found small positive effects on internalizing problems such as depression and anxiety, with weaker evidence for externalizing and family problems. The atlas therefore rates the evidence at the "probably efficacious" level. SFBT is used in school, child and family, and community settings, but evidence for severe disorders is limited.

## Critique and Limits

- **Limitation in complex cases**: the effectiveness of SFBT in personality disorders and severe psychological disorders is less clear.
- **Potential superficiality**: the focus on the solution may ignore deep content or unresolved conflicts.
- **Brevity and resources**: the brevity can be driven by service constraints rather than by clinical need.
- **Selective application**: many practitioners take SFBT techniques (the miracle question, compliments) without committing to the underlying constructivist philosophy, which has produced "hollowed-out" versions.

## Legacy and Influence

De Shazer and Berg left a deep legacy in normalizing the idea of "brief therapy" as an authentic approach rather than an economic expedient. Berg and de Shazer established the Brief Family Therapy Center (BFTC) in Milwaukee in 1978, and SFBT has since spread internationally. The Solution Focused Brief Therapy Association (SFBTA) is a professional organization for practitioners and researchers.
