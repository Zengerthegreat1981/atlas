# Common Law Tradition

## Historical Context

Before discussing the origins of English common law, it is worth noting that Islamic jurisprudence developed from the second century AH a parallel judicial tradition based on the accumulation of judicial reasoning and the rulings of judges within the framework of the principles of jurisprudence (*usul al-fiqh*), known as the jurisprudence of "adjudication and fatwa." Judges such as Abu Yusuf in Baghdad, built their rulings on established juristic precedents while taking local custom into account, without this becoming a formally binding system of precedent as in English law. Common law in its technical sense arose in England after the Norman Conquest of 1066, when the kings sought to unify the scattered judicial customs through the travelling royal courts. A "general" legal system gradually took shape, applying to the entire Kingdom of England and distinct from the codified law (civil law) that prevailed on the European continent under the influence of codified Roman law.

## Central Ideas

The common law tradition rests on the principle of binding judicial precedent (*stare decisis*), which requires lower courts to follow the rulings that higher courts have established in similar cases, so that the law accumulates through the body of judicial precedents rather than from pre-written statutory texts. Among its central ideas is also the principle of the "rule of law," elaborated by the jurist Albert Venn Dicey, which holds that both ruler and ruled are subject to a single law applied by independent courts. The tradition is also distinguished by its reliance on a jury system, and by the historical separation between the courts of "common law" and the courts of "equity," which arose to correct the rigidity of common law in cases where a litigant could find no equitable remedy.

## Leading Thinkers

Sir Edward Coke, a leading judge and jurist of the early seventeenth century, defended the independence of the common law courts against royal claims. Sir William Blackstone, in his *Commentaries on the Laws of England*, provided an influential comprehensive and systematic account of English law (1765–1769). In the nineteenth century, John Austin developed the early legal positivist theory that interpreted law as a command issued by a supreme sovereign authority, while in America Oliver Wendell Holmes approached law from a realist perspective linking it to the prediction of courts' behavior. In the twentieth century, H.L.A. Hart, in *The Concept of Law*, presented a developed positivist theory distinguishing between primary and secondary rules, which became a fundamental reference in Anglo-American legal philosophy.

## Impact and Application

Common law spread through the former British colonies to include the United States, Canada, Australia, India and many countries of Africa and Asia, making it one of the most widely adopted legal systems in the world today, alongside the civil law tradition and Islamic jurisprudence. Its influence is visible in the adoption by many countries with an Islamic background of a mixture of common law and Sharia rules in their judicial systems, as is the case in Pakistan, Nigeria and Malaysia, which has opened a continuing jurisprudential and legal debate about how to reconcile the flexibility of judicial precedent with the stability of definitive Sharia rulings. The debate also continues between proponents of legal positivism, who separate law from morality, and proponents of natural law, who link the legitimacy of law to its conformity with higher moral principles, a debate that intersects considerably with the Islamic jurisprudential discussion of the relationship between revelation (*shar'*) and reason.

An important internal debate in this tradition is the historical dispute between the courts of common law and the courts of equity, which in England ended with the Judicature Acts of 1873 and 1875, which merged the two jurisdictions into a single judicial structure. The twentieth century also saw the rise of American Legal Realism, whose proponents, among them Karl Llewellyn, questioned the capacity of judicial precedents alone to explain judges' behavior and emphasized the influence of social and political factors on the formulation of rulings, which paved the way for the later emergence of the Critical Legal Studies movement. The distinction between common law and the civil law tradition remains one of the central themes of comparative law: the former relies on extracting the rule from accumulated facts, while the latter proceeds from comprehensive legal codes from which the ruling is deduced. This methodological contrast is somewhat reminiscent of the distinction in the history of Islamic jurisprudence between the approach of the People of Hadith and the approach of the People of Opinion: the People of Hadith in the Hijaz relied on extracting individual texts directly, while the People of Opinion in Iraq expanded in analogy and in deriving general principles from the objectives of the texts.

This methodological distinction has led to a modern comparative debate about which of the two systems is better able to keep pace with rapid social change. Proponents of common law argue that the flexibility of judicial precedent allows judges to adapt the rule to new facts without waiting for legislative intervention, while proponents of the civil law tradition argue that comprehensive codification provides greater legal certainty and limits arbitrary judicial discretion. Several countries in Asia and Africa, after their independence from British colonialism, adopted the common law system with amendments that take local custom and Sharia rulings into account, giving rise to hybrid systems sometimes called "mixed legal systems," which bear witness to the ability of the jurisprudential and common law traditions to coexist within a single institutional framework.

## Equity, Jury and Precedent

Equity began with petitions to the king and his chancellor where common law procedure gave no adequate remedy. The Court of Chancery developed the trust, the injunction and specific performance. Conflict with the common law judges came to a head around the *Earl of Oxford's Case* (1615), after which James I upheld Chancery's power to restrain unconscionable use of common law judgments. Although later legislation fused the courts, equitable doctrines and discretionary remedies remain distinct.

The jury grew from sworn local inquiry. The Assize of Clarendon (1166) used juries to present suspected offenders. After the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) withdrew clerical participation in ordeals, trial juries came to decide guilt. *Bushell's Case* (1670) established that jurors could not be punished for their verdict. Civil juries have since largely disappeared in England but remain important in the United States.

Precedent hardened gradually. Medieval Year Books recorded argument without a rule of strict binding force, and reports by named reporters varied in quality. Reliable reporting and a settled court hierarchy in the nineteenth century supported strict *stare decisis*. The House of Lords held itself bound by its own decisions in 1898 and announced in a 1966 Practice Statement that it could depart from them; the Court of Appeal's limited exceptions were set out in *Young v Bristol Aeroplane* (1944).
