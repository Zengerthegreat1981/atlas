# Environmental Anthropology

Environmental anthropology is the study of the relationships between human societies and their environments. It asks how people understand, use, and transform the natural world — through farming, hunting, fishing, herding, mining, and conservation — and how environments, in turn, shape social life, politics, and belief. It is a field that spans the ecological, the economic, and the cultural, and that has become one of anthropology's most urgent areas as climate change, deforestation, and biodiversity loss reshape the planet.

## History and Origins

The field's roots lie in cultural ecology, developed by Julian Steward in the 1950s. Steward's Theory of Culture Change: The Methodology of Multilinear Evolution (1955) argued that societies adapt to their environments through a "cultural core" of subsistence practices and social arrangements, and that similar environments could produce similar adaptations in different parts of the world. This ecological approach was extended by Roy Rappaport, whose Pigs for the Ancestors: Ritual in the Ecology of a New Guinea People (1968) showed how a ritual cycle of pig feasts regulated warfare, population, and land use among the Tsembaga Maring of Papua New Guinea — demonstrating that religion could have ecological functions.

In the 1960s and 1970s, the field engaged with debates about population, resources, and the commons. Garrett Hardin's essay "The Tragedy of the Commons" (1968) argued that shared resources would inevitably be overexploited, while anthropologists such as Emilio Moran, in Human Adaptability (1979), showed that local communities often manage common resources sustainably through customary rules. Marvin Harris's cultural materialism, in books such as Cows, Pigs, Wars, and Witches (1974), offered materialist explanations for religious practices — famously arguing that the Hindu taboo on eating cattle made ecological and economic sense in India.

From the 1980s, political ecology reshaped the field. Associated with Eric Wolf and later with scholars such as Piers Blaikie and Harold Brookfield, political ecology argued that environmental problems could not be understood without attention to power, markets, colonialism, and the state. Land degradation, deforestation, and water scarcity, in this view, are political outcomes as much as ecological ones. At the same time, the rise of environmentalism and the conservation movement gave anthropologists a new role: studying protected areas, indigenous land rights, and the conflicts between conservation and local livelihoods.

## Key Figures and Works

Roy Rappaport remains a foundational figure, and his later work, Ecology, Meaning, and Religion (1979), connected ecological anthropology to the study of ritual and meaning. Emilio Moran's work in Amazonia documented how farmers, ranchers, and indigenous peoples use and transform the rainforest, and his Through Amazonian Eyes (1993) gave voice to local understandings of the forest. Kay Milton's Environmentalism and Cultural Theory (1996) explored how environmental concern itself is culturally constructed, while Philippe Descola's Beyond Nature and Culture (2005 in French; English translation 2013) argued that the Western distinction between "nature" and "culture" is itself a cultural product, and that many societies — notably Amazonian peoples — understand humans and nonhumans as sharing a common social world.

The political ecology tradition is represented by Paul Robbins's Political Ecology: A Critical Introduction (2004), which became the standard textbook, and by the work of James Fairhead and Melissa Leach, whose Misreading the African Landscape (1996) showed that supposedly degraded landscapes in West Africa were in fact created by local farming practices. The anthropology of climate change emerged as a major strand with Anthropology and Climate Change: From Encounters to Actions (2009), edited by Susan Crate and Mark Nuttall, which documented how Arctic, Pacific, and mountain communities experience and respond to a warming world.

## Method

Environmental anthropology uses long-term ethnography in places where people live with and from their environments — villages, forests, fisheries, pastoral camps, and cities — combined with ecological and historical methods. Researchers map land use, document farming and hunting practices, record local ecological knowledge, and trace how households, communities, and states manage resources. Increasingly they combine ethnography with satellite imagery, GIS mapping, and participatory research in which local people document their own territories.

The field's distinctive contribution is its attention to local knowledge and its refusal to separate the natural from the social. Anthropologists document how indigenous peoples classify plants and animals, predict weather, and manage forests — knowledge that often proves more accurate and sustainable than official science. This work has supported indigenous land claims and community-based conservation, and has challenged the idea that protected areas must exclude people.

## Influence and Contemporary Directions

Environmental anthropology has shaped conservation policy, development practice, and climate politics. Its ethnographies of protected areas showed that fortress conservation often displaces local people and fails to protect biodiversity, supporting a shift toward community-based conservation. Its studies of climate change have given voice to the communities most affected and least responsible for global warming, from Inuit hunters in the Arctic to Pacific Islanders facing sea-level rise. The field also informs debates about environmental justice, showing how pollution, extraction, and land grabs fall disproportionately on poor and marginalised peoples.

Contemporary directions include the anthropology of the Anthropocene — the proposed geological epoch defined by human impact on the planet; multispecies ethnography, which studies relationships between humans, animals, plants, and microbes; research on water, energy, and extractive industries; and the study of environmental movements and climate activism. The field also engages with the "ontological turn", asking how different peoples understand the nature of the world itself. What unites these directions is the conviction that environmental problems are human problems, and that understanding them requires understanding the cultures that create them.
