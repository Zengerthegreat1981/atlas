# Modern Legal Positivism (Kelsen, Hart)

A current in the philosophy of law whose adherents share one central thesis despite differing formulations: **the validity of a legal rule does not depend on its conformity with morality** (the Separation Thesis). In this way, positivists are distinguished from natural law traditions that treat moral obligation as a condition for a rule to count as law at all.

## Usul al-Fiqh and the question of the source of obligation: a necessary comparison

Islamic legal theory (usul al-fiqh) raises from the outset a parallel question, with an inverted answer: are good and evil established by reason independently of revelation (as the Mu'tazila held), or does the divine ruling alone constitute the source of moral and legal obligation, so that there is no good or evil except through the Law (the dominant Ash'ari position, including **al-Ghazali** in *al-Mustasfa*)? This theological debate makes most Sunni jurisprudence closer to a model that ties the legitimacy of a ruling to its divine source rather than to any independence of reason from it. In a purely formal respect, this corresponds to the positivist separation thesis (tying validity to a specific formal source rather than to independent moral content), with a crucial difference: for positivists the source is human and social (the rule of recognition or the basic rule), whereas for the jurists the source is transcendent and divine. As for **al-Shatibi**, he offered a different synthesis through the theory of the objectives of the Law (maqasid): particular legal rulings are derived within general aims (the maqasid) whose wisdom reason can grasp, even though the source of their obligation is the Law itself. He thus approaches a middle position between pure legal voluntarism and pure natural rationalism.

This trunk of the Atlas includes two principal modern formulations that developed in near independence: **Hans Kelsen's pure theory of law** in Austria, and **H.L.A. Hart's theory of social rules** in Britain. It also includes **Lon Fuller's** critique of both, from a position that rehabilitates a structural (not purely moral) link between law and morality.

## Why "modern" rather than "classical"?

Classical positivism (Bentham, Austin, the nineteenth century) grounded legal obligation in the **command backed by sanction** issued by a political sovereign. Modern positivism, the subject of this file, moved beyond the model of the sovereign command to more abstract models: **a hierarchy of rules deriving from a basic norm** in Kelsen, and **a system of primary and secondary rules supported by a rule of recognition** in Hart. This historical distinction is what explains devoting this file to a separate node from a possible future entry on Bentham and Austin.

## Hart and Kelsen: detailing the two theses

Kelsen built his pure theory of law (Reine Rechtslehre) on a strictly hierarchical conception: every legal rule derives its validity from a higher rule, up to a "basic norm" (Grundnorm) that is methodologically presupposed rather than empirically given, whose sole function is to explain why we regard the original constitution as binding in the first place. Kelsen aimed through this to purge the science of law of any political, moral, or social content, achieving a rigorous methodological objectivity that mirrors the standing of the natural sciences. Hart offered a less abstract alternative in *The Concept of Law* (1961): a legal system is a union of **primary rules** (which impose obligations directly on individuals) and **secondary rules** (which specify how primary rules are made, modified, and applied). The most important secondary rule is the **rule of recognition**, which sets the criterion of legal validity within a system through the actual social practice of officials, rather than as a methodological assumption as in Kelsen. Fuller criticized both positions on the grounds that law has an **"internal morality"**: formal conditions such as clarity, generality, non-contradiction, and the possibility of compliance. A system that neglects these cannot remain "law" in practice, however much its proponents claim its formal validity. This critique reopens the door to a structural link between the form of law and its morality, without a full return to traditional natural law.

## Inclusive and exclusive positivism: the Raz–Coleman dispute

Hart's own heirs later divided over a fine point in the theory of the rule of recognition: may the criteria of legal validity include moral considerations (for example, a constitution declaring that any law contrary to "justice" is void)? **Jules Coleman** adopted the position of "Inclusive Positivism", which permits this, while **Joseph Raz** held to "Exclusive Positivism", which rejects it. Raz argued that introducing morality, even in a procedural form, undermines law's basic practical function: the ability to identify and apply it without resorting to an open moral debate in every case. In a related debate, a famous exchange took place between **Hart** and **Patrick Devlin** on whether the state may criminalize conduct that is harmless to anyone other than the actor (such as homosexual conduct between consenting adults, the issue addressed by the Wolfenden Report of 1957) on the sole ground of protecting "public morality". Devlin defended society's right to protect its moral cohesion, while Hart upheld the principle of liberty in the tradition of Mill: criminalization is justified only to prevent harm to others.
