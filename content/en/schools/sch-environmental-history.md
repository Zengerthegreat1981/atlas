# Environmental History (Alfred Crosby, Donald Worster)

A historical field that treats nature (epidemics, species, climate, land) as an active participant in human history, not merely its silent background.

## Alfred Crosby and the Columbian Exchange

In *The Columbian Exchange: Biological and Cultural Consequences of 1492* (1972), the historian **Alfred Crosby** reinterprets European colonization of the Americas through a biological lens: the transmission of diseases (smallpox, measles) from Europe to the Americas destroyed the overwhelming majority of the indigenous population (estimates reach 90% in some regions) before any decisive military confrontation. This biological rather than political factor was the most decisive in the success of European colonization. He later developed the concept of "ecological imperialism" (*Ecological Imperialism*, 1986) to describe how imported European plants and animals reshaped entire environments (Australia, the Americas) in favor of European settlement.

## Donald Worster and Environmental History as Political Critique

In *Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s* (1979), the historian **Donald Worster** develops an approach that links the environmental disaster (the dust storms of the Great Plains) to a specific exploitative capitalist economic pattern of land use, rather than to purely "natural" climatic factors. This is environmental history with an explicit critical dimension, which assigns human economic decisions responsibility for the "natural" catastrophe.

## William Cronon and the Transformation of the Land in New England

The historian William Cronon, in *Changes in the Land* (1983), offers another influential model of the field. He compares the systems of land use among the indigenous peoples of New England (who practiced mobile agriculture and seasonal hunting that preserved relative ecological diversity) with those of the European settlers (who introduced the concept of fixed private ownership of land and intensive commercial agriculture). He shows how this shift in the system of ownership reshaped the entire ecosystem, beyond the mere biological exchange described by Crosby.

## The Institutional Establishment of the Field

Environmental history gained recognition as a formal academic specialty with the founding of the American Society for Environmental History in 1977, which began publishing a specialized journal. This provided an institutional framework for methodical research accumulation that went beyond individual pioneering works to become a full academic field, now including specialized branches such as the history of climate, the history of epidemics, the history of national parks and conservation.

## Integrating Paleoclimate History

The field has gradually benefited from the development of paleoclimatology and techniques of analyzing tree rings and polar ice cores. This has enabled historians to link specific historical events (such as famines or the collapse of civilizations) to scientifically documented climate fluctuations with growing precision, rather than relying only on the descriptive accounts of weather and crops found in written sources.

## The Debate on Human-Centeredness

A methodological debate within the field concerns the degree of "anthropocentrism" acceptable in environmental historical writing. One current tends to focus on how nature affects human destinies (as in Crosby), while another, closer to "deep ecology," calls for treating ecosystems and non-human beings as having historical value in themselves, independent of their use or harm to humans. This philosophical disagreement directly shapes the choice of research questions and the angle of the narrative.

## Applied Example: The Black Death

The Black Death in fourteenth-century Europe is cited as a classic example of this field's approach. Environmental historians explained the spread of the epidemic and its enormous demographic impact (the death of roughly a third of Europe's population) through combined environmental factors: climatic changes that preceded the epidemic and weakened harvests, and transcontinental trade networks along the silk routes that carried the causative bacteria. They highlight how a biological-environmental factor can reshape the social and economic structure of an entire continent, outweighing the impact of any individual political decision.

## Relation to Braudel's Longue Durée

Environmental history borrows from Fernand Braudel his sensitivity to the nearly fixed geographical structures that move history more slowly than the pace of transient political events. But Braudel's analysis treats geography as a relatively fixed background within which human action moves, whereas environmental history grants nature itself an independent historical agency: a virus that mutates, an invasive species that spreads, a climate that fluctuates, as forces that shape the human course rather than mere stable stage.

## Expansion of the Field toward the History of the Anthropocene

With the rising scientific debate about the concept of the "Anthropocene" (the geological epoch in which human activity itself becomes a geological force affecting the Earth system), environmental history has expanded to incorporate this new time frame. Some historians have begun tracing the origins of this planetary human influence back to the Industrial Revolution, or even to the beginnings of agriculture itself, contributing to a multidisciplinary debate that brings history together with the earth and climate sciences.
