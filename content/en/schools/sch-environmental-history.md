# Environmental History (Alfred Crosby, Donald Worster)

Environmental history is an interdisciplinary field of historical inquiry that investigates the mutual, reciprocal relationships between human societies and the natural environment over time. Rather than treating nature as a passive, static backdrop against which human politics and cultural dramas unfold, environmental history recognizes ecological factors—including climate fluctuations, soil chemistry, pathogen vectors, vegetation regimes, and non-human animal populations—as dynamic historical agents that actively condition and are transformed by human action.

## Alfred Crosby and the Columbian Exchange

A foundational landmark of modern environmental history was established by American historian Alfred W. Crosby (1931–2018) in his study, *The Columbian Exchange: Biological and Cultural Consequences of 1492* (1972). Crosby argued that the European colonization of the Americas after 1492 was fundamentally a biological and ecological event rather than merely a military or diplomatic conquest.

Crosby traced the transoceanic transfer of organisms:
- **Pathogens:** The inadvertent introduction of Old World infectious diseases (smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus) into immunological naive Indigenous populations resulted in catastrophic virgin soil epidemics, with some estimates of Native American population loss ranging as high as 80 to 90 percent in the following century or so (figures remain debated). Historians such as Crosby stressed that this demographic collapse weakened Indigenous resistance as much as European weaponry did.
- **Flora and Fauna:** The importation of European domestic animals (horses, cattle, pigs, sheep) and cultivated crops radically transformed American ecosystems. Simultaneously, American domesticates (potatoes, maize, cassava, sweet potatoes) were introduced to Europe, Asia, and Africa, fueling global demographic expansion.

In *Ecological Imperialism: The Biological Expansion of Europe, 900–1900* (1986), Crosby expanded this thesis to explain why European emigrants successfully founded "Neo-Europes" in temperate zones across North America, Australia, New Zealand, and the Argentine pampas, arguing that their primary advantage lay in a "portmanteau biota" of domesticated animals, weeds, and pathogens that overwhelmed native ecosystems.

## Donald Worster and Agro-Ecological Critique

Donald Worster (born 1941), a prominent American environmental historian, developed a materialist and institutional approach to environmental history that links ecological crises directly to economic structures and cultural ideologies. In *Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s* (1979), Worster analyzed the catastrophic drought and dust storms that devastated the American Southern Plains during the Great Depression.

Rejecting explanations that attributed the disaster purely to natural meteorological misfortune, Worster argued that the Dust Bowl was the outcome of capitalist market imperatives and aggressive mechanized agriculture. Federal land policies and grain speculation encouraged farmers to plow up millions of acres of fragile, drought-adapted native prairie sod for intensive commercial wheat monoculture, exposing the topsoil to wind erosion when cyclical droughts returned. Worster's study helped establish environmental history as a rigorous critique of economic hubris and unsustainable resource exploitation.

## William Cronon and Changing Land Ethics

Historian William Cronon made major contributions to environmental history by integrating ecological science with cultural analysis. In *Changes in the Land: Indians, Colonists, and the Ecology of New England* (1983), Cronon compared the ecological regimes of southern New England's Indigenous peoples with those of incoming English colonists. Indigenous groups practiced seasonal subsistence rounds, controlled understory burning, and mobile agriculture that maintained high ecological diversity without depleting local resources. In contrast, European settlers introduced bounded private property, intensive monoculture, commercial forestry, and livestock grazing, systematically converting diverse forest ecosystems into deforested pastures.

In *Nature's Metropolis: Chicago and the Great West* (1991), Cronon examined the urban-ecological linkages that bound the growth of nineteenth-century Chicago to the resource extraction of its vast western hinterland (lumber from northern forests, grain from prairie fields, and meat from Great Plains cattle ranches), arguing that urban centers and natural environments form integrated ecological systems.

## Institutionalization and Paleoclimatology

Environmental history emerged as an autonomous academic discipline during the 1970s, marked by the founding of the American Society for Environmental History (ASEH) in 1977 and the launch of its journal (founded as *Environmental Review* in 1976 and renamed *Environmental History* in 1996). The field rapidly diversified to include forest history, water law, urban environmental history, the history of toxics, and conservation policy.

Methodologically, environmental historians collaborate closely with the natural sciences, utilizing data from paleoclimatology, dendrochronology (tree-ring analysis), ice-core drilling, palynology (fossil pollen analysis), and archaeobiology. These scientific tools enable scholars to measure past climatic fluctuations—such as the Roman Climate Optimum, the Medieval Warm Period, and the Little Ice Age—with high chronological precision, allowing them to examine correlations between climatic shifts, agricultural harvests, epidemics, and sociopolitical crises.

## Environmental History and Braudel's Longue Durée

Environmental history shares significant intellectual ground with Fernand Braudel's concept of the *longue durée*, particularly Braudel's insistence on the profound influence of geographic and climatic structures on human civilizational patterns. However, modern environmental historians diverge from classical Annaliste geography in two vital respects:
1. **Agency of Nature:** While Braudel tended to treat physical geography as an essentially fixed, immutable stage upon which human history moved, environmental historians view the biophysical world as highly dynamic and reactive, capable of rapid and catastrophic evolutionary agency (such as mutating viral pathogens or invasive biological species).
2. **Anthropogenic Reciprocity:** Environmental history emphasizes that human economic and technological systems constantly alter and degrade the biophysical environment, creating anthropogenic feedbacks that return to constrain human civilizational development.

## The Anthropocene and Planetary History

In recent decades, environmental history has expanded toward planetary scales through engagement with the concept of the **Anthropocene**—the proposed geological epoch in which collective human activity has become a primary geological and Earth-system force. Historians such as J. R. McNeill (*Something New Under the Sun*, 2000) have analyzed the unprecedented twentieth-century acceleration in fossil-fuel consumption, carbon emissions, deforestation, and biodiversity loss, while Dipesh Chakrabarty ("The Climate of History," 2009) has asked what the Anthropocene means for historical thinking. This planetary turn challenges traditional historical periodization, compelling historians to integrate deep evolutionary time with the modern history of global industrial capitalism.
