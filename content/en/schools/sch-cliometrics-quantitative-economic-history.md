# Cliometrics and Quantitative Economic History

The application of the tools of econometrics and official statistics to questions of economic history, in an effort to test historical hypotheses quantitatively instead of relying on descriptive narrative alone.

## Origins and Method

This current arose in the United States in the late 1950s, when economists (often not historians by original training) began applying statistical mathematical models to quantitative historical data (price records, population censuses, shipping and trade records), in order to test causal hypotheses with a precision difficult to reach through qualitative narrative alone.

## Influential Examples

- **Robert Fogel**, *Railroads and American Economic Growth* (1964): he uses a "counterfactual" model to estimate what would have happened to American economic growth had the railroads not been built, concluding that their effect was less decisive than the traditional historical account had assumed. This method provoked wide debate about the legitimacy of "counterfactual history" in statistical form (see section 7 of this chapter).
- **Fogel and Stanley Engerman**, *Time on the Cross* (1974): a controversial quantitative analysis of the American slave economy, arguing that the slave system was economically "efficient" by productivity standards. This conclusion met with sharp methodological and ethical criticism from historians who saw it as an abstraction that overlooks the human suffering behind the numbers.

## Limitations

- **The problem of moral abstraction**: the focus on quantitative measurement may reduce the lived human experience to numbers (as in the critique of *Time on the Cross*), a fundamental tension between statistical precision and interpretive sensitivity.
- **Dependence on data quality**: quantitative results are highly sensitive to the quality and completeness of raw historical records, which are often incomplete or biased at their original source (such as customs or tax records that do not capture the informal economy).
