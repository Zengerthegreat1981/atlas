# Islamic Peripatetic Philosophy

An Islamic philosophical tradition that took Aristotle's philosophy, as read through its Greek, Syriac, and Neoplatonic commentators, as the basis for building a comprehensive science of being, soul, intellect, and politics. It reached its peak with Avicenna (Ibn Sina, d. 428 AH / 1037 CE) in the East and Averroes (Ibn Rushd, d. 595 AH / 1198 CE) in the Maghreb and al-Andalus. It rests on the view that rational demonstration and revelation do not conflict, and that philosophers and prophets address one truth in two different languages. The Arabs called it "Mashshā'iyya" (Peripatetic), after the "Peripatetics," the followers of Aristotle who used to study while walking in the Lyceum, and they called Aristotle "the First Teacher."

## Context and Founding

The tradition began in Abbasid Baghdad with the great translation movement from Greek and Syriac in the second to fourth centuries AH, carried out by translators including Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 260 AH) and his son Ishaq, Thabit ibn Qurra (d. 288 AH), Qusta ibn Luqa, Abu Bishr Matta ibn Yunus (d. 328 AH), and Yahya ibn 'Adi (d. 364 AH). Al-Kindi (d. c. 252 AH) is often called the first major philosopher writing in Arabic; he wrote *On First Philosophy* and *Treatise on the Intellect*, and he affirmed the agreement of philosophy and religious law. Then came al-Farabi (d. 339 AH), who organized logic and the sciences and set out the political and psychological doctrine, and from him it passed to Avicenna, who built the most elaborate Peripatetic system.

## Major Principles

- **Existence and essence**: Avicenna distinguished essence from existence, made existence additional to the essence of contingent beings, and held that in the Necessary Existent essence and existence are identical.
- **The argument from necessity and contingency**: every existent is either necessary in itself or contingent in itself; the contingent needs a cause, and the chain ends in the Necessary Existent, who is God. Avicenna called this the "proof of the truthful" (burhan al-siddiqin).
- **Emanation and the ten intellects**: from the Necessary proceeds the First Intellect, and from it the celestial intellects and souls, down to the Active Intellect, which bestows forms upon the world of generation and corruption and supplies the human intellect with intelligibles.
- **The soul**: a spiritual, non-bodily substance that comes into being with the body and does not perish with its perishing; Avicenna's illustration is the "flying" or "suspended" man, which indicates the direct awareness of the self.
- **Demonstration and logic**: Aristotelian logic, especially the *Posterior Analytics*, is an instrument for distinguishing right from wrong and a criterion of certainty.
- **Prophecy and the virtuous city**: for al-Farabi the prophet is a philosopher who connects with the Active Intellect through the imaginative faculty and so addresses the public in symbols, and the head of the virtuous city combines wisdom and religious law (*The Opinions of the Inhabitants of the Virtuous City*).
- **The resurrection of bodies**: Avicenna took a position between interpretation and acceptance, holding spiritual resurrection to be rationally established and bodily resurrection to be established by revealed law.

## Major Figures

- **Al-Kindi** (d. c. 252 AH): "the philosopher of the Arabs," who introduced philosophical terminology into Arabic.
- **Al-Farabi** (d. 339 AH): "the Second Teacher," author of *Enumeration of the Sciences*, *The Opinions of the Inhabitants of the Virtuous City*, and *The Harmonization of the Opinions of the Two Sages* (attributed to him).
- **Avicenna** (d. 428 AH): "al-Shaykh al-Ra'is," author of *The Healing*, *The Salvation*, and *Pointers and Reminders*.
- **Abu al-Barakat al-Baghdadi** (d. after 560 AH): author of *Al-Mu'tabar fi al-Hikma*, who criticized many of the principles of Peripateticism.
- **Ibn Bajja** (d. 533 AH): author of *The Regime of the Solitary*.
- **Ibn Tufayl** (d. 581 AH): author of *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*.
- **Averroes** (d. 595 AH): "the Great Commentator" on Aristotle, and author of *The Decisive Treatise* and *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*.
- **Nasir al-Din al-Tusi** (d. 672 AH): commentator on the *Pointers*, and a link between Peripateticism and Imami theology (kalam).

## Criticism and Extension

- **Al-Ghazali**: wrote *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (c. 488 AH), listing twenty problems, and pronounced the philosophers unbelievers on three: the eternity of the world, God's not knowing particulars, and the denial of the resurrection of bodies; Averroes answered him in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*.
- **Ibn Taymiyya** (d. 728 AH): criticized Aristotle's logic in *Refutation of the Logicians*, and held that the Aristotelian definition and syllogism are not necessary for certainty.
- **Al-Suhrawardi** (d. 587 AH): founded Illuminationism as a critique of Peripateticism, while drawing on Avicenna's logic.
- **The School of Isfahan and Mulla Sadra**: incorporated Peripateticism into the Transcendent Theosophy together with illumination and gnosis.
- **The Latin West and Judaism**: Avicenna and Averroes were translated into Latin in Toledo and elsewhere in the sixth and seventh centuries AH, and they influenced Thomas Aquinas, Albertus Magnus, and the Latin Averroists; the Jewish philosopher Moses Maimonides was influenced by al-Farabi, Avicenna, and Ibn Bajja in his *Guide of the Perplexed*.
- **Modern Arab thought**: Muhammad Abed al-Jabri in *Nahnu wa-l-Turath* (1980) and *Takwin al-'Aql al-'Arabi* (1984) drew a distinction between an Avicennan East influenced by gnosis and a rational Averroist West, a division that many have discussed, and Abd al-Rahman Badawi charted the entry of Aristotle into Arabic in *Aristu 'inda al-'Arab* (1947).

## Limits of the School

- Peripateticism was not a homogeneous unity: al-Farabi and Avicenna differed over details of the soul and logic, and Averroes criticized them over emanation and the ten intellects.
- There is a debate over how Peripatetic Avicenna himself was: Henry Corbin read his "Eastern" (mashriqiyya) philosophy as a mystical, illuminationist departure from Aristotelianism, whereas Dimitri Gutas argues that it is simply Avicenna's own mature philosophical system within the Aristotelian tradition.
- It has been held against the Peripatetics that their reliance on translated sources, some of them pseudonymous, such as the *Theology of Aristotle* (which is a summary of Plotinus's *Enneads*), weakened their distinction between Aristotle and Plotinus.
