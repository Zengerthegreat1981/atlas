# History of Western Law

## Introduction: Why Write the History of Law?

The **history of Western law** is concerned with studying the development of legal systems in Europe and the Anglo-Saxon world across the ages, not as a sequential narrative of legislation, but as an attempt to understand how legal rules take shape from the interaction between political authority, social custom, and the moral and religious thought prevailing in each era. This field cannot be understood in isolation from the parallel experience of **Islamic jurisprudence**. Since al-Shafii in *al-Risala*, the scholars of Usul al-Fiqh developed a methodology for tracing the development of rulings through tools such as abrogation (*naskh*), consensus (*ijma*) and analogy (*qiyas*). Later, al-Juwayni and al-Ghazali developed the theory of public interest (*maslaha*) that later underpinned the idea of the overarching objectives (*maqasid*) of the law. In function, this corresponds to the project of "the historical development of law" among Westerners, although the underlying epistemological sources differ substantially.

## Historical Context: From Rome to Modernity

The classical account usually begins with Roman law and its codification in the *Corpus Juris Civilis* of Justinian (compiled 529–534 CE). That text was rediscovered in the eleventh century at the University of Bologna, giving rise to the movement of the **Glossators**, followed by the **Commentators** (Post-Glossators), and from it emerged the continental **civil law** tradition. In parallel, from the Norman Conquest (1066), England developed a different tradition, the **common law**, based on judicial precedent rather than central legislative codification. This foundational division still governs the map of contemporary legal systems.

## Central Ideas: Nation, Spirit and Custom

At the start of the nineteenth century, in opposition to calls (notably by A. F. J. Thibaut, 1814) for codifying German law on the French model (the Napoleonic Code of 1804), **Friedrich Carl von Savigny** led what became known as the **Historical School of Law** (*Historische Rechtsschule*). He argued that law is not the product of abstract rational legislation that can be enacted at any time and place. Rather, it is an organic expression of the "spirit of the people" (*Volksgeist*), its history and customs. It grows gradually, like language, and is not made all at once by a legislative decision. This thesis later influenced the drafting of the **German Civil Code** (BGB, 1900) through his follower Georg Friedrich Puchta and the Pandectist school.

## Key Thinkers: Henry Maine

The Englishman **Henry Sumner Maine**, in his book *Ancient Law* (1861), developed a complementary thesis: that societies move historically from law based on **status** (where rights and duties are determined by family or class membership) to law based on **contract** (where obligations are determined by the free will of individuals). This thesis influenced the sociology of law as much as legal historiography itself. This evolutionary linear current was later criticized by historians who regarded it as projecting a single, Europe-centered model onto diverse histories.

## Impact and Application: From History to Comparison

The history of Western law contributed to the foundation of the modern field of **comparative law**, since understanding the roots of each legal system (Roman-Germanic, common law, or mixed) became a precondition for understanding its internal logic and explaining its differences from others. It also influenced Islamic jurisprudence through the project of **Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri**, who, in drafting the Egyptian Civil Code (1949), drew on a methodology combining an investigation of the history of Islamic jurisprudence with the techniques of Western comparative law. This produced a hybrid model that was influential in several Arab countries.

## The Historical School versus the Analytical School

A methodological tension arose within the field between two currents: a **historical-evolutionary** current (represented by Savigny and Maine), which sees law as the product of slow organic growth that cannot be reduced to a single legislative moment, and a later **analytical-positivist** current (represented by Austin and then Hart), which separates the study of the logical structure of law from the history of its actual emergence. Later historians, such as Harold Berman in *Law and Revolution* (1983), sought to transcend this division by showing how the "Western legal tradition" itself emerged from a specific historical revolutionary moment: the Gregorian papal reform of the eleventh century and the establishment of a systematic canon law, rather than from a continuous, linear organic development as the German Historical School had imagined.

## Comparative Law and Multiple Paths

Subsequent research in legal history showed that the binary picture (common law versus civil law) simplifies a more complex reality. Mixed systems emerged (Scotland, South Africa, Louisiana) that combined elements of both traditions. Eastern Europe, after the Cold War, also followed particular paths to rebuild its legal systems, combining a former socialist legacy with Western European models. This diversity made legal history a field that works closely with the sociology of law and legal anthropology, in order to understand how official texts interact with actual local practices.

## Critique

Traditional Western legal history is criticized from two angles. First, its tendency to portray European legal development as a universal, inevitable course that other civilizations should follow, ignoring the autonomy of other legal traditions such as Islamic jurisprudence with its own logic, not as a stage preceding Western modernity. Second, its occasional immersion in institutional detail at the expense of the deeper philosophical question: why law changes at all, and how this change relates to power and interest rather than to the abstract "spirit" alone. Social historians, influenced by Marxism and American legal realism, have raised an additional critique: that traditional institutional history tends to neglect the role of marginalized classes, women and colonized peoples in shaping actual law, limiting itself to recounting the decisions of judicial and legislative elites.
