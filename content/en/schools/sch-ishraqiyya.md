# Illuminationist Philosophy (Ishrāqiyya)

An Islamic philosophical current founded by Shihab al-Din Yahya ibn Habash al-Suhrawardi (executed in Aleppo in 587 AH / 1191 CE), called the "Wisdom of Illumination" (hikmat al-ishraq). In it "discursive wisdom" (the inferential wisdom of the Peripatetics) is set against "experiential wisdom" (the wisdom of unveiling), and light is adopted as the foundation of existence and knowledge. He claims that it is a revival of an ancient, eternal wisdom going back to Hermes (Idris), Plato, the ancient sages of Persia (Kay Khusraw, Farshawshtar, and Jamasp), and the early Sufis such as al-Bistami and al-Hallaj.

## Life and Context

Al-Suhrawardi was born in Suhraward near Zanjan around 549 AH / 1154 CE, studied at Maragha with Majd al-Din al-Jili and at Isfahan with Zahir al-Din al-Qari, and then travelled to Anatolia and Syria. In Aleppo he was received by al-Malik al-Zahir Ghazi, son of Saladin, and he provoked the aversion of the jurists through his boldness in theology; he was accused of allowing the appearance of a prophet after the Prophet, and so he was tried, imprisoned, and killed in 587 AH, at about thirty-six years of age. His students called him "the Slain Shaykh" and "Shaykh al-Ishraq."

## Major Works

- **The Philosophy of Illumination (Hikmat al-Ishraq)** (in Arabic): the highest-ranking work, in two parts: logic and light.
- **Al-Talwihat, al-Lamahat, al-Muqawamat, and al-Mashari' wa-l-Mutarahat**: books of the Peripatetic phase.
- **Hayakil al-Nur (The Temples of Light)**: a short treatise on lights.
- **The Tale of the Occidental Exile, The Red Intellect, The Song of Gabriel's Wing**, and others: symbolic tales in Arabic and Persian about the soul's journey from the West (the world of matter) to the East (the world of light).
- **Al-Alwah al-Imadiyya**: a work dedicated to the Artuqid ruler Imad al-Din Qara Arslan.

## Central Ideas

- **Light**: light is a reality manifest in itself that needs no definition; it is the first of existents, and darkness is its non-existence. Existents differ by the intensity and weakness of light, and the highest of the lights is the "Light of Lights" (God), from which issue the high dominating lights (the ranks of the intellects) and then the governing lights.
- **Knowledge by presence**: knowledge is not based only on conception and assent through definitions and syllogisms, but occurs through the direct presence of the known before the knower. By this a person knows himself with an immediate knowledge, by neither definition nor demonstration.
- **Critique of the Peripatetic definition**: he held that the Aristotelian definition by genus and differentia does not encompass the quiddity, and that simple realities are known by direct witnessing.
- **The world of images ('alam al-mithal)**: an intermediate world between intellect and sense, with self-subsistent forms (suspended images) that are not in a substrate, which explains vision, unveiling, the resurrection, and the images of dreams; Henry Corbin called it the "imaginal world" (mundus imaginalis).
- **Lords of the species (arbab al-anwa')**: he revived the idea of the Platonic Forms as governing lights of the natural species.
- **Deification (ta'alluh)**: the sage's aim is to combine discursive inquiry with experiential taste, and one who confines himself to only one of them is not counted a sage.

## Commentators and Continuation

- **Shams al-Din al-Shahrazuri** (d. after 687 AH): author of "Nuzhat al-Arwah" on the biographies of the sages, and of a commentary on the "Philosophy of Illumination."
- **Ibn Kammuna** (d. 683 AH): a Jewish philosopher from Baghdad, author of a "Commentary on al-Talwihat" and "al-Jadid fi al-Hikma," and famous for his book "Tanqih al-Abhath li-l-Milal al-Thalath."
- **Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi** (d. 710 AH): wrote the best-known commentary on the "Philosophy of Illumination" in 694 AH, and was a student of Nasir al-Din al-Tusi.
- **Jalal al-Din al-Dawani** (d. 908 AH): commentator on "Hayakil al-Nur" in "Shawakil al-Hur," of the Shiraz school.
- **Mulla Sadra** (d. c. 1045 AH): wrote glosses on al-Shirazi's commentary, and merged Illuminationist concepts with the primacy of existence in Transcendent Theosophy.
- **Henry Corbin** (d. 1978 CE): published al-Suhrawardi's texts and studied his legacy within "Iranian Islam," and highlighted his role in the philosophy of imagination and the "imaginal world."

## Difference from Peripateticism

The Peripatetics present rational demonstration alone as the path to knowledge, make substance and accident the structure of existence, and regard the image as subjective imagining. The Illuminationists, by contrast, combine demonstration with unveiling, make light the structure of existence and its ranks, affirm an imaginal world with an independent existence, and hold that primary knowledge is by presence and needs no definition. This difference is nevertheless not a complete break: al-Suhrawardi relies on Ibn Sina's logic and physics in many places, and the "Philosophy of Illumination" remains in dialogue with the Peripatetics throughout the text.

## Ibn Sab'in and Illuminationism

A distinctive parallel to the Illuminationist critique of Peripateticism emerged in the western Islamic world with the Andalusian philosopher and mystic **Abd al-Haqq Ibn Sab'in** (c. 614–669 AH / 1217–1270 CE), author of *Budd al-'Arif* and the *Sicilian Questions* (answers sent to the Hohenstaufen emperor Frederick II). Like al-Suhrawardi, Ibn Sab'in launched a radical critique of Aristotelian logic, rejecting the standard Peripatetic definition by genus and specific difference as incapable of yielding genuine knowledge of essences, and tracing true wisdom back to Hermes and an ancient lineage of divinely guided sages. However, whereas al-Suhrawardi constructed an elaborate ontology of graded lights and affirmed an independent intermediate realm of images (*'alam al-mithal*), Ibn Sab'in advanced a doctrine of radical and unqualified unity (*al-wahda al-mutlaqa*). In Ibn Sab'in's ontology, existence is strictly one and indivisible; the appearance of multiplicity, cosmic hierarchies, and graded hypostases is an illusion of the estimative faculty. While Suhrawardi's Illuminationism (*ishraq*) retained systematic philosophical architecture to lead the seeker toward direct presence (*hudur*), Ibn Sab'in's "divine wisdom" (*al-hikma al-ilahiyya*) discarded metaphysical mediation in favor of an uncompromising realization that nothing exists other than God.

## Limits and Criticism

- Critics among the Peripatetics and the theologians (mutakallimun) hold that the science of lights remains closer to a metaphysical representation than to strict demonstration, and that his claim of an eternal wisdom is founded on sources that cannot be historically documented.
- Jurists accused him of heresy (zandaqa), of denying the divine attributes (ta'til), or of claiming prophecy, accusations that later sources tried to refute.
- His philosophical influence among Sunnis remained limited compared with his influence on the Iranian Shi'i heritage, and its transmission to the Arab world was slow.
