# Indigenous Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Indigenous ethics is a framework that brings together the diverse value systems of local communities, such as the Indigenous peoples of North America, Australia and Africa. These systems share an emphasis on the organic interconnection between humans, the land, society and future generations. This contrasts with modern Western ethics, which focuses on the autonomous individual as the bearer of rights.

## Historical Context

These moral systems were transmitted orally for thousands of years through stories, rituals and customs before academic philosophers began to study them systematically in the late twentieth century. That study took place in the context of movements to restore Indigenous rights and of critiques of colonialism, which had suppressed this knowledge in favor of Western models imposed by colonial administrations. The field gained momentum with the rise of global environmental awareness, as many Indigenous resource-management practices came to be recognized as models of long-term sustainability.

## Central Ideas

Among the most prominent principles is the concept of relationality, which holds that the human being is not separate from nature but part of a living web that includes animals, plants and the land itself. This produces ethical obligations toward non-human beings that are no less serious than obligations toward humans.

Another principle is responsibility between generations. It is often expressed as the "Seventh Generation Principle," commonly associated with the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), which requires that any collective decision be evaluated by its impact on the well-being of the seven generations to come, not merely on immediate interest.

A third is the model of restorative justice, which focuses on repairing the damaged relationship between offender, victim and community, rather than on punitive retribution. This approach today inspires reforms within Western criminal justice systems themselves.

## Key Thinkers

Vine Deloria Jr. contributed to establishing a philosophical critique of Western philosophy from the perspective of Native Americans in his book *God Is Red* (1973). Later writers such as Kyle Powys Whyte and Robin Wall Kimmerer have developed contemporary environmental and relational ethics grounded in Indigenous knowledge and kinship. Alongside them stand the contributions of traditional elders, who transmitted this knowledge orally across generations without it being attributed to a single individual author in the Western academic sense.

## Application and Impact

These principles are used today in drafting policies for the management of shared natural resources. They also support environmental justice movements that demand the involvement of Indigenous communities in decisions concerning their lands, as in disputes over oil pipelines in North America that tribes opposed on grounds of water protection and sacred sites.

## Comparison with Islamic Environmental Ethics

Indigenous ethics converges with the Qur'anic concept of *istikhlaf* (human stewardship on earth) in its respect for nature. This concept makes the human being a trustee of the earth rather than its absolute owner, based on the verse "And it is He who has made you successors upon the earth" (Qur'an 6:165). A hadith reported in Ibn Majah, whose chain many scholars grade as weak, warns against wastefulness even in water during ablution, "even if you were at a flowing river." This warning parallels the principle of responsibility between generations in Indigenous thought, despite the difference in the doctrinal framework. Islamic stewardship is founded on an explicit divine command that entails accountability in the hereafter. Indigenous ethics rests on a cosmic conception of vital interconnection, without necessarily referring to a personal legislating God.

## Tension with Industrial Modernity

This ethical system clashes in practice with the modern industrial development model, which is based on maximizing production and consumption in the short term. This has led to recurrent disputes over extracting resources from lands that Indigenous peoples regard as sacred or as intergenerational trusts. The dispute over the Dakota Access Pipeline, which the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe opposed in 2016, is one example. It became a global symbol of the tension between the logic of immediate economic profit and the logic of long-term responsibility between generations.

## Oral Knowledge and Narrative

The way these ethics are transmitted differs radically from academic written philosophy. They are passed on through stories, myths and collective rituals rather than through abstract philosophical treatises. A tale about an animal that disrupts the balance of the forest and is punished is not mere entertainment. It is a complete moral lesson on which the community's practical conduct is built. This makes the academic recording of these ethics a real methodological challenge. Many elders of these peoples fear that written documentation will strip wisdom of its living context, which is tied to oral performance and place.

Nevertheless, careful academic documentation of these teachings has become necessary to protect them from total disappearance. This documentation must be carried out in close cooperation with traditional knowledge holders, with respect for their intellectual rights, and by involving them directly in drafting any written text about their knowledge. It should not be limited to studying them as an external object from the perspective of a foreign researcher. An equal partnership in producing knowledge is an ethical requirement no less important than the content of the knowledge itself.
