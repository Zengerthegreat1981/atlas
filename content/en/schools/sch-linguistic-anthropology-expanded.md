# Linguistic Anthropology

Linguistic anthropology is the study of language as a cultural resource and speaking as a cultural practice. It asks how people use language to do things in the world and how those uses shape the societies they live in. Where descriptive linguistics asks how a language is structured, and cognitive anthropology asks how people classify and think, linguistic anthropology asks what speaking accomplishes socially: how a greeting, a pronoun, a silence, or a shift between languages carries meaning no dictionary can capture.

## History

The school was born in the United States at the start of the twentieth century as one of the four fields of North American anthropology. Franz Boas and his students recorded the languages of Indigenous peoples of North America, many of them endangered. Boas's introduction to the Handbook of American Indian Languages (1911) argued that language, race, and culture were independent of one another, and that each language had to be described in its own terms rather than forced into the grammatical categories of Latin or English.

Boas's student Edward Sapir carried the project from description toward theory. In Language (1921) he argued that language was a purely human and non-instinctive method of communicating ideas, emotions, and desires by means of voluntarily produced symbols, and that each language embodied a distinctive way of apprehending the world. Sapir's student Benjamin Lee Whorf pushed the argument further, claiming in essays written in the 1930s and collected posthumously in Language, Thought, and Reality (1956) that speakers dissect nature along lines laid down by their native languages.

A second phase began in the early 1960s, when Dell Hymes and John Gumperz shifted attention from language as a system to speech as social action. Hymes's introduction to the ethnography of communication in 1964, and the collection Directions in Sociolinguistics (1972), which he edited with Gumperz, argued that knowing a language meant knowing when to speak, to whom, in what manner, and with what consequences — what Hymes called communicative competence.

## Key Figures

Franz Boas (1858–1942), founder of American anthropology, established the documentary and relativist program on which the school rests. Edward Sapir (1884–1939), his student, linked linguistic structure to culture and thought. Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897–1941) gave the relativity thesis its boldest formulation. Dell Hymes (1927–2009) founded the ethnography of communication and the concept of communicative competence. John Gumperz (1922–2013) developed interactional sociolinguistics, showing how subtle cues of intonation and code choice shape misunderstanding across cultural boundaries. Michael Silverstein (1945–2020) of the University of Chicago developed the semiotic theory of indexicality and metapragmatics that underpins much contemporary work. Alessandro Duranti has written on Samoan oratory. Elinor Ochs and Bambi Schieffelin pioneered the study of language socialization, showing how children are socialized into culture through language and into language through culture.

## Key Works

Boas's Handbook of American Indian Languages (1911) is the founding documentary project. Whorf's Language, Thought, and Reality (1956) collected the essays that made linguistic relativity famous. Hymes's "Introduction: Toward Ethnographies of Communication" (1964) launched the second paradigm, and Gumperz and Hymes's Directions in Sociolinguistics (1972) consolidated it. Silverstein's "Shifters, Linguistic Categories, and Cultural Description" (1976) introduced the Peircean semiotic framework that reshaped the field. Ochs and Schieffelin's "Language Acquisition and Socialization: Three Developmental Stories" (1984) founded the study of language socialization. Silverstein and Greg Urban's Natural Histories of Discourse (1996) argued that the divide between fleeting speech and formed text is itself socially constructed. Duranti's Linguistic Anthropology (1997) is the standard textbook.

## Method

Linguistic anthropologists combine long-term ethnographic fieldwork with close analysis of recorded speech. Participant observation establishes the social settings in which talk matters; audio and video recording preserves the details of interaction — pauses, overlaps, intonation, gesture — that a notebook cannot. Transcription is itself an analytical act: the ethnographer decides what to represent and how, and the transcript becomes the evidence for claims about meaning. Analysis moves between the micro-detail of a single exchange and the macro-structure of the society, tracing how a pronoun choice or a code-switch indexes identity, authority, or stance. The field's methods have been adopted in education, law, and medicine, wherever the fine grain of talk matters.

## Influence and Contemporary Debates

Linguistic anthropology reshaped anthropology's understanding of culture, showing that culture lives not only in beliefs and institutions but in the moment-by-moment work of speaking. It gave the Sapir-Whorf debate a new empirical footing: later work showed that language can shape attention, memory, and spatial reasoning without determining thought outright, a subtler relativity than Whorf's boldest claims. The field has also become a central site for the study of language endangerment and revitalization, as Indigenous communities work to reclaim languages suppressed by colonial schooling.

Two areas show how the school's founding concern, what speaking does in the world, extends to the present. The first is code-switching, the alternation between languages or varieties within a conversation. Jan-Petter Blom and John Gumperz's study of dialect and standard in a Norwegian community (1972) showed that switches signal social meaning, such as a change of topic, relationship, or footing, rather than mere lack of competence. The second is digital communication: work on texting, social media, and messaging, for example Ilana Gershon's *The Breakup 2.0* (2010) on "media ideologies" and Jones, Chik, and Hafner's edited *Discourse and Digital Practices* (2015), asks how new media create new patterns of interaction and new forms of linguistic identity.
