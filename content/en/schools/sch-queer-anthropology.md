# Queer Anthropology

Queer anthropology is the study of sexuality, gender, and non-normative desire across cultures, and of the ways societies organize intimacy, kinship, and identity. It asks how categories such as homosexual, lesbian, gay, transgender, and queer itself are made, lived, and contested in different places — and how people whose desires and bodies fall outside local norms build lives, communities, and forms of resistance. The field takes its name from queer theory, but its method is ethnographic: it grounds abstract debates about sexuality in the texture of everyday life.

## History

The field's roots lie in the anthropology of gender and sexuality in the 1970s and 1980s. Esther Newton's *Mother Camp* (1972), an ethnography of drag queens in the United States, was a landmark: it treated a stigmatized subculture with ethnographic seriousness and showed that gender is performed rather than simply given. Gilbert Herdt's *Guardians of the Flutes* (1981), a study of ritualized male same-sex practices among the Sambia of Papua New Guinea, challenged the assumption that homosexuality was a single, universal identity, showing instead that same-sex behavior could carry entirely different meanings in different societies. Gayle Rubin's essay "Thinking Sex" (1984) provided a theoretical foundation, arguing that sexuality must be analyzed as a political system with its own hierarchies. The term "queer anthropology" gained currency in the 1990s as anthropologists engaged with queer theory's critique of stable identity categories. The edited volume *Out in the Field* (1996), by Ellen Lewin and William Leap, brought together reflections on doing ethnography as a lesbian or gay anthropologist. Martin Manalansan's *Global Divas* (2003) examined Filipino gay men in New York, showing how migration, race, and care work shape queer life. Tom Boellstorff's *The Gay Archipelago* (2005) studied gay and lesbian Indonesians, arguing that sexuality is shaped by national belonging and religious politics. In the 2010s the field expanded to address trans lives, queer kinship, and the globalization of LGBTQ rights discourses.

## Key Figures and Works

Esther Newton's *Mother Camp* (1972) is the field's founding ethnography, and her later work on the Fire Island community of Cherry Grove extended its scope. Gilbert Herdt's *Guardians of the Flutes* (1981) and *Same Sex, Different Cultures* (1997) established the comparative study of same-sex sexuality. Gayle Rubin's "Thinking Sex" (1984) remains the most cited theoretical text, introducing the idea of a "charmed circle" of acceptable sexuality. Ellen Lewin and William Leap's *Out in the Field* (1996) made the anthropologist's own sexuality a legitimate topic of reflection. Martin Manalansan's *Global Divas* (2003) connected queer life to migration and global labor. Tom Boellstorff's *The Gay Archipelago* (2005) is a model of how to study sexuality without imposing Western categories. Margot Weiss's *Techniques of Pleasure* (2011) examined BDSM communities in the San Francisco Bay Area, extending queer anthropology to questions of power, consent, and embodiment. Niko Besnier and Kalissa Alexeyeff's work on gender diversity in the Pacific has shown how categories such as fa'afafine in Samoa complicate Western ideas of transgender identity. Evelyn Blackwood's research on tombois in Indonesia has likewise challenged the assumption that lesbian identity travels unchanged across cultures.

## Method

Queer anthropology shares the discipline's commitment to long-term fieldwork, but it brings distinctive methodological concerns. Researchers must navigate secrecy and risk: in societies where same-sex desire is criminalized or stigmatized, protecting informants is a matter of safety, not merely ethics. The field is also reflexive about the researcher's own position, since questions about sexuality are rarely neutral and the ethnographer's gender and desires shape what they can observe. A key method is the careful study of local categories: rather than asking whether a society "has" homosexuality, anthropologists ask what local terms exist — fa'afafine, hijra, tomboi, bakla — and what social roles they carry. Life histories are central, tracing how people come to understand their desires within family, religion, and the state. The field also studies institutions: bars, churches, clinics, NGOs, and dating apps as sites where queer life is organized. Increasingly, queer anthropologists work collaboratively with the communities they study, blurring the line between research and activism.

## Influence and Debates

Queer anthropology's central contribution is the demonstration that sexuality is not a universal drive expressed in different costumes, but a culturally and historically specific organization of desire, identity, and power. This insight challenged both the medical model of homosexuality and the assumption that Western identity categories — gay, lesbian, transgender — describe human nature. The field influenced queer theory by grounding it in ethnographic evidence, and influenced anthropology by showing that kinship, gender, and the state cannot be understood without sexuality. A central debate concerns the globalization of LGBTQ identities: some scholars argue that Western categories are imposed on other societies through human-rights discourse and media, while others emphasize how people in the Global South actively remake those categories for their own purposes. Another debate concerns the relationship between queer anthropology and activism, as researchers navigate solidarity with communities under threat. The field has also shaped public understanding of gender diversity, offering alternatives to binary models of sex and gender. As states around the world debate LGBTQ rights, queer anthropology provides evidence that there is no single natural order of sexuality — only many human ways of living desire.
