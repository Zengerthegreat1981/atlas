# Critical Kantianism

The philosophical current built on the "critical philosophy" of Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) in Germany in the 1780s and 1790s and after. His major works include the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781; second edition 1787), the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) and the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790). This entry addresses the founding and early reception of the doctrine and the controversy that arose around it in Kant's lifetime and immediately after; Kant's ethics in contemporary philosophy belongs to another entry.

## The Critical Project

Kant wanted to overcome the dispute between the rationalists (Leibniz and Wolff), who held that reason reaches the great truths by concepts alone, and the empiricists (Locke and Hume), who reduced knowledge to sensation. His answer was that knowledge has two elements: a matter that comes from the senses, and a priori forms in the mind (space and time in sensibility, the categories in the understanding) that organize this matter. Hence he distinguished between the "appearance" that we know and the "thing in itself" that we cannot penetrate. In this way he argued for the validity of natural science and against the possibility of traditional metaphysics as theoretical knowledge, which tries to know the soul, the world and God by theoretical reason alone, including its contradictions (the antinomies). Conversely, practical philosophy argued for the primacy of moral reason, which gives us freedom, duty and the three postulates (freedom, immortality and the existence of God) as conditions of morality rather than theoretical knowledge.

## The Copernican Revolution

Kant said that reading Hume woke him from his "dogmatic slumber", and that he wanted to do in philosophy what Copernicus did in astronomy: instead of assuming that knowledge conforms to fixed objects, we assume that objects conform to the a priori conditions of our knowledge. By this reversal it became possible to explain how laws such as causality are true with universal necessity of every possible experience, something Hume was unable to do, because the a priori forms are what make experience objective. He called this position "transcendental idealism", to distinguish it from Berkeley's idealism.

## The Third Critique

The *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790) treats the faculty of judgment in two domains. Aesthetic judgment claims universal validity for judgments of beauty without resting on a concept, and is described as disinterested and as a free play of the cognitive faculties; the sublime is treated alongside it. Teleological judgment considers natural organisms as if they were purposively organized, a regulative principle rather than a constitutive claim. Kant intended the work to bridge the gap between the realms of nature and freedom, and it strongly influenced Schiller, Schelling, and Romantic aesthetics.

## First Reception

- **First reception**: in 1782 the *Göttingische gelehrte Anzeigen* published a review of the *Critique* accusing it of being an idealism like Berkeley's; Kant replied in the *Prolegomena* (1783), and in 1785 published the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*.
- **Reinhold's Letters**: Karl Leonhard Reinhold presented the critical philosophy in his *Letters on the Kantian Philosophy* (1786–1787) in the *Teutscher Merkur*, which won it wide fame; he then sought to ground it on a first principle that he called the "principle of consciousness" in his *New Theory of the Human Faculty of Representation* (1789).
- **Jacobi**: in *David Hume on Faith* (1787) he wrote that "without the thing in itself I cannot enter the system, and with it I cannot remain in it", a phrase that became the label of the sharpest objection.
- **Maimon and Schulze**: Salomon Maimon (1790) and Gottlob Ernst Schulze ("Aenesidemus", 1792) pushed the objection further, to the claim that the "thing in itself" is a contradictory concept and that the categories have no justification, which spurred Fichte, Schelling and Hegel to move toward idealism.
- **Religion**: Kant published *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* (1793), which provoked Prussian censorship in 1794 and he was obliged not to write on religion.
- **Jena**: in the 1790s the University of Jena became a center for the spread of Kantianism through the *Allgemeine Literatur-Zeitung*, and Reinhold, Schiller, and (from 1794) Fichte taught there.

## Leading Supporters and Innovators

- **Karl Leonhard Reinhold** (1757–1823): advocate of Kantianism, then founder of the "philosophy of elements".
- **Carl Christian Erhard Schmid** (1761–1812): author of the *Dictionary for the Easier Use of Kant's Writings* (1788).
- **Friedrich Schiller** (1759–1805): linked beauty to freedom in the *Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man* (1795).
- **Salomon Maimon** (c. 1753–1800): the critic who turned skepticism into a path of renewal.
- **Fichte** (1762–1814): made the absolute I the foundation of the doctrine in the *Science of Knowledge* (1794), without Kant's agreeing with him.

## Extension

The Kantian legacy split into several paths: the idealism of Fichte, Schelling and Hegel; the Neo-Kantianism of Marburg and Baden, from the later nineteenth century, which took up the call to return to Kant; and an influence on analytic philosophy (Strawson) and on phenomenology. In modern Islamic thought, Muhammad Iqbal in *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* (1930) discussed Kant's critique of the arguments for the existence of God, and in Arab culture Abdel Rahman Badawi offered expositions of German idealism, and Kant's major books were translated into Arabic in multiple translations.

## Limits and Critique

- The relation of the "thing in itself" to appearance has remained a matter of continuing dispute, with readings pulled between the "two-worlds" and the "two-aspects" interpretations (Henry Allison).
- Hegel criticized the formalism of Kantian ethics, a dispute that bears on contemporary moral philosophy.
- Later studies debated how well-founded the classification itself is, since many of these figures also belong to early German Idealism, and it is hard to separate "Kantianism" from "post-Kantianism".
