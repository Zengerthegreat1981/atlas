# Augustinianism

A philosophical-theological tradition founded by **Augustine of Hippo** (354–430 CE), bishop of Hippo in North Africa. Augustine is regarded as one of the most influential Christian thinkers in the West before Thomas Aquinas and as a formative figure of **Latin Christian philosophy**. His Augustinianism influenced medieval philosophy (Thomism, Bonaventurianism), the Protestant Reformation (Luther and Calvin), and modern philosophy (existentialism, phenomenology). His contributions include epistemology (divine illumination), the theory of language, political theology (*The City of God*), the theory of time, the problem of evil, and the problem of freedom and grace.

## Founding Context

Augustine was born in Tagaste (present-day Algeria) to a Christian mother (Monica) and a pagan father (Patricius). He studied rhetoric in Carthage and was influenced by Mani (Manichaeism) before being converted to Christianity in Milan in 386 CE at the hands of Bishop Ambrose (Ambrose of Milan). He was baptized, then returned to Africa and became bishop of Hippo (395 CE until his death in 430 CE). He lived through the fall of Rome to the Visigoths (410 CE), and wrote *The City of God* in response to the pagans.

## Prominent Figures

- **Augustine himself** (354–430): the synthesizer, who left an extensive body of treatises, letters and sermons.
- **Ambrose** (Ambrose of Milan, c. 339–397): bishop of Milan, whose preaching and allegorical reading of scripture influenced Augustine, and who baptised him in 387.
- **Monica** (Monica of Hippo, c. 331–387): his mother, venerated as a saint.
- **Possidius** (bishop of Calama): Augustine's disciple and biographer (*Life of Augustine*).
- **Simplicianus** (d. 400): the Milanese priest who encouraged Augustine's conversion and succeeded Ambrose as bishop.
- **Marius Victorinus** (c. 280–c. 365): Roman rhetorician and convert, translator of Platonist books, whose conversion story moved Augustine (*Confessions* VIII).

## Central Concepts

### 1. Divine Illumination (Illuminatio)

Augustine's theory of knowledge: the human mind cannot reach truth by itself but needs divine **illumination** (an inner light). Christ, the inner teacher, illuminates the mind so that it can grasp eternal truths (*De Magistro*). The doctrine was shaped by his reading of Platonist books (Plotinus and Porphyry) but is reworked in Christian terms.

### 2. The City of God and the City of Earth (Civitas Dei / Civitas Terrena)

In *The City of God* (De Civitate Dei, 413–426 CE), Augustine divided history into two cities:
- **The City of God** (Civitas Dei): the community of believers who live for the "love of God."
- **The City of Earth** (Civitas Terrena): the community of those who live for "love of self even to the contempt of God."

History is a struggle between the two cities. The ecclesial state is a distant goal (the end of history), but on earth a **state** remains that restrains evil. This gave a basis for Christian **political theology**.

### 3. Time (Tempus)

In *Confessions* XI Augustine asks what time is and concludes that past, present and future exist in the soul as memory, attention and expectation, so that time is a "distension of the mind" (*distentio animi*). History is read as a linear drama from creation to judgment, not a cycle. God is outside time.

### 4. The Problem of Evil (Theodicy)

If God is good and omnipotent, why does evil exist? Augustine's reply: evil is not a "thing" (res) but an **absence** of good (privatio boni), like darkness, which is the lack of light. Evil results from the **misuse** of free will (the fall of Adam).

### 5. Original Sin (Peccatum Originale)

All human beings were born in the sin of Adam, because Adam is "in" all of us as the representative of humanity. Therefore every human being needs divine redemption (Christ). This theoretically conflicts with **Pelagius**, who held that a human being can do good by nature.

### 6. Grace (Gratia)

The human task depends on a free divine **grace** (gratia). Grace is not earned but given. In his later anti-Pelagian writings, and against monks of southern Gaul (later called Semi-Pelagians), he defended **predestination** (Praedestinatio): God chose some for salvation from eternity.

### 7. The Inner Trinity (Trinitas Animi)

The human being is the image of God. Just as the Trinity is three persons in one substance, so too the human soul: **memory** (Memoria), **understanding** (Intellectus), **will** (Voluntas). These are offered as an imperfect analogy of the Trinity, not as a proof of it.

### 8. Self-Recollection (Memoria Sui)

The *Confessions* (Confessiones) are a self-recollection (a spiritual autobiography): the human being remembers God within himself. "Our heart is restless until it rests in you" (Cor nostrum inquietum est, donec requiescat in te).

## Principal Works

- **De Trinitate** (The Trinity, 399–419): the divine Trinity, and the model of the soul.
- **De Civitate Dei** (The City of God, 413–426): political theology.
- **Confessiones** (Confessions, 397–401): the spiritual autobiography.
- **De Doctrina Christiana** (On Christian Doctrine, 397–426): the theory of interpretation.
- **Enchiridion ad Laurentium** (The Handbook, 421): a summary of theology.
- **De Magistro** (The Teacher, 389): language and teaching.
- **De Libero Arbitrio** (On Free Choice of the Will, 387–395): against the Manichaeans.
- **Retractationes** (Retractions, 426): a review of his books.

## Later Augustinian Traditions

- **Medieval Augustinianism**: Anselm of Canterbury, Bonaventure, and the Augustinian Order of friars (formed in 1244), with thinkers such as Giles of Rome.
- **The Reformation**: Luther (himself an Augustinian friar) and Calvin drew heavily on Augustine's doctrines of sin, grace and predestination.
- **Jansenism**: the seventeenth-century movement based on Cornelius Jansen's *Augustinus* (1640).
- **Modern readers**: Joseph Ratzinger (Pope Benedict XVI) wrote on Augustine's theology of the Church, and Hannah Arendt's dissertation was on Augustine's concept of love.

## Later Influence

- **In medieval theology**: it shaped much of Latin theology (Thomas Aquinas, Bonaventure, Duns Scotus).
- **In the Protestant Reformation**: Luther and Calvin drew on it, especially on the questions of "grace" and "justification by faith."
- **In modern philosophy**: Augustine's "if I am mistaken, I am" (*si fallor, sum*, *City of God* XI.26) was compared to Descartes's cogito by contemporaries such as Arnauld, and his analysis of time and inner experience influenced twentieth-century phenomenology (for example Heidegger and Husserl's lectures on time).
- **In contemporary philosophy**: recent political theologians such as Eric Gregory and Charles Mathewes have returned to Augustine's account of love and the two cities.

## The Philosophical Predicament

Augustine has been subject to criticism:
1. **Sexuality**: his account of concupiscence and its transmission of original sin has been criticised as burdening Christian attitudes to sexuality.
2. **The tension between will and grace**: if God chose some for salvation, is divine justice then diminished?
3. **Coercion**: his later defence of state pressure against the Donatists (reading "compel them to come in", Luke 14:23) was used to justify religious coercion, and the early and late Augustine are debated by scholars.
4. **Political theology**: his doctrine of just war and of the two cities has been invoked in later justifications of war and of the Church's authority.
