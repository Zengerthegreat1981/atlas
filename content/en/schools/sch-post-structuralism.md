# Post-Structuralism

Post-structuralism is a philosophical and critical current of French origin that appeared in the 1960s and 1970s. It set out from structuralism (Saussure, Lévi-Strauss) in order to engage it and to dismantle its claim that a closed, fixed structure explains phenomena. It shares with structuralism the view that language and relations precede the subject and meaning, but it holds that structures are unstable and fissured, that meaning is deferred and plural, and that the subject itself is an effect of discourses and powers. The label is Anglo-Saxon and was not used by its own proponents; most of them refused to be described by it. Its boundaries with deconstruction (Derrida's textual practice, often treated as a branch of post-structuralism) and with postmodernism (a wider cultural and philosophical label, associated especially with Lyotard) are drawn differently by different scholars.

## Arab Reception

The Arab world played a role in receiving this thought. Mutaa Safadi and his collaborators translated Foucault's *The Order of Things* into Arabic (1990), and Mutaa Safadi wrote *Critique of Western Reason* (1990) on modernity and what comes after it. Abdullah al-Ghadhami, in *Sin and Atonement: From Structuralism to Anatomical Criticism* (1985), applied the method to modern Arabic poetry. Abdelkebir Khatibi introduced, in *Maghreb pluriel* (1983), the concept of "double critique", which reads the West and the heritage together. Among Arab thinkers this thought engages with the question of the heritage: is "deconstruction" a tool for reading the heritage, or a mode alien to it? Among those working on this is Mohammed Arkoun, who drew on historical studies and French criticism.

## Principal Figures

- **Michel Foucault** (1926–1984): professor at the Collège de France. *Madness and Civilization* (1961), *The Order of Things* (1966), *Discipline and Punish* (1975), *The History of Sexuality* (1976–1984). He analyzed the "episteme": the unconscious cognitive structure that determines what can be said in a given era.
- **Jacques Derrida** (1930–2004): in 1967 he published *Of Grammatology*, *Writing and Difference* and *Speech and Phenomena*. The author of deconstruction and of the critique of the "metaphysics of presence".
- **Gilles Deleuze** (1925–1995): *Difference and Repetition* (1968), and *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) with Félix Guattari. A philosophy of difference, multiplicity and becoming.
- **Félix Guattari** (1930–1992): psychoanalyst and activist, co-author with Deleuze of *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980).
- **Roland Barthes** (1915–1980): moved from structural semiology to "text theory" and wrote *The Pleasure of the Text* (1973).
- **Julia Kristeva** (born 1941): published *Semeiotike* (1969) and coined "intertextuality" (1966), then *Powers of Horror* (1980).
- **Luce Irigaray** (born 1930): wrote *Speculum of the Other Woman* (1974), a critique of the erasure of the female body.
- **Jean-François Lyotard** (1924–1998): *The Postmodern Condition* (1979), in which he expresses doubt about "grand narratives".

## Founding Concepts

- **The death of the author**: for Barthes (1967), the author is not the source of meaning but a place where a fabric of codes meets. Meaning is produced by the reader.
- **The episteme**: for Foucault, the conditions of cognitive discourse in a given period. In the Renaissance, resemblance; in the Classical age, representation; in the modern age, "man" and history. He closed *The Order of Things* by suggesting that "man" is a recent invention that may be erased "like a face drawn in sand at the edge of the sea".
- **Discourse and power**: discourse is a practice that produces its objects (madness, sexuality, illness). For Foucault, power is a diffuse network with no center, and resistance arises along with it.
- **Genealogy**: taken from Nietzsche, it shows that our values and institutions are the product of historical struggles, not of rational development.
- **Deferred difference (différance)**: for Derrida, meaning is generated from differentiation and deferral among signs, not from the presence of an essence.

## Internal Tendencies

- **Analysis of discourse and power**: Foucault and the later Barthes, oriented toward institutions and history.
- **The philosophy of difference and desire**: Deleuze and Guattari, who rethink desire outside Oedipal psychoanalysis.
- **French feminism**: Kristeva, Irigaray and Hélène Cixous ("The Laugh of the Medusa", 1975), who critique the masculine structure of language and the exclusion of the feminine from philosophical discourse and psychoanalysis; in the Anglophone world Judith Butler drew on Foucault and Derrida to argue that gender is performatively constituted.

## Influence

- **Cultural studies and literary criticism**: reading texts as networks.
- **Postcolonial studies**: Edward Said built *Orientalism* (1978) on Foucault's concept of discourse.
- **Gender studies**: Judith Butler wrote *Gender Trouble* (1990), influenced by Foucault and Derrida.
- **History and sociology**: the analysis of "truth", the "body" and the "prison" became fields of research.

## Limits and Criticisms

- **Relativism**: the current is accused that if every discourse is an instrument of power, no standard for critique remains. Foucault replied that critique is possible from a historical position.
- **Obscurity**: critics such as John Searle (who disputed Derrida's reading of J. L. Austin in 1977) complain of unclear argument, and the Sokal affair (1996) was a hoax directed at the misuse of scientific language in some postmodern writing.
- **Subject and action**: Habermas argues in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985) that performative contradictions accompany the critique of reason by reason.
- **Ideology**: Marxists hold that interest in discourse neglects the economic structure.
