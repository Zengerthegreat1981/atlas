# Phenomenology of Religion: Otto, Eliade and Their Legacy

An analytic label for a current in comparative religious studies that extended from Germany and the Netherlands in the early twentieth century to the United States and Britain by its end. It was united by a shared methodological commitment: to study religious experience and religious phenomena with descriptive empathy (epoché, the suspension of value judgment), without reducing them to an external sociological, psychological or economic explanation. It is represented in succession by Rudolf Otto (the holy and the numinous); Gerardus van der Leeuw and William Brede Kristensen in the Netherlands; Joachim Wach (its sociological-institutional dimension); Mircea Eliade (the broad anthropological-comparative extension); and Ninian Smart (the classificatory-pedagogical codification in the English-speaking world). The school also extends into the study of Islam and Sufism in the work of Henry Corbin and Annemarie Schimmel.

## The Common Thread: Non-Reductionism

The central shared thesis is that religion (or "the sacred") is a phenomenon with an essence of its own (sui generis) that cannot be fully understood by reducing it to a social function (as in Durkheim) or to a psychological-defensive mechanism (as in Freud). This does not mean rejecting social and psychological approaches as complementary tools. It insists instead that reducing religion to them alone misses something essential in the religious experience itself. The method borrows from Edmund Husserl the concepts of the "suspension of judgment" (epoché) and "eidetic intuition," though many religious phenomenologists took these as loose borrowings rather than a strict application.

## Phenomenology and Islam: Corbin and Schimmel

Phenomenology was not confined to Christianity and the ancient religions; the Arab-Islamic heritage also has a presence in it. Scholars have compared Otto's concept of the holy, in its two aspects of the awe-inspiring (tremendum) and the attractive (fascinans), with the duality of "majesty and beauty" (*jalal* and *jamal*) in the divine names of God. Annemarie Schimmel, a scholar of Islam and historian of religion, treated the polarity of divine majesty and beauty in *Mystical Dimensions of Islam* (1975). Henry Corbin, a companion of Eliade in the Eranos circle, applied the phenomenological method to Illuminationist wisdom and Sufism. He wrote *Avicenna and the Visionary Recital* (1954), *Creative Imagination in the Sufism of Ibn ʿArabī* (1958) and *Iranian Islam* (1971–1972), proposing that the "imaginal world" (mundus imaginalis) is a real level of being between the sensible and the intellectual, not to be reduced to illusion. In the Arab world, Hassan Hanafi, in his Paris thesis (1966), applied the Husserlian phenomenological method to the religious phenomenon, and re-read the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh) as a science of understanding. The method, however, meets difficulty when it approaches traditions that rest on law and scripture more than on symbol and manifestation.

## From the Individual to the Institutional to the Universal to the Classificatory

An internal development can be traced within this current. Otto, in *The Idea of the Holy* (1917), begins with inward individual experience: the numinous, expressed as the "mysterium tremendum et fascinans" (the awe-inspiring and fascinating mystery). Van der Leeuw, in *Phänomenologie der Religion* (1933; English translation *Religion in Essence and Manifestation*, 1938), classifies religious phenomena (power, sacrifices, sacred places) with empathetic understanding. Wach adds a social-institutional dimension: how the experience takes shape in doctrine, ritual and community. Eliade broadens the scope through comparison across distant cultures in search of general patterns, such as the sacred and the profane, the "hierophany" and the "axis mundi" (*Traité d'histoire des religions*, 1949; English translation *Patterns in Comparative Religion*, 1958). Smart, finally, in *The Religious Experience of Mankind* (1969) and *Dimensions of the Sacred* (1996), codifies all of this in a pedagogical classificatory framework of seven dimensions (doctrinal, mythic, ritual, experiential, ethical, social and material). This framework serves the teaching of religious studies in English-speaking universities, and he rejects the narrow term "religion" in favor of "worldview."

## Later Criticism

This current, especially Eliade's comprehensive comparative method, was subjected to later methodological critique by historians of religion, most prominently Jonathan Z. Smith. They argued that the search for "universal" patterns across cultures neglects the precise historical and power context of each religious tradition, a dispute between "comprehensive comparison" and "contextualism." Donald Wiebe and Russell McCutcheon argued that phenomenology is a disguised theology, one that uses the category "the sacred" to shield religion from critical analysis, and that "empathy" does not guarantee objectivity. Talal Asad, in *Genealogies of Religion* (1993), showed that the concept of "religion" as independent of authority and politics arose in a Western Protestant context and was then generalized. This places the phenomenological "essence" itself in question. Eliade's political leanings in his Romanian youth, and his proximity to the Iron Guard, also provoked debate about the relation between his mythology and his stance. Feminist and postcolonial studies criticized the method for speaking in the name of a general "religious person" and for overlooking differences of gender, class and colonialism.
