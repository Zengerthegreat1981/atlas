# Colonial Anthropology

Colonial anthropology is the body of ethnographic work produced under, and in the service of, European colonial rule, roughly from the early nineteenth century to the middle of the twentieth. It is not a school with a manifesto but a historical formation: the anthropology that existed because empires needed knowledge about the peoples they governed. Its classic texts — the great monographs of the interwar years — were written by scholars who lived in villages under colonial administration, travelled on colonial infrastructure, and often advised colonial officials. Understanding colonial anthropology means understanding both what it achieved as a science and how deeply its questions, categories, and silences were shaped by empire.

## History and Emergence

Anthropology and colonialism grew up together. In the nineteenth century, the expansion of British, French, Dutch, and Belgian empires created both the demand and the opportunity for ethnographic knowledge: administrators needed to know whom they were ruling, missionaries needed to know whom they were converting, and museums needed to display the spoils of conquest. Early work was often frankly administrative — handbooks for district officers, linguistic surveys, and censuses that sorted populations into "tribes" with fixed boundaries. The discipline professionalized in the early twentieth century, above all in Britain, where Bronislaw Malinowski's long fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands (1915–1918) and his *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) established participant observation as the method that would define anthropology.

The interwar years were the golden age of colonial anthropology. In British Africa, the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute (founded 1937) and the colonial governments of the Gold Coast, Nigeria, and Tanganyika funded and hosted fieldwork; in French Africa, the Institut Français d'Afrique Noire played a similar role. Anthropologists studied kinship, law, and politics in societies that were simultaneously being taxed, conscripted, and missionized, and their monographs became the standard accounts of those societies. The relationship was rarely one of simple servitude — many anthropologists were critics of colonial policy — but it was structural: the fieldwork was possible because the empire was there, and the knowledge produced was useful to the empire because it mapped the terrain of rule.

## Key Figures

Bronislaw Malinowski (1884–1942), a Polish-born anthropologist working in Britain, founded modern fieldwork and the functionalist study of institutions, though his own writings largely ignored the colonial context of his Trobriand research. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown (1881–1955) built a comparative science of social structure in the Andaman Islands and later in South Africa, where his students worked within the administrative apparatus of the Union. E. E. Evans-Pritchard (1902–1973) produced the classic studies of the Nuer and the Azande in Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, works of extraordinary ethnographic depth that treated their subjects as if the colonial state did not exist. In France, Marcel Griaule (1898–1956) led the Dakar-Djibouti expedition (1931–1933) and built a school of African ethnography closely tied to the colonial administration. After the Second World War, the critic who did most to expose the connection was Talal Asad (born 1932), whose edited volume *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973) argued that colonial power had shaped the discipline's theories, not just its fieldwork conditions.

## Key Works

The canon of colonial anthropology is the canon of classical ethnography itself. Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) and *Crime and Custom in Savage Society* (1926) set the standard for the functional analysis of institutions. Radcliffe-Brown's *The Andaman Islanders* (1922) and *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952) codified structural-functionalism. Evans-Pritchard's *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande* (1937) and *The Nuer* (1940) remain models of ethnographic reasoning. On the critical side, Asad's *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973) reframed the whole tradition, and Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), though a work of literary and cultural criticism rather than anthropology, gave the critique its widest audience by showing how Western scholarship had constructed the "Orient" as an object of domination. George Stocking's historical studies, notably *Victorian Anthropology* (1987), supplied the archival record of the discipline's imperial entanglements.

## Method

Colonial anthropology's method was intensive, long-term fieldwork in a single community, conducted in the local language, with the ethnographer living among the people studied. That method produced unrivalled depth of knowledge. But it also had characteristic blind spots. The "ethnographic present" froze societies in a timeless past, as if colonialism, wage labor, and mission Christianity were not already transforming them. The village or tribe was treated as a bounded, self-contained unit, and history was bracketed out. Above all, the ethnographer's authority rested on a colonial asymmetry: the anthropologist could leave, publish, and build a career on the encounter, while the people studied could not answer back in the same forums. These were not individual failings but features of the conditions under which the knowledge was produced.

## Influence and Debates

Colonial anthropology's legacy is double. On one side, it left an archive of extraordinary richness — the monographs of the 1920s to the 1950s remain primary sources for the histories of the societies they describe, and their methods of long-term fieldwork still define the discipline. On the other, it left a set of categories — "tribe," "primitive," "customary law" — that colonial states used to govern, and that postcolonial states inherited. The critique launched by Asad and developed by postcolonial anthropology forced the discipline to reckon with its own history: to read the classics against the grain, to write history back into ethnography, and to ask who benefits from anthropological knowledge. The question is not whether the colonial monographs should be discarded — they cannot be — but how to use them honestly, with their conditions of production in full view.
