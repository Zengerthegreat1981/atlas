# Development Anthropology

Development anthropology is the branch of anthropology that studies planned social and economic change: the dams, irrigation schemes, health programs, microcredit projects, and poverty-alleviation plans through which governments and international agencies try to improve people's lives. It is distinct from applied anthropology in its focus. Where applied anthropology covers any use of anthropological knowledge outside the university, development anthropology concentrates on the world of "development" itself — its institutions, its language, and its effects on the people it claims to help. Its founding question is deceptively simple: what actually happens when a development project arrives in a village, and who benefits? The answers anthropologists have given have been uncomfortable ones, and they have made the field one of anthropology's most politically charged branches.

## Origins and History

The field grew out of the postwar development era. After 1945, newly independent states and agencies such as the World Bank and the United States Agency for International Development launched ambitious programs to modernize agriculture, build infrastructure, and reduce poverty in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Anthropologists were hired as consultants, and many discovered that projects designed in distant offices failed because they misunderstood local land tenure, kinship obligations, gender roles, and systems of knowledge. Early work was largely practical: how to design a resettlement scheme that did not destroy a community, or how to introduce a new crop without undermining women's income. By the 1970s a second current had appeared, the anthropology of development, which turned the ethnographic lens on the developers themselves, treating the development industry as a culture with its own rituals, categories, and power. Many researchers move between practical consultancy and that critical stance. In 1976 David Brokensha, Michael Horowitz, and Thayer Scudder founded the Institute for Development Anthropology, which trained and employed anthropologists on aid-funded work. Michael Cernea pressed the World Bank to treat social organization as part of project design and to attach safeguards to involuntary resettlement, an argument set out in the volume *Putting People First* (1985).

The field's critical turn came in the late 1980s and 1990s. James Ferguson's The Anti-Politics Machine (1990), a study of a rural development project in Lesotho (the Thaba-Tseka project, whose funders included the World Bank and the Canadian International Development Agency), showed how a project that largely failed on its own terms nevertheless succeeded in extending state power into rural areas, depoliticizing poverty by treating it as a technical problem. Arturo Escobar's Encountering Development (1995) argued that "development" was not a neutral description of economic progress but a discourse invented after the Second World War that turned whole regions into "underdeveloped" places in need of intervention. Together these works established the idea that development projects must be judged not only by their stated goals but by their unintended effects: new forms of control, new inequalities, and new dependencies. They are sometimes grouped as post-development or postcolonial critique, although the arguments are not the same. David Mosse's *Cultivating Development* (2005), based on his own role in a rural project in western India, argued that policy models often interpret and legitimate what happens on the ground more than they steer it. Field studies also keep returning to ordinary findings that project design misses: a named community contains competing interests, household members do not pool all income, and a refusal that looks irrational may protect food supply or customary rights.

## Key Figures and Works

The field's literature divides between practitioners and critics, and its most important figures often worked in both roles. Thayer Scudder, who with Elizabeth Colson studied the Gwembe Tonga displaced by the Kariba Dam in the 1950s, became a pioneer of the study of forced resettlement, and later wrote extensively on its stages and consequences. Michael Cernea, the World Bank's first sociologist, developed the impoverishment risks model for displaced people and showed that displacement systematically produces landlessness, joblessness, and social disintegration unless each risk is addressed. James Ferguson's The Anti-Politics Machine (1990) and Arturo Escobar's Encountering Development (1995) became the canonical critical texts. Katy Gardner and David Lewis, in Anthropology, Development and the Post-Modern Challenge (1996), tried to hold the two sides together, arguing that anthropologists could criticize development while still engaging with it. More recent work by Tania Murray Li, notably The Will to Improve (2007), has shown how development programs in Indonesia render complex social problems technical so that experts can act on them, and how the people targeted by such programs negotiate, resist, or redirect them.

## Method

Development anthropologists use the classic tools of ethnography — long-term fieldwork, participant observation, and open-ended interviews — but they apply them in unusual places: project offices, donor meetings, and the corridors of ministries, as well as in villages. The method is often multi-sited, following a project from the headquarters where it is designed to the community where it lands, documenting how a policy changes meaning at each step. A central technique is the study of "the project" as a social form: its documents, its budgets, its evaluations, and the categories it uses to classify people as beneficiaries, target groups, or stakeholders. Anthropologists also practice rapid appraisal methods and participatory research, working with communities to map their own resources and priorities. The field's ethical stance is distinctive: the anthropologist is often both employee and critic, paid by the agency whose work she evaluates, and the tension between loyalty and honesty is a permanent feature of the discipline.

## Influence and Legacies

Development anthropology changed development practice from the inside. Cernea's work shaped the World Bank's policies on involuntary resettlement; anthropological research on gender led agencies to recognize that projects aimed at "households" often channelled resources to men and impoverished women; studies of indigenous knowledge showed that local farming and healing systems were not obstacles to progress but resources for it. At the same time, the field's critique reshaped anthropology itself, pushing it toward the study of power, bureaucracy, and global institutions. Today the field addresses climate adaptation, humanitarian aid, post-conflict reconstruction, and the rise of new donors, and it faces a persistent question: can anthropology improve development, or does engagement merely lend legitimacy to a system that should be dismantled? The field has never settled that question, and its vitality comes from the argument itself.

Employment inside an agency adds practical constraints: short timetables, limits on what may be published, and clashes between a funder's aims and residents' wishes. Consent to be interviewed is not consent to the project itself. Participatory exercises can shift real decisions toward residents, or they can ask people to endorse choices already taken. Some practitioners answer the charge of co-optation by pointing to harm avoided and to stronger claims for compensation. Newer topics include cash transfers, microfinance, and Chinese and other South–South programmes, where the older split between donor and recipient fits poorly. Evaluation, on this view, has to ask who defined success and over what stretch of time it was measured.
