# Austrian School of Economics

The **Austrian School** is a tradition in political economy that emerged in Vienna in the late nineteenth century with **Carl Menger**, and rests on a rigorous individualist methodology: all economic phenomena, from prices to economic cycles, must be explained by tracing them back to purposeful individual decisions made under incomplete information and real time, rather than to mathematical equilibrium equations that assume complete prior knowledge. From this starting point the school developed a deep structural critique of both socialist central planning and Keynesian government intervention.

## Origins: Menger and the Principles of Economics

Menger founded the school with his book *Principles of Economics* (1871), which took part in launching the Marginal Revolution alongside Jevons in England and Walras in Switzerland, but which stood apart through a qualitative rather than mathematical methodology: the value of a good is subjective, arising from an individual's assessment of its importance for satisfying his particular needs, not an objective property residing in the good itself as classical theory, based on labour costs, had held.

## Böhm-Bawerk and Wieser: a second generation of foundations

Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk developed a theory of capital and interest in which the productivity of more roundabout methods of production and a preference for present over future goods both enter the explanation of interest. Friedrich von Wieser developed the idea later called opportunity cost and a theory of imputation for factor incomes. In the usual periodisation Menger is the first generation and Wieser and Böhm-Bawerk the second, before Mises and Hayek carried the arguments into debates over socialism and the business cycle.

## Mises and Hayek: from Vienna to the Anglo-Saxon world

Friedrich Hayek moved to the London School of Economics in 1931, and Ludwig von Mises left Vienna for Geneva in 1934 (as Austrian politics darkened) and for the United States in 1940, carrying the school's ideas to Britain and America. Mises developed a radical critique of the feasibility of central socialist planning through what became known as the **economic calculation problem**. Hayek extended this critique to any central attempt to coordinate knowledge dispersed among millions of individuals. The **Austrian business cycle theory**, which links crises to artificial credit expansion by banks, originated with Mises (1912) and was elaborated by Hayek, notably in *Prices and Production* (1931).

## The socialist calculation debate

This critique was the centre of the **socialist calculation debate** that began in the 1920s, when Mises and Hayek confronted socialist economists such as Oskar Lange over whether a central planner could simulate the market computationally without real markets and genuinely competitive prices.

## Academic marginalization in the mid-century

The school suffered relative marginalization in Anglo-Saxon academic circles after the Second World War, as the neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis came to dominate the teaching of macroeconomics. Mises and Hayek continued writing from less central academic positions (New York University in Mises's case), and their direct influence on official institutions declined.

## Revival in the 1970s

The school saw a notable revival in the 1970s, marked by Hayek's share of the 1974 Nobel Prize in Economics (with Gunnar Myrdal) and by a new generation, most prominently Israel Kirzner and Murray Rothbard. Kirzner developed an academic branch, closer to the mainstream, focused on entrepreneurship and the discovery of information. Rothbard developed a radical political branch that joined the economic analysis to a libertarian argument for what he called anarcho-capitalism, rejecting almost any government role in the economy.

## Subjective method and real time

Methodologically, the Austrian School is distinguished by its rejection of formal mathematical modelling and of the assumption of static general equilibrium. It insists that the economy is a dynamic process unfolding in real time through individual entrepreneurial actions that discover profit opportunities previously unknown. This is what set it methodologically apart even from other neoclassical schools that shared its belief in market mechanisms.

## Criticism from the mainstream

Leading economists have criticized the school for rejecting empirical testing and the strict statistical verification that became the standard in modern academic economics. They argue that relying on abstract logical deduction ("praxeology" in Mises's terms) without testable modelling makes it difficult to distinguish the school's claims from pure ideological doctrine.

## Influence on policy and beyond academia

The school's influence went beyond narrow academic circles into political associations such as the Mont Pèlerin Society, which Hayek convened in 1947. Its ideas played a role in shaping the arguments against central planning in post-war debates, including debates in the post-Cold War period about the transition economies of Eastern Europe.

## Internal variation on the state and politics

Although the school's leading figures shared a rejection of central planning, their positions on the legitimate limits of the state varied. Hayek accepted a limited role for the state in providing a basic social safety net and establishing the general legal framework for competition, while Rothbard later rejected almost any role for the state in the economy, advocating what he called "anarcho-capitalism." This variation shows that the "Austrian School" is a broad methodological umbrella rather than a political doctrine uniform in every detail.
