# Latin American Dependency Theory

A Latin American school of thought that arose in response to American modernization theory. It argued that the "underdevelopment" of Latin American countries is not a temporary stage on the road of natural development but a permanent structural product of a relationship of dependence on the advanced industrial economies.

## The Central Idea

Theotonio dos Santos formulated the concept of "dependence" (Dependencia) clearly: the economy of the dependent country is conditioned, in its growth and contraction, by the structure of the economy of another, dominant country to which it is subordinate, so that it cannot develop autonomously, apart from the interests of the center. Fernando Henrique Cardoso (later president of Brazil) and Enzo Faletto, in *Dependency and Development in Latin America* (1969), put forward a more complex formulation, "Dependent Development," which recognizes the possibility of real industrial growth within peripheral countries. Yet this growth itself remains conditioned by the interests of foreign capital and the local elites allied with it, and so produces distorted development that is socially unequal even when positive indicators of economic growth are achieved.

## Historical Context

Dependency theory had an important background in the work of the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLA/CEPAL) from the late 1940s, where the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch developed an early analysis of the "deterioration of the terms of trade" between raw-material exporters in the periphery and exporters of industrial goods in the center. This economic thesis developed into a broader sociological-political theory in the late 1960s. It was shaped by frustration at the failure of "import-substitution industrialization" policies to achieve genuinely independent development despite their wide application in Brazil, Argentina and Mexico, and by the direct influence of critical Marxism and the experience of the Cuban Revolution, which moved the question of whether development independent of world capitalism was possible to the forefront of intellectual and political debate on the continent.

## Main Ideas and Method

Dependency theorists adopted a structural-historical method that rejects abstract generalization in favor of the careful analysis of specific national cases. They shared the assumption that the capitalist world economy is structurally divided into an advanced industrial "center" and a "periphery" that supplies it with raw materials and cheap labor. This division, they held, is not a passing stage on the road of linear development envisaged by American modernization theory (Rostow and others) but a permanent structural relationship that the world capitalist economy continually reproduces for the benefit of the center. Andre Gunder Frank's thesis of the "development of underdevelopment," an early radical formulation holding that real development is blocked so long as the periphery remains integrated in the world capitalist market, contrasts with the more flexible formulation of Cardoso and Faletto on "dependent development." The latter recognizes the possibility of limited real industrialization but holds that it remains conditioned by the interests of foreign capital, the state, and the local bourgeoisie allied with them (a "triple alliance", a term popularised by Peter Evans in *Dependent Development*, 1979, to describe the partnership of the state, multinational capital and local capital in Brazil).

## Key Works and Representatives

Fernando Henrique Cardoso (born 1931), a Brazilian sociologist who later became president of Brazil (1995–2003), represented a rare case of an academic theorist moving from critical dependency theory to applying, in power, market-oriented economic reforms. This contradiction prompted wide debate about the relationship between theory and actual political practice. Theotonio dos Santos (1936–2018), a Brazilian economist, gave in his well-known article "The Structure of Dependence" (1970) the tightest and most concise formulation of the concept. Enzo Faletto, a Chilean sociologist, co-authored with Cardoso their pivotal joint work.

## Impact and Critique

This school offered an early structural critique of modernization theory that preceded Wallerstein chronologically and directly influenced his formulation of the broader world-systems theory, and it remained a basic reference in the sociology of development in Latin America and Africa. It was criticized by liberal economists, who held that the later economic success of the "Asian Tigers" contradicts the structural inevitability of dependence assumed by the more hard-line formulations (especially Frank's). Other Marxist critics held that the school's focus on external relations between center and periphery came at times at the expense of a careful internal class analysis of the relations of production within each country. Despite these criticisms, the concepts of dependency remained strongly present in the analysis of contemporary global inequality and in the field of the international political economy. The approach also travelled beyond Latin America: Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972) and the work of Samir Amin on unequal development drew on related ideas about the structural subordination of the periphery.
