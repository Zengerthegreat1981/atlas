# Deontological Ethics

Deontological ethics is a current in moral philosophy holding that whether an act is right or wrong is determined by the properties of the act itself (whether it conforms to a duty or a correct rule), not by its consequences. This contrasts with utilitarianism and consequentialism, which judge an act by its outcome. The name derives from the Greek *deon* (duty); the term "deontological" entered English moral philosophy through C. D. Broad's *Five Types of Ethical Theory* (1930).

## Intellectual Foundations

- **Kant's categorical imperative**: the founding formulation of modern deontological ethics, set out by Immanuel Kant in *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), usually translated: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." Kant held that ethics is built neither on consequences nor on feeling, but on duty and a single universal rational principle. The categorical imperative binds rational agents regardless of their desires or the consequences. Kant held that an act has moral worth when it is done from duty, from a good will, and not merely in accordance with duty or from inclination. A second formulation requires treating humanity, in oneself and in others, always as an end and never merely as a means; a third presents moral agents as legislating members of a "kingdom of ends."
- **Ross's multiple prima facie duties**: In the twentieth century, the British philosopher William David Ross developed a pluralist alternative to Kant's single-principle formulation. In *The Right and the Good* (1930) he held that there are several "prima facie duties," self-evident in themselves and not derived from one comprehensive principle: fidelity (keeping promises and truthfulness), reparation for wrongs done, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, and non-maleficence (not harming others). These duties are *prima facie* rather than absolute: they bind unless they conflict with a stronger duty in a particular situation. In that case the agent must use judgment to determine the duty that is actually binding in that situation.
- **Other deontological views**: Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) treats rights as "side constraints" on action, and Thomas Nagel and others defend agent-relative constraints, such as a prohibition on intentionally harming the innocent even to prevent greater harm. The doctrine of double effect, associated with Thomas Aquinas, is often invoked to distinguish intended harms from foreseen side effects.

## The Debate with Utilitarianism

The disagreement between deontological ethics and utilitarianism is one of the most central divides in modern moral philosophy. Utilitarianism judges an act by how far it maximizes the overall good or utility. Deontological ethics insists that some acts are wrong in themselves, such as lying or killing an innocent person, even when they produce a better outcome overall. For this reason deontological ethics is closer to protecting the rights of the individual from being sacrificed for the collective benefit.

## Limitations

- The best-known objection to Kant's strict formulation concerns its "formalism." The universalizability test prevents logical contradiction, but it may also permit the generalization of rules that many regard as morally corrupt. A standard example is Kant's essay "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy" (1797), in which he maintains that lying is impermissible even to a murderer who asks where an intended victim is hiding; many Kantians, such as Korsgaard, have argued that his wider theory can accommodate a more moderate view.
- Ross's pluralist model in turn lacks a decisive criterion for ranking conflicting duties. The final judgment is left to practical moral assessment rather than to a strict rule. Some philosophers criticize this as a retreat from the rigor of Kant's original project.
