# Comparative Religious Food and Dietary Rituals Studies

An academic field arising from the intersection of anthropology and sociology with comparative religious studies, which examines how religion organises food practices and eating through rules, rituals and symbols.

## Main Themes

### 1. Dietary Laws

The Torah, the Qur'an, and Hindu and Buddhist texts specify what is permitted to eat and what is forbidden, and how these laws play a role in identity and religious belonging.

### 2. Ritual Meals and Celebrations

Religious meals (the breaking of the fast, feasts, and ritual soups) express the religious event and strengthen the sense of community.

### 3. Fasting

Religious abstention from food or certain types of food as a spiritual practice (Islamic fasting, Christian fasting, Jewish fasting).

### 4. Sacrifice and Religious Slaughter

The use of food (especially animals) in sacred ritual practices.

## Methodology

The field relies on ethnographic field studies, analysis of religious texts (scriptures and legal codes), and the study of contemporary practices and historical transformations of dietary rules.

## Historical Context and Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The Islamic dietary system provides a central body of material for this field. The Qur'an, in the chapters of al-Ma'idah and al-An'am, details the principal prohibitions (carrion, blood, pork, and that over which the name of other than God has been invoked), while the concept of *halal* is built on the principle of original permissibility (the basic rule that things are permitted), in contrast with the more detailed Jewish *kosher* system, which classifies permitted animals according to specific physiological signs (chewing the cud and having cloven hooves). The fast of Ramadan, with its complete abstention from food and drink from dawn to sunset, preceded by the meal of suhur before dawn and broken each evening by a collective iftar, is a prominent research example of how temporary dietary deprivation can be turned into a tool of social solidarity and spiritual deepening; it is academically compared with the Christian Great Lent and the Jewish fast of Yom Kippur.

## Notable Works and Contemporary Issues

Contemporary researchers have discussed how the concepts of halal and kosher have, in recent decades, moved from purely consumer-oriented jurisprudential rules to a global industry with commercial certification and attestation standards, and how Muslims and Jewish immigrants in Western societies negotiate the application of these rules within modern industrial food systems that did not exist at the time of the original legislation (such as the question of mechanised slaughter and chemical additives). The volume edited by Johanna Pink on Muslim consumption cultures is one example for the Islamic case.

## Impact and Critique

This field has shown that religious dietary rules are not merely old health regulations, as some earlier rationalist readings interpreted them (for example Maimonides' health-based explanation of the prohibition of pork in the *Guide of the Perplexed*), but an integrated symbolic system that participates in building religious identity and collective distinction. This is illustrated, for example, by the continued observance of halal and kosher even after the disappearance of the apparent health justifications assumed by some earlier commentators.

## Fasting in Different Religions: A Deeper Comparison

Fasting offers a rich research case for comparison across traditions. Islam imposes a full month of collective daytime abstention from food and drink (Ramadan). Orthodox Christianity observes extended periods of abstention from meat and dairy products, notably Great Lent before Easter, while contemporary Catholic fasting rules are considerably lighter. Judaism practises an intense but short fast (Yom Kippur, 25 consecutive hours). Hinduism prescribes individual fasting patterns, often linked to personal vows or specific lunar days (Ekadashi). This diversity in the duration, intensity and collectivity of fasting reflects different theological conceptions of the function of bodily deprivation: atonement for sin, training in self-control, or symbolic collective solidarity.

## Food and Collective Identity in the Diaspora

The field is also concerned with how modern migration affects religious dietary rules. Muslim and Jewish communities in the West face practical challenges in securing halal or kosher meat in environments not originally designed for these needs. This has led to the emergence of specialised certification and supply chains, and sometimes to legal disputes over the right of schools, prisons and public hospitals in the West to provide meals compatible with these religious rules. This question intersects with wider debates on the limits of cultural and religious pluralism in the modern secular state.

## Ritual Feasts and Seasonal Celebrations

The field also examines ritual feasts linked to specific agricultural or religious seasons: for example, the Eid al-Fitr feast that follows the month of Ramadan as a collective celebration of the end of the period of deprivation; the Jewish Passover (Seder) meal, which includes specific symbolic foods (unleavened bread and bitter herbs) that retell the story of the Exodus from Egypt through the senses and not through speech alone; and American Thanksgiving, which grew out of a religious harvest observance and has become largely a secular national occasion. This shows how a food ritual can retain its festive structure while its religious content changes over time.
