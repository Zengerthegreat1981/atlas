# Hegelianism

A philosophical doctrine founded by Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831), whose core is that **all of reality is an expression of the Absolute Idea in a necessary development** that proceeds through dialectic, that is, through the movement of concepts that negate themselves in order to pass to something richer than themselves. Its peak lies between 1807 (*Phenomenology of Spirit*) and 1831 (Hegel's death), and its influence extended for decades in Germany, Britain, Italy, France, Russia, and America. This page deals with the Hegelian system itself and the history of its worldwide reception, while the pages on the Right and the Left deal with the split within the school, and the page on German Idealism deals with the broader framework.

## Works and System

- ***Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807)**: the journey of consciousness from sense-certainty to absolute knowing, containing the dialectic of master and slave and the unhappy consciousness.
- ***Science of Logic* (1812–1816)**: the categories from being, nothing, and becoming to the Absolute Idea.
- ***Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1817, then 1827 and 1830)**: the condensed presentation of the system in three parts: logic, philosophy of nature, and philosophy of spirit.
- ***Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (1820)**: abstract right and morality, then ethical life (family, civil society, and the state).
- **The lectures** (published after his death from students' notebooks): on the philosophy of history, aesthetics, religion, and the history of philosophy.
- **Editions**: the first collected edition (*Werke*, 1832-45) was prepared by a society of Hegel's friends; the standard modern editions are the Suhrkamp *Werke in zwanzig Bänden* (ed. Moldenhauer and Michel, 1969-71) and the critical *Gesammelte Werke* (Meiner, from 1968). The lectures are partly reconstructed from student transcripts, so their wording is less secure than that of the works Hegel published himself.

## Major Pillars

- **Dialectical logic**: an essence is grasped only through its negation and the negation of the negation. The common triad "thesis, antithesis, synthesis" is not Hegel's expression but Fichte's, transferred to Hegel by Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus in 1837; Hegel himself speaks of the moment of abstract understanding, the negative dialectical moment, and the speculative (positively rational) moment.
- **Philosophy of nature**: nature is "the Idea in the form of otherness," in which the categories appear in material form.
- **Philosophy of spirit**: subjective spirit (individual consciousness), objective spirit (right, morality, the state), and absolute spirit (art, religion, philosophy).
- **History as progress in the consciousness of freedom**: in the *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* he sketches a movement from the Orient, where one is free, through the Greek and Roman world, where some are free, to the modern Germanic world, where all are held to be free.
- **"What is rational is actual, and what is actual is rational"**: the statement from the preface to the *Elements of the Philosophy of Right*, which has been interpreted as a justification of the existing order, while others have interpreted the actual as the genuinely realized, not the merely contingent existent.
- **Recognition**: self-consciousness is realized in mutual recognition, which is the origin of later readings (Kojève and Honneth).

## Diffusion

- **German Hegelianism**: split into right, center, and left, then weakened after 1848 in the face of positivism and Neo-Kantianism.
- **British Hegelianism**: T. H. Green and Bradley (*Appearance and Reality*, 1893), Bosanquet and McTaggart; it was dominant at Oxford in the last quarter of the 19th century.
- **Italian Hegelianism**: Benedetto Croce and Giovanni Gentile in the 20th century.
- **America**: the "St. Louis Hegelians," led by William Torrey Harris.
- **France**: Alexandre Kojève's lectures (1933–1939) on the *Phenomenology of Spirit* influenced Bataille, Lacan, Merleau-Ponty, and Raymond Aron, so that Hegelianism became a gateway to French existentialism.
- **Russia**: Belinsky, Herzen, and Bakunin in the 1830s and 1840s.
- **Contemporary revival**: Charles Taylor (*Hegel*, 1975), and Robert Pippin, Terry Pinkard, and Robert Brandom in a non-metaphysical reading.

## Arab Reception

Arab intellectuals have engaged with Hegel since the mid-twentieth century, and among those who most introduced him to the Arab reader is Imam Abd al-Fattah Imam through his studies and translations. The passages of the *Philosophy of History* in which Hegel classified the Islamic world within the "Oriental world" and described Islam as fanaticism have provoked criticism from Arab and non-Arab scholars who see in them a Eurocentrism, while others hold that Hegel's dialectical logic is a tool for analyzing Arab history itself, which some Arab Marxists have attempted.

## Limits and Criticism

- "Hegelianism" is an umbrella covering contradictory currents, and there is a debate over whether Hegel is a metaphysical "absolute idealist" or a critical, historical thinker who is still alive.
- Kierkegaard criticized his system because it overlooks the real individual, Schopenhauer criticized his style, and Marx criticized his idealism (the dialectic "stands on its head").
- Karl Popper in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) accuses Hegel of glorifying the state, an accusation that scholars answer by saying that the Hegelian state is a state of law, not of despotism.
- The intractability of his language and constructions has led many to see him as deliberately obscure, which necessitates multiple readings.

## Dialectic and the Meaning of "Sublation"

The most important key to the system is the concept of "sublation" (Aufhebung): the transition that cancels a thing, preserves it, and raises it to a higher level at the same time. Becoming sublates being and nothing because it preserves each of them, and the family and civil society are sublated by the concept of the state. In this sense the dialectic is not based on pure antagonism but on a gradual expansion of meaning, which is why Hegel saw philosophy as "its own time comprehended in thought": it does not advance ahead of its time but understands it after it has been realized, like the owl of Minerva, which flies at dusk.
