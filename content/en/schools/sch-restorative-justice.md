# Restorative Justice

## Historical Context

Classical Islamic jurisprudence contains elements that resemble restorative justice within the system of "reconciliation" (*sulh*) and "pardon in retribution" (*al-afw an al-qisas*). Jurists distinguished between the right of God and the right of the individual in crimes against persons, and the Quran gave the heir of the victim a choice between retaliation (*qisas*), pardon, or accepting blood money (*diya*). This is reflected in Quran 2:178, which allows the victim's kin to forgo retaliation, with blood money then to be paid fairly and in good conduct. Jurists elaborated extensively in the chapter on "offenses" (*al-janayat*) on the rules of blood money, reconciliation and pardon, considering that the aim of punishment is not mere revenge but the repair of harm and the restoration of harmony between people. This was reflected in the institution of "reconciliation," led by tribal and clan notables to settle disputes outside the official judicial framework. Ibn al-Qayyim, in *al-Turuq al-Hukmiyya*, also discussed judicial method and the purposes of governance and punishment.

As an institutional concept in its modern sense, restorative justice emerged in the West during the 1970s as a critical reaction to the failure of the traditional penal system to achieve rehabilitation. It drew partly on the customary justice practices of Indigenous peoples in New Zealand and Canada, notably Maori practice in New Zealand (which informed the family group conferences introduced in 1989) and First Nations peacemaking and sentencing circles in Canada.

## Central Ideas

Restorative justice is founded on redefining crime as a violation of human relationships rather than merely a breach of an abstract legal rule. It holds that the victim, the offender and the community should all take part together in determining how to repair the harm, rather than restricting justice to a two-party relationship between the state and the offender. Among its central ideas are also the preference for direct dialogue between the parties through mediation mechanisms and restorative circles, and the achievement of justice through acknowledgment, apology and compensation rather than deprivation of liberty. This intersects substantially with the idea of reconciliation in Islamic jurisprudence, which gives the parties authority to dispose of their private rights even in grave crimes such as intentional homicide. This contrasts with many positive legal systems in which the state monopolizes the right to criminal prosecution.

## Key Thinkers

**Howard Zehr** is among the most prominent theorists of modern restorative justice. In his book *Changing Lenses* he presented a comprehensive theoretical framework that redefines criminal justice from the perspective of needs rather than punishment. **John Braithwaite**, through his theory of "reintegrative shaming," explained how social confrontation can rehabilitate the offender without rejecting them. In the Islamic jurisprudential heritage, **Ibn al-Qayyim al-Jawziyya** wrote on the purposes of governance and punishment, including the relation of justice to the public interest.

## Impact and Application

Restorative justice programs are today widely used in the criminal justice systems of a number of Western countries such as New Zealand, Canada and Australia, especially in juvenile cases and minor offenses. The South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission, established after the end of apartheid, is often discussed alongside restorative approaches, although it was not a restorative justice program in the narrow sense. In the Islamic context, contemporary jurisprudence features a renewed debate about the possibility of employing traditional customary reconciliation mechanisms within the framework of modern official judicial systems, achieving integration between the objectives of Shariah in repairing harm and preserving social brotherhood, and contemporary human rights approaches to victims' rights.

One of the most prominent internal debates in this school concerns the suitability of restorative justice for serious crimes such as domestic violence and sexual assault. Some critics warn that pressuring the victim to reconcile may entrench the imbalance of power between her and the offender. A similar caution exists in Islamic jurisprudence, where jurists require that a pardon must not be the result of coercion or the social weakness of the heir of the victim. Combining the discretionary punishment (*taʿzir*), which the state holds as the authority with general jurisdiction, with the right of individuals to pardon their private right, also raises a precise jurisprudential question about the duality of the public and private right in a single offense. Earlier jurists addressed this problem by distinguishing between the *hadd* punishment for theft (*hadd al-qatʿ*), which is generally not open to pardon once the case has reached the court, and retaliation (*qisas*), which is dropped by the pardon of the heir of the victim. Some contemporary scholars argue that drawing on the mechanisms of Shariah reconciliation can enrich modern restorative justice models with a firm ethical reference that goes beyond the purely procedural character of many Western programs.

It should also be noted that the academic expansion of the concept of restorative justice over the last decades has gone beyond the scope of individual crime to include transitional justice in post-conflict societies, where mechanisms of acknowledgment and collective reconciliation are used to address the legacy of widespread human rights violations. An example is Rwanda after the genocide, through the community-based *gacaca* courts. This trend parallels the discussions of jurists on ending civil strife and on the treatment of rebels (*bughat*), in which reconciliation and amnesty were weighed against the continuation of fighting, an application of the jurisprudential maxim "the greater harm is removed by the lesser harm."
