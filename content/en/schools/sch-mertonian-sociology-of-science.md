# The Sociology of Science and the Academic Institution

Robert Merton's foundational contribution to the study of the scientific community as a social institution with its own internal norms of control, and not merely a neutral cognitive activity detached from its social context.

## Historical Context

Merton wrote his foundational article in 1942, in the midst of the Second World War, when Nazi Germany was promoting an "Aryan science" against a supposed "Jewish science," and the Soviet Union was imposing a "proletarian science" (such as Lysenko's campaign against Mendelian genetics). His article was thus an implicit defense of the independence of liberal democratic science from any direct ideological or political interference by the totalitarian state. It affirmed that a free, independent scientific community urgently needs self-regulating internal norms that protect it from the kind of coercive totalitarian politicization seen in his time.

## The Central Idea

In his 1942 article "A Note on Science and Democracy" (reprinted in 1973 as "The Normative Structure of Science"), Merton identified four institutional norms (the acronym CUDOS, adding "originality", was popularised later by John Ziman) that govern the ideal scientific community. **Communalism**: research findings are common property of the entire scientific community, not the exclusive private property of the individual researcher. **Universalism**: scientific claims are evaluated by objective, impersonal criteria fixed in advance, regardless of the claimant's ethnic, religious or national identity or social standing. **Disinterestedness**: the scientist seeks objective knowledge for its own sake, not for direct personal gain or immediate commercial benefit. **Organized Skepticism**: every scientific claim, whatever its source or the standing of its author, is subjected to rigorous systematic critical scrutiny by peers before being accepted into the shared body of knowledge. Merton also studied "multiple discovery" (for example in "Singletons and Multiples in Scientific Discovery", 1961), building on earlier work by Ogburn and Thomas: the recurrent historical observation that many major scientific discoveries (such as calculus by Newton and Leibniz, or the theory of evolution by Darwin and Wallace) occur independently and almost simultaneously among several researchers who do not know one another. This indicates that science is a cumulative social process governed by the logic of the maturity of the knowledge available at a given historical moment, not merely individual genius isolated from its context. He later formulated the concept of the "Matthew Effect" (1968, named after a verse in the Gospel of Matthew about those who have receiving more) to describe the tendency of the institutional scientific system to grant ever greater recognition, resources and funding to researchers who are already famous, at the expense of less well-known researchers whose actual contribution may be similar or even objectively greater.

## Major Works

Merton's original 1942 article, "A Note on Science and Democracy", was later reprinted under the title "The Normative Structure of Science" in his reference volume *The Sociology of Science: Theoretical and Empirical Investigations* (1973). That volume also included the "Matthew Effect" article and other studies from several decades of work in the field.

## Impact and Critique

This body of work as a whole founded the sociology of science as an independent and flourishing academic subfield for many successive decades. However, the idealized CUDOS norms were later fundamentally criticized by later sociologists, notably Ian Mitroff, whose study of the Apollo moon scientists (1974) found "counter-norms" alongside the Mertonian ones. They were also criticized by sociologists of scientific knowledge (such as David Bloor and the proponents of the "Strong Programme"), who considered Merton's description an idealized, normative one that does not always match actual scientific practice on the ground. In practice there are sharp personal rivalries, deliberate research secrecy, and multiple institutional and funding interests that sometimes clearly conflict with these four ideal norms assumed in theory. This makes them closer to an organizing normative ideal against which actual deviation is measured than an accurate empirical description of the everyday practice of most scientists.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The CUDOS framework offers an interesting lens for analyzing the history of the sciences in classical Islamic civilization. The circles of great Muslim scholars, philosophers and physicians (such as Ibn al-Haytham, Ibn Sina, al-Biruni and al-Khwarizmi) included traditions of critical commentary on earlier authorities (Ibn al-Haytham, for instance, wrote doubts about Ptolemy), which can be compared to organized skepticism, though historians caution against reading modern norms back into medieval practice. Knowledge also moved across Greek, Persian and Indian sources through institutions such as the House of Wisdom in Baghdad and observatories across the wider Islamic world, although the exact role of the House of Wisdom is debated.
