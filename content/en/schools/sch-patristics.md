# Christian Patristics

A Christian theological-philosophical tradition extending from roughly the 2nd century CE to the 8th century CE, covering the writings of the Church **Fathers**: the **Apostolic Fathers** (writers of the late first and early second centuries, some traditionally linked to the apostles), the **Apologists**, and the great theologians of the age of the ecumenical councils. Patristic thought drew on Greek philosophy (Platonic, Stoic, and later Neoplatonic) to articulate Christian doctrine. It is traditionally divided into the **Greek Fathers** (including the Alexandrians, Cappadocians, and Antiochenes), the **Latin Fathers**, and the **Syriac Fathers**.

## Founding Context

In the first centuries the Church was sometimes persecuted (under Nero, Domitian, Decius, Valerian, and Diocletian) and developed its organization and canon. In the 2nd century movements such as Gnosticism, Marcionism, and Montanism challenged emerging teaching, and the Fathers answered by appealing to the **Rule of Faith** (*regula fidei*), the Church's summary of apostolic teaching, together with the scriptures. In Alexandria, Clement and Origen used Greek philosophy as a resource for understanding the faith; Clement described philosophy as a kind of preparatory training for the Greeks.

## Leading Figures

### The Apostolic Fathers (late 1st to 2nd centuries CE)

- **Clement of Rome** (writing c. 96 CE).
- **Ignatius of Antioch** (d. c. 108-110 CE).
- **Polycarp of Smyrna** (c. 69-155 CE).
- **Papias of Hierapolis** (c. 60-130 CE).

### The Apologists (2nd-3rd centuries CE)

- **Justin Martyr** (c. 100-165 CE): the best-known early Christian apologist to engage with Greek philosophy. He held that the divine Logos was partly at work in Greek thinkers, and wrote in his *Second Apology* that whatever was well said by anyone belongs to Christians.
- **Tatian the Syrian** (2nd century): *Address to the Greeks*.
- **Athenagoras of Athens** (fl. c. 177): *Plea for the Christians*.
- **Tertullian of Carthage** (c. 155-c. 220): a Latin writer who was suspicious of philosophy, famously asking "What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?" (*Prescription against Heretics* 7). He is often wrongly quoted as saying "I believe because it is absurd"; his actual wording is that the Son of God's death is credible because it is unfitting (*On the Flesh of Christ* 5).

### The School of Alexandria

- **Clement of Alexandria** (c. 150-c. 215 CE): author of the *Protrepticus*, *Paedagogus*, and *Stromata*, which engage Greek philosophy and Christian teaching.
- **Origen** (c. 185-253/254 CE): a highly influential Greek theologian of the third century, author of *On First Principles* and biblical commentaries; some of his teachings were later condemned.

### The School of Antioch and Syriac Fathers

- **Lucian of Antioch** (d. 312 CE).
- **Diodore of Tarsus**, **Theodore of Mopsuestia**, and **Theodoret of Cyrrhus**: Antiochene exegetes and theologians, who stressed the literal-historical sense of scripture.
- **Aphrahat** (c. 270-345) and **Ephrem the Syrian** (c. 306-373): leading writers of the Syriac tradition.

### The Fathers of the 4th Century

- **Athanasius of Alexandria** (c. 296-373 CE): defender of Nicene teaching against Arius.
- **Basil the Great** (Basil of Caesarea, c. 330-379 CE).
- **Gregory of Nyssa** (c. 335-c. 395 CE).
- **Gregory of Nazianzus** (c. 329-390 CE).
- **John Chrysostom** (c. 349-407 CE).
- **Cyril of Alexandria** (c. 376-444 CE).

### The Fathers of the West

- **Cyprian** (c. 200-258 CE).
- **Ambrose** (c. 339-397 CE).
- **Jerome** (c. 347-420 CE).
- **Augustine** (354-430 CE): the most influential of the Latin Fathers; see sch-augustinianism.
- **Boethius** (c. 480-524 CE): a bridge between late antique philosophy and the medieval West.

### Later Fathers (5th-8th centuries CE)

- **Leo the Great** (Pope Leo I, c. 400-461 CE).
- **Gregory the Great** (c. 540-604 CE): the last of the four great Latin Fathers.
- **Maximus the Confessor** (c. 580-662 CE): a major Byzantine theologian.
- **John of Damascus** (c. 675-749 CE): who summarized Greek patristic theology in *The Fount of Knowledge*.

## Central Concepts

### 1. God as Love

Christian writers, drawing on scripture, spoke of God as love rather than as a remote first principle of the philosophers: the Father eternally begets the Son and the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, and divine action toward the world is understood as love (*agape*).

### 2. The Trinity

At the **Council of Nicaea** (325 CE) the Son was declared "of one substance" (*homoousios*) with the Father against Arius; at **Constantinople** (381 CE) the teaching on the Holy Spirit was completed. The Cappadocian Fathers (Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa) articulated the formula of one essence (*ousia*) in three persons (*hypostaseis*), using terms drawn from Greek philosophical vocabulary.

### 3. The Incarnation

At the **Council of Ephesus** (431 CE) and the **Council of Chalcedon** (451 CE) the Church taught that Christ is one person in two natures, truly God and truly man. Chalcedon was meant as a middle course between positions associated with Nestorius (who stressed the distinction of the natures and objected to calling Mary "Theotokos") and Eutyches (who taught one nature after the union).

### 4. Salvation (Soteriology)

Patristic soteriology speaks of redemption, reconciliation, and deification (*theosis*): Athanasius wrote that God became man so that man might become divine. Different Fathers emphasized different images.

### 5. Original Sin

The idea that all human beings inherit the consequences of Adam's sin was developed above all by Augustine, drawing on earlier writers such as Irenaeus, Tertullian, and Ambrose, and he defended it against Pelagius. Eastern Fathers more often spoke of an inherited mortality and corruption.

### 6. God Beyond Being and Knowledge

The apophatic tradition, in Gregory of Nyssa and later in Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 500), held that God exceeds all human concepts and is known by negation as much as by affirmation.

### 7. Providence

God knows and governs the world. Christian writers defended this against Epicurean denial of providence and against Stoic fatalism.

## The Philosophical Predicament

Patristics has faced several criticisms and debates:
1. **The Hellenization thesis**: Adolf von Harnack argued in the *History of Dogma* (1886-1889) that doctrine became "Hellenized" in this period, which many scholars have disputed.
2. **Orthodoxy and coercion**: critics hold that conciliar definitions and imperial involvement turned diverse early Christian thinking into compulsory orthodoxy.
3. **The role of Greek philosophy**: Orthodox theologians such as Georges Florovsky defended a "Christian Hellenism" as providential, while others emphasize Jewish and Semitic roots and the distinctiveness of Christian thought.
