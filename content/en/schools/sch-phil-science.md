# Philosophy of Science

Philosophy of science is an inquiry into the nature of scientific knowledge: What distinguishes science from other things? How are theories tested? Does science advance cumulatively or through revolutions? Do successful theories describe the world as it is? It took shape as an independent field in the twentieth century with the Vienna Circle, and then branched into major schools: logical positivism, Popper's falsificationism, Kuhn's revolutions, Lakatos' research programmes, Feyerabend's methodological anarchism, and scientific realism and its opponents.

## Roots in the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Arabic-Islamic scholarship addressed several of these questions long before the modern period. Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–1040), in the "Book of Optics," established a method that combines induction, experiment, and mathematical demonstration, made testing and verification of results a condition of knowledge, and criticized Ptolemy in "Doubts Concerning Ptolemy"; Abdelhamid Sabra studied the "Optics of Ibn al-Haytham" and showed its methodological value. Al-Biruni (973–1048) is noted for his comparative method and his emphasis on empirical checking of reports and measurements. Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), in "The Incoherence of the Philosophers," discussed causality and denied rational necessity between cause and effect, and Ibn Rushd replied in "The Incoherence of the Incoherence" in defence of causality. Ibn Khaldun wrote in the "Muqaddima" (1377) on the conditions of the science of civilization and on the critique of reports. In the modern era, Zaki Naguib Mahmoud wrote "Positivist Logic" (1951), transmitting the ideas of the positivists; Mohammed Abed al-Jabri studied "An Introduction to the Philosophy of Sciences"; and Yumna Tarif al-Khouli chronicled the "Philosophy of Science in the Twentieth Century." Shawqi Galal translated Kuhn's "The Structure of Scientific Revolutions" (1992).

## Logical Positivism and After

The Vienna Circle issued the manifesto "The Scientific Conception of the World" (1929) and sought a criterion of meaning based on empirical verification. Hempel, with Oppenheim, set out the "covering law" model of explaining phenomena (1948), and in "Philosophy of Natural Science" (1966) expounded the logical problems of confirmation. Carnap, in "Logical Foundations of Probability" (1950), formulated an attempt to build an inductive logic. And Reichenbach, in "Experience and Prediction" (1938), distinguished the context of discovery from the context of justification.

## The Major Schools

- **Popper (1902–1994)**: in "The Logic of Scientific Discovery" (1934; English translation 1959), he made falsifiability the criterion of scientific status and criticized induction. Science advances by intuition, trial, and criticism.
- **Kuhn (1922–1996)**: in "The Structure of Scientific Revolutions" (1962), he said that science moves between "normal science" under a paradigm and crises that lead to revolutions, and that successive paradigms are not entirely commensurable by a common measure.
- **Lakatos (1922–1974)**: in "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes" (1970), he held that the unit is the research programme, which has a hard core and a protective belt, and is judged to be progressive or degenerating. Where Popper's early falsificationism treats a failed prediction as grounds for rejecting a theory, Lakatos's "sophisticated falsificationism" holds that a programme is abandoned only when a rival programme explains its successes and predicts novel facts; his later papers appeared in *The Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes* (1978).
- **Feyerabend (1924–1994)**: in "Against Method" (1975), he held that any fixed methodological rule contradicts the history of science, and in "Science in a Free Society" (1978) he called for the separation of science from the state.
- **The semantic view of theories**: for Patrick Suppes and Bas van Fraassen (1980), a theory is a family of models, not a set of sentences.

## Realism and Anti-Realism

Realists hold that the success of theories requires belief in their approximate truth; Hilary Putnam formulated the "no miracles" argument (1975), and Richard Boyd developed it into an abductive defence of realism. Van Fraassen, in "The Scientific Image" (1980), offered "constructive empiricism": it suffices that a theory be empirically adequate. And Laudan's argument from the "pessimistic induction" (1981) added that the history of science is full of successful theories that proved false.

## Social and Feminist Perspectives

David Bloor founded the "Strong Programme" in the sociology of scientific knowledge, and set it out in "Knowledge and Social Imagery" (1976), in which truth and falsehood are explained by the same causal method. Evelyn Fox Keller, in "Reflections on Gender and Science" (1985), criticized the masculine images pervading the language of science. Fields of special philosophies of science then appeared, such as philosophy of physics, of biology, and of economics.

## Limits and Criticisms

- The falsifiability criterion became an inconclusive tool, because theories are tested within groups of hypotheses (the Duhem–Quine thesis).
- Kuhn was criticized for the vagueness of the meaning of "paradigm" and on a charge of relativism, which he later softened.
- Social movements accuse traditional philosophy of science of neglecting the role of institutions, values, and power.
- These inquiries do not settle the relation between philosophy of science and its history; Kuhn himself called for linking them, and the connection remained a matter of dispute.
