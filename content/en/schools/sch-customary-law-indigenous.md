# Customary and Indigenous Law

## Historical Context

**Customary and indigenous law** is the system of binding rules arising from the continuous practice of a community and its belief in their binding force, rather than from written legislation issued by a central authority. In Islamic usul al-fiqh, **custom** ('urf) is a recognized subsidiary source of evidence under the maxim "custom is binding" (al-'adah muhakkama), one of the five major legal maxims of later Hanafi and Shafi'i codifications of legal maxims, and a theme developed by jurists such as al-Qarafi in *al-Furuq* and al-Shatibi in *al-Muwafaqat*. This holds provided that custom does not contradict a definitive scriptural text; in that case it is considered "corrupt custom" and is not acted upon. The jurists distinguished between **general custom**, prevailing in all regions, and **special custom**, confined to a group or trade, and between verbal custom (the customary meaning of words) and practical custom (repeated transactions). This distinction largely parallels the modern anthropological distinction between local and regional customs. In the West, academic interest in customary law was established in the early twentieth century with the rise of legal anthropology, and was renewed strongly in the late twentieth century by the **indigenous peoples' rights** movements in Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the Americas. These movements demanded that former colonial states recognize indigenous legal systems as independent sources of right, not merely as "customs" subject to the discretion of positive law.

## Central Ideas

The field rests on a central thesis: **legal legitimacy is not monopolized by the modern state and its written legislation**. Groups produce rules that are effectively binding through stable repetition and an inner sense of obligation. Usul jurists called this regularity (*iṭṭirād*) and required it for the valid invocation of custom. The field distinguishes between **legal pluralism**, meaning the coexistence of multiple legal systems (state, custom, religion) within a single territory, and the demand for **indigenous legal sovereignty**. The latter claims the independence of indigenous peoples' systems from the control of the colonial state from the outset, not merely partial recognition of them.

## Key Thinkers

Among the pioneers of legal anthropology is **Bronisław Malinowski**, whose *Crime and Custom in Savage Society* (1926), based on fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands, argued that "primitive" societies possess complex mechanisms of legal obligation despite the absence of formal courts. **E. Adamson Hoebel**, with Karl Llewellyn in *The Cheyenne Way* (1941), developed a method for studying "trouble cases" as a tool for extracting the customary rule from actual practice. In the field of indigenous peoples' rights, the writings of **James Tully** in *Strange Multiplicity* (1995) and the rulings of the Supreme Court of Canada in *Delgamuukw v. British Columbia* (1997) have served as contemporary references for the study of "Aboriginal title." This is a right grounded in occupation and use prior to the arrival of the colonizer, not in a state grant.

## Impact and Application

This field has influenced the constitutions of several countries (Bolivia, Ecuador, South Africa), which recognize customary or indigenous law to varying degrees, for example in matters of personal status, land and community justice. It should be distinguished from customary international law, a body of rules arising from the practice of states, which Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice lists as a source distinct from treaties. It has contributed to claims for the return of land and for compensation over forced assimilation policies against indigenous peoples in Australia and Canada.

## Criticism

Two criticisms are directed at the legal recognition of custom. The first is that recording custom through the modern judicial system (in writing and codification) may freeze it and strip it of its original flexibility, which rests on continuous development. The second is a tension between protecting the cultural distinctiveness of custom and respecting the rights of individuals within the group (such as women) when custom conflicts with universal human rights standards. This tension parallels the jurists' debate over the limits of the "corrupt custom" that is rejected under Sharia.

## The Relationship of Custom to Competing Theories

Customary law intersects sharply with **natural law jurisprudence**. Both assume a source of obligation independent of state legislation, but they diverge in their grounding. Natural law refers obligation to a universal reason shared by all humans, whereas customary law refers it to the particular historical practice of a specific group. Methodologically, this makes customary law closer to **legal pluralism** than to normative universalism. The field also meets the **TWAIL** (Third World Approaches to International Law) perspective at a common point: both expose how colonial positive law was used to exclude authentic legal systems and to brand them as "backward" or as lacking legal character altogether, paving the way for replacing them with the colonizer's law under the pretext of "civilization."

## Regional Models of Legal Pluralism

Many states have adopted different formulas for reconciling positive and customary law. In South Africa, the Constitution (1996) recognizes customary law (sections 211 and 212), including in matters of marriage, inheritance and land, provided it is consistent with the constitutional Bill of Rights. In India, personal status law is applied according to the individual's religious community (Hindu, Muslim or Christian) in a system known as "personal legal pluralism." In New Zealand, the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) with the Māori peoples underlies the specialized Waitangi Tribunal, established in 1975 to inquire into Māori claims that the Crown has breached the principles of the Treaty, including claims over land. Australia remains the most prominent example of the legal debate over *terra nullius*, the treatment of land as legally "empty" before British colonization. The High Court of Australia rejected this assumption in *Mabo v Queensland (No 2)* (1992), in favor of recognizing the Aboriginal title of the indigenous population.
