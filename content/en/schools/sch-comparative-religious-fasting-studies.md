# Comparative Religious Fasting Studies

A subfield within comparative religious studies that examines the act of voluntary, organized abstention from food as a religious practice that produces social and symbolic meaning: spiritual authority, the marking of the body as sacred, and a means of gendered control. It is not merely a ritual obligation. The field differs from the general study of food and religion (`sch-food-religion-studies`), which is concerned with the rules of permitted and forbidden food as boundaries between communities. This field focuses on the act of abstention itself and the symbolic authority it produces in the fasting body.

## The Founders: Shaw and Bell

**Teresa M. Shaw** (a scholar of early Christianity), in *The Burden of the Flesh: Fasting and Sexuality in Early Christianity* (Fortress Press, 1998), offers a historical study of early Christian patristic writings (the third and fourth centuries). It shows how bodily fasting was linked to the control of sexual desire among Church Fathers, and how the fasting body became a sign of spiritual discipline and moral superiority, especially among female ascetics. **Rudolph M. Bell** (a historian at Rutgers University), in *Holy Anorexia* (University of Chicago Press, 1985), studied the lives of 261 Italian female saints, between the thirteenth and twentieth centuries, who practiced severe fasting that sometimes approached total abstention from food. He interpreted this phenomenon through a comparison with modern anorexia nervosa. This reading provoked a broad methodological debate later about the legitimacy of projecting modern diagnostic categories onto medieval religious practices.

## The Analytical Axis

The two works meet in a shared thesis: fasting is not negative deprivation but a practice that produces power. For Shaw, it produces spiritual and moral authority. For Bell, who found that extreme fasting was sometimes the only means available to medieval women to gain spiritual authority and independence from the family and the official Church, it produces social power and a sacred status for the female ascetic. This axis intersects with the work of Caroline Walker Bynum (`thk-caroline-walker-bynum`, *Holy Feast and Holy Fast*, 1987), which extends the same reading from the individual saint's biography to the collective meaning of food and fasting among medieval women.

Bell's reading, which projects the modern category of anorexia nervosa onto a medieval phenomenon, remains a point of methodological disagreement; Bynum in particular argued that medieval women's fasting must be understood within its own religious meanings.

## The Islamic Branch: Möller and Ramadan in Java

**André Möller** (`thk-andre-moller`) adds an Islamic branch to this field with his study *Ramadan in Java: The Joy and Jihad of Ritual Fast* (2005), an ethnographic field study of Ramadan fasting in Java. His method is ethnographic fieldwork, which differs from the textual and historical method of Shaw and Bell. Yet his thesis meets theirs in reading fasting as a practice that produces collective social meaning (the iftar and the tarawih prayers), not merely as individual deprivation.

## The Jewish Branch: Diamond and Rabbinic Asceticism

**Eliezer Diamond** (`thk-eliezer-diamond`) adds a Jewish branch to this field with his book *Holy Men and Hunger Artists: Fasting and Asceticism in Rabbinic Culture* (2004). It is a textual and historical study of the attitude of rabbinic literature (the Mishnah and the two Talmuds) toward fasting and voluntary bodily asceticism. His method is textual and historical like that of Shaw and Bell, but it examines an entirely different body of texts (rabbinic literature rather than the Christian patristic writings). He examines fasting both as a communal ritual response to crisis and as a voluntary practice of the individual rabbinic holy man, and treats rabbinic asceticism as part of rabbinic culture rather than outside it.

## The Hindu Branch: Pearson and Feminine Vrata Fasting

**Anne Mackenzie Pearson** (`thk-anne-mackenzie-pearson`) adds a Hindu branch to this field with her book *Because It Gives Me Peace of Mind: Ritual Fasts in the Religious Lives of Hindu Women* (1996), an anthropological field study of the fast of the **vrata**, the ritual vow practiced by Hindu women in Varanasi through specific lunar cycles. Her method is ethnographic fieldwork like that of Möller in the Islamic branch, but she adds an explicit gender dimension. She presents fasting as a source of religious agency and well-being for the women who keep it, rather than only as a patriarchal imposition.

## The Buddhist Branch: Prebish and Uposatha

**Charles S. Prebish** (`thk-charles-prebish`) represents a Buddhist branch with his book *Buddhist Monastic Discipline: The Sanskrit Prātimokṣa Sūtras of the Mahāsāṃghikas and Mūlasarvāstivādins* (1975). It is a textual study and translation of the monastic rule texts of two early Buddhist schools, and so differs in method from the ethnographic work of Möller and Pearson. The rule texts frame the **Uposatha**, the fortnightly observance at which monks recite the monastic code and confess infractions. Buddhist monastics traditionally do not eat after midday, and lay devotees who keep the eight precepts on Uposatha days observe the same restriction, so that bodily discipline and communal renewal are closely linked in this tradition.
