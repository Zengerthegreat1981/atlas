# Wallerstein's World-Systems Analysis

The framework of Immanuel Wallerstein (1930–2019), which made the whole "world-system", rather than the individual nation-state, the basic unit of analysis for understanding development and underdevelopment.

## The Central Idea

In *The Modern World-System* (Volume I, 1974), Wallerstein argued that capitalism, since its emergence in sixteenth-century Europe, produced a single interconnected world economy structurally divided into three functional zones: the "core" (Core), the advanced industrial states that monopolize high-value-added production and capital; the "periphery" (Periphery), the states that produce raw materials and cheap labor and are economically exploited for the benefit of the core; and the "semi-periphery" (Semi-Periphery), an intermediate zone combining characteristics of the two categories, which plays a stabilizing role by absorbing tensions between the two poles. In contrast to Parsonian modernization theory, which assumes that each state develops in isolation from the others through successive linear stages, Wallerstein argued that the "underdevelopment" of the periphery and the "development" of the core are inseparable products of a single exploitative relationship within one system, so that neither can be understood in isolation from the other.

## Intellectual Context: From African History to Universal Theory

Immanuel Wallerstein began his academic career as a specialist in the history of colonial Africa and African nationalist movements. This direct field background led him to feel personally the limits of the modernization theory dominant in the 1950s and 1960s (developed by Talcott Parsons and Walt Rostow), which assumed that every "developing" state inevitably follows a single linear path toward industrialization and modernization similar to the West's, ignoring the colonial legacy and the actual relations of economic exploitation between states. Wallerstein was also influenced by the French "Annales" school of history (especially Fernand Braudel and his concept of the "long duration," Longue Durée), in adopting a historical perspective of wide scope spanning centuries instead of focusing on short isolated time segments.

## The Historical Development of the World-System

Across his several volumes (1974, 1980, 1989, 2011), Wallerstein traced the development of the capitalist world-system through successive historical stages: its emergence in western and central Europe in the sixteenth century, tied to the expansion of commercial agriculture and the transatlantic slave trade; its colonial expansion to include the Americas and parts of Asia and Africa; and then the dominance of successive core powers (the "Dutch hegemony," then the "British," then the "American"), each of which organizes the world-system economically and militarily in its era before entering a cycle of decline that makes way for a new hegemony. He argued that these cycles are not random but follow a recurring structural logic tied to the dynamics of capital accumulation at the level of the world-system as a whole rather than of individual national economies.

## The Relationship to Dependency Theory

Wallerstein drew directly on the dependency theory developed by Latin American economists and sociologists (Raúl Prebisch, Andre Gunder Frank) in the 1950s and 1960s, which argued that Latin America's "underdevelopment" is not a stage preceding development but the direct historical product of its exploitative relationship with the industrial capitalist centers. Wallerstein developed this conception into a more comprehensive universal framework that embraces the whole world in a single, integrated system, adding the intermediate "semi-periphery" category, which was not present with the same clarity in the original dependency theory.

## Influence and Criticism

This framework offered a comprehensive structural alternative to the dominant modernization theory, intersecting directly with Latin American dependency theory, which preceded it in time and influenced it. It became a basic analytical tool in the sociology of development and globalization, which Wallerstein later taught at the Fernand Braudel Center he founded at Binghamton University. It was criticized from several angles: by neoclassical economists, who saw in it a neglect of the role of domestic policies and local corruption in explaining underdevelopment, by historians and Marxist scholars (notably Robert Brenner and Theda Skocpol, both in 1977), who argued that the model overemphasizes trade and market relations relative to class relations and state structures within countries, and by others who held that its comprehensive macro model sometimes simplifies the complexity and diversity of local historical paths in favor of a rigid general structure that may not apply with the same precision to every regional context.

## An Additional Note

Wallerstein also served as president of the International Sociological Association (1994–1998), and in the 1990s he chaired the Gulbenkian Commission, whose 1996 report *Open the Social Sciences* addressed the structure of the social sciences. The report questioned the traditional disciplinary boundaries between sociology, economics, history and political science and argued for more historically grounded, interdisciplinary inquiry, in line with his own approach.

Wallerstein and his collaborators founded the journal *Review*, published by the Fernand Braudel Center, as a main platform for publishing world-systems research.

## A Concluding Note

World-systems analysis remains today a living reference in studies of contemporary economic globalization. Researchers use it to analyze the rise of China and East Asia in recent decades, and whether this represents an actual move from the "periphery" to the "core" of the world-system, or a reshaping of the structure of the system itself without a fundamental change in its general hierarchical logic.
