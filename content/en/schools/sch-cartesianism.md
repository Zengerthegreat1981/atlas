# Cartesianism

A philosophical tradition founded by **René Descartes** (1596-1650), the French philosopher and mathematician often called the "father of modern philosophy." Cartesianism marks a turn from Scholastic Aristotelian philosophy to a new philosophy grounded in the method of doubt, the certainty of the thinking self (the *cogito*), and a mechanical account of nature. Descartes also made foundational contributions to mathematics, including the application of algebra to geometry (analytic geometry) in *La Géométrie* (1637), developed in parallel with Pierre de Fermat.

## Context

Descartes was educated by the Jesuits at La Flèche and spent much of his working life in the Dutch Republic. His century saw the Thirty Years' War (ended by the Peace of Westphalia, 1648) and the new science of Galileo, Kepler and later Newton (*Principia*, 1687). Descartes sought to give philosophy the certainty of mathematics and to replace the Scholastic account of nature with one based on matter and motion.

## Major figures

- **René Descartes** (1596-1650): founder.
- **Marin Mersenne** (1588-1648): friar and correspondent who circulated Descartes's work and gathered the *Objections* to the *Meditations*.
- **Pierre Gassendi** (1592-1655): atomist and empiricist, author of the Fifth Set of Objections, a critic rather than a follower.
- **Antoine Arnauld** (1612-1694): Port-Royal theologian, author of the Fourth Set of Objections, which raised the problem of circularity and questions about the soul-body argument.
- **Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia** (1618-1680): correspondent who pressed Descartes in 1643 on how an immaterial mind can move a body.
- **Henricus Regius** (1598-1679) and **Henricus Reneri** (1593-1639): early Dutch followers at Utrecht; Regius later broke with Descartes, who answered him in *Notae in Programma* (1648).
- **Frans Burman** (1628-1679): recorded the 1648 conversation with Descartes.
- **Claude Clerselier** (1614-1684): edited Descartes's letters and posthumous writings.
- **Louis de La Forge** (1632-1666): *Traité de l'esprit de l'homme* (1666).
- **Nicolas Malebranche** (1638-1715): developed occasionalism in *The Search after Truth* (1674-1675).
- **Pierre-Sylvain Régis** (1632-1707) and **Bernard Lamy** (1640-1715): French popularisers of Cartesianism.
- **Blaise Pascal** (1623-1662): a critic of Descartes's confidence in reason.

## Central concepts

### 1. Methodical doubt

In the *Meditationes* (1641) Descartes sets aside whatever can be doubted (the senses, dreaming, the possibility of a deceiving demon) in order to find something indubitable. The doubt is a method, not a settled scepticism.

### 2. The cogito

"I think, therefore I am" (*je pense, donc je suis* in the *Discourse*, 1637; *cogito, ergo sum* in the *Principles*, 1644). In the *Meditations* the formulation is "I am, I exist" whenever it is conceived by the mind. It is the first certainty and the foundation on which Descartes tries to rebuild knowledge.

### 3. Clear and distinct ideas

What is perceived clearly and distinctly is true; Descartes grounds this rule on the existence of a God who is not a deceiver. Critics, beginning with Arnauld, objected that this reasoning is circular (the "Cartesian circle").

### 4. Two substances

Mind (*res cogitans*) is a thinking, unextended substance; body (*res extensa*) is an extended, non-thinking substance. The real distinction between them raises the problem of interaction, which Elisabeth pressed on him.

### 5. Arguments for God

Descartes offers a causal argument from the idea of an infinite being (Meditation III) and an ontological argument from the concept of a supremely perfect being (Meditation V). Kant later criticised the ontological argument in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781).

### 6. Mechanical physics

Matter is identified with extension; physical phenomena are explained by the size, shape and motion of particles, with God conserving the total quantity of motion. Animals are treated as machines. Descartes's vortex physics was later displaced by Newton's.

### 7. The pineal gland

In *The Passions of the Soul* (1649) Descartes proposed that the soul interacts with the body chiefly through the pineal gland. The proposal was criticised from the start as failing to explain interaction.

- *Discours de la méthode* (1637), with *La Dioptrique*, *Les Météores* and *La Géométrie*.
- *Meditationes de prima philosophia* (1641), with the Objections and Replies.
- *Principia philosophiae* (1644).
- *Les Passions de l'âme* (1649).
- *Le Monde* and *L'Homme*, withheld after Galileo's condemnation (1633) and published posthumously (*L'Homme* 1662 in Latin, 1664 in French; *Le Monde* 1664).
- *Regulae ad directionem ingenii* (written c. 1628, published 1701).

## Currents and reception

- **Occasionalism**: Geulincx and Malebranche held that mind and body do not interact directly and that God is the true cause of their apparent correlation.
- **Spinoza**: reworked Cartesian metaphysics into a single substance.
- **Leibniz**: criticised Cartesian physics and proposed monads and pre-established harmony.
- **Materialist heirs**: La Mettrie (*L'Homme machine*, 1747) extended the Cartesian machine model of the body to the whole human being, against Descartes's own dualism.
- **Opposition**: Scholastic and Jesuit schools opposed Descartes; his works were placed on the Index in 1663, and the teaching of Cartesian doctrine was restricted in France and in some Dutch universities at various times. Locke rejected innate ideas.

## Later influence

- Locke, Hume and Kant all wrote in response to Descartes's epistemology.
- The mind-body problem remains a central topic in philosophy of mind, where "Cartesian" often serves as a label for dualist positions and as a target.
- The Cartesian mechanical model of the body shaped early modern physiology and natural philosophy.

## Limits and criticism

- The Cartesian circle and the problem of mind-body interaction were pressed by contemporaries and never resolved to general satisfaction.
- Cartesian physics (the plenum and vortices) was displaced by Newtonian physics in the eighteenth century, and Cartesianism as an academic school largely gave way to Newtonian and Lockean approaches by mid-century.
- It is useful to distinguish Descartes's own philosophy from the varied "Cartesianisms" of his followers, who altered and sometimes abandoned central doctrines.
