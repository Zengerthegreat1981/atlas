# Critical Race Theory (CRT)

An American legal intellectual movement that emerged in the early 1980s as a critical offshoot of **Critical Legal Studies**, shaped by scholars (foremost among them **Derrick Bell**, with Alan Freeman, Richard Delgado, Kimberlé Crenshaw and Mari Matsuda) who held that CLS, despite its radical critique of law's neutrality, **neglected the experience of race** specifically, as an independent axis of analysis not reducible to a general critique of class or formalism.

## A Precedent from Islamic Thought and Siyasa Shar'iyya: Justice Between Text and Social Reality

Islamic political and jurisprudential thought has long been concerned with a question close to CRT's core concern: that formal justice in texts is not sufficient on its own unless the actual conditions of people and the balance of social power are taken into account. Ibn Taymiyya, in *al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya fi Islah al-Ra'i wa al-Ra'iyya* (Governance in Accordance with Sharia for the Reform of the Ruler and the Ruled), holds that the function of the imam is to achieve actual justice, which may require procedural judgments (siyasa shar'iyya) that go beyond the literal text whenever public interest demands it and provided they do not violate a fixed objective of the Sharia. Al-Qarafi distinguishes carefully between the ruler's actions according to public interest and fixed legal rulings, which comes close to, from a distance and from a markedly different normative standpoint, CRT's skepticism about the adequacy of abstract textual reform without addressing the actual balances of power. The essential difference, however, is that this Islamic tradition presupposes a fixed divine normativity (the five universal objectives, *al-maqasid al-khamsa*) by which justice is measured, whereas CRT begins from a structural, secular critique of power itself without any transcendent reference.

## From CLS to CRT: The Point of Departure

CLS arose in the 1970s from a critique, strongly influenced by Marxism, of law's neutrality, focusing on how liberal law serves the interests of the economically dominant class under the cover of formal neutrality. Bell and his colleagues held that this class analysis, despite its partial validity, dissolves the experience of race into a general economic analysis that does not explain the particularity of American racial oppression (slavery, Jim Crow laws, institutional segregation), which cannot be reduced to relations of production alone. This methodological break, CRT's insistence that **race is an independent analytical axis** rather than a branch of a broader class axis, is what distinguished the new movement; the name "critical race theory" was adopted at a workshop held in 1989.

## Central Thesis: Racism Is Structural, Not an Isolated Individual Phenomenon

CRT theorists argue that racism in American society is not a set of scattered individual acts that can be corrected by anti-discrimination laws alone, but a **structural pattern rooted in legal institutions themselves**, including institutions that appear formally neutral. This leads CRT to doubt whether gradual legal reforms (such as civil rights laws) can achieve actual equality unless the structure itself is addressed.

## Bell's Thesis: Interest Convergence

Derrick Bell's best-known theoretical contribution is **interest convergence**: he argues that legal progress for Black Americans (such as the 1954 *Brown v. Board of Education* ruling ending school segregation) does not happen merely because it is morally deserved, but when **the interests of the minority intersect with the interests of the white majority** for another reason (such as concern for America's international image during the Cold War).

## Methodological Tools: Counter-Narrative and Historical Awareness

Other figures from CRT's founding generation developed distinctive methodological tools. **Richard Delgado** advanced the method of **Legal Storytelling** (counter-narrative), holding that personal stories and imagined narratives about minorities' legal experience carry knowledge that abstract legal analysis alone cannot grasp. **Mari Matsuda** applied it in her study of the relationship between hate speech and freedom of expression, proposing that American constitutional law should take the actual experience of victims of racist speech more seriously, rather than relying on a formal neutrality toward speech whatever its content. All these tools share one methodological assumption: that **awareness of the actual history of race in America** (slavery, Jim Crow laws, institutional racial segregation) is a necessary condition for understanding contemporary law correctly, not merely a historical background that can be bypassed by reading the current legal text in isolation from it.

## Intersectionality: A Bridge from Within CRT

**Kimberlé Crenshaw**, one of the leading thinkers of CRT's second generation, developed the concept of **intersectionality**, which examines how axes of discrimination (race, gender, class) intersect to produce experiences that cannot be explained by any single axis alone.

## Criticism of CRT

CRT has faced sharp criticism from several currents. Liberal opponents (such as Randall Kennedy, despite his partial sympathy with it) argue that it weakens the principle of formal equality before the law as a shared goal, replacing it with a group analysis that may entrench racial classification rather than transcend it. Conservative critics (such as Richard Posner) argue that the methodology of "counter-narrative," using personal stories instead of traditional logical argument, lacks rigorous academic standards of verification. Nevertheless, CRT has remained, despite these criticisms and the broad political controversy it provoked in the 2010s over its teaching in American schools, an influential framework in legal, educational and public policy studies.
