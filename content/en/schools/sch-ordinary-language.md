# Ordinary Language Philosophy

Ordinary language philosophy is a current in analytic philosophy that flourished at Oxford and Cambridge between about 1930 and 1970. It held that many philosophical problems arise from the misuse of words or from tearing them from the context of their ordinary use, and that the philosopher's task is to examine the actual use of words carefully before constructing a theory. Its most prominent figures are Gilbert Ryle, J. L. Austin, and Peter Strawson, and it draws on the later Wittgenstein in the *Philosophical Investigations* (1953).

## A Counterpart in the Arabic Tradition: Khabar and Insha'

The Arabic tradition is closely connected to the questions of this current. Arab rhetoricians, grammarians, and jurists distinguished between the "khabar" (statement), which admits of truth and falsity, and the "insha'" (performative utterance), which does not, such as command, prohibition, interrogation, and wishing, and they counted forms such as "I sold," "I married [him to her]," and "I divorced" as insha' utterances that bring the act into existence by the mere act of pronouncing them. This is close to what Austin called "performatives." Al-Sakkaki in *Miftah al-'Ulum* (13th century) and Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani in *Dala'il al-I'jaz* (11th century) offered an analysis of meaning according to context and situation and by the theory of "nazm" (composition), that is, that meaning arises from the arrangement of speech and its relations, not from isolated words. The jurists (usulis) dealt with the difference between assignment (wad') and usage (isti'mal) and the rules by which words indicate rulings. Mas'ud Sahrawi studied this in *Al-Tadawuliyya 'inda al-'Ulama' al-'Arab* (2005), and Taha Abdurrahman built in *Al-Lisan wa-l-Mizan* (1998) a pragmatic reading of logic and speech. This is a convergence of questions and not evidence of direct influence.

## Origins and Figures

The later Wittgenstein was the deepest source: in the *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) he held that the meaning of a word in most cases is its use in the language, that language consists of "language games" tied to forms of life, and that "philosophical problems arise when language goes on holiday." At Cambridge his followers, such as John Wisdom and Norman Malcolm, worked. At Oxford:

- **Gilbert Ryle** (1900–1976): in *The Concept of Mind* (1949) he criticized the Cartesian "ghost in the machine" and showed that describing the mind as a hidden substance is a "category mistake"; mental acts are dispositions and patterns of behavior.
- **J. L. Austin** (1911–1960): in *How to Do Things with Words* (1962, from the Harvard lectures of 1955) he distinguished between constative and performative utterances, then between the locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary act. In "A Plea for Excuses" (1956) he used an analysis of what we say when we make excuses to understand moral responsibility. In *Sense and Sensibilia* (1962) he attacked Ayer's theory of "sense data."
- **P. F. Strawson** (1919–2006): he replied to Russell in "On Referring" (1950), and in *Individuals* (1959) he built a "descriptive metaphysics" that shows the conceptual frameworks with which we think.
- **Paul Grice**: "Meaning" (1957) and, from his 1967 William James Lectures, "Logic and Conversation" (published 1975), in which he set out the theory of conversational implicature.
- **H. L. A. Hart**: applied the method to the philosophy of law in *The Concept of Law* (1961).

Austin used to convene at Oxford a weekly Saturday-morning discussion group in which its members examined words with precision and patience, so the practice became known as "Oxford philosophy," and it was marked by a style of piecemeal analysis of examples instead of building grand theories.

## Austin, Wittgenstein, and Strawson

Austin and the later Wittgenstein share an attention to how words are used, but they differ in aim and style. Wittgenstein's method was therapeutic, aiming to dissolve philosophical confusion, whereas Austin proposed a more systematic, almost scientific, classification of the varieties of speech and action. Strawson's "On Referring" (1950) argued against Russell that referring is something speakers do with expressions on occasions of use; this "use" approach was later challenged by Kripke's critique of descriptivist theories of names, which helped move the field toward systematic semantics.

## Theses

- **Meaning and use**: the meaning of a word is not understood without looking at the way it is used.
- **Conceptual therapy**: many problems "dissolve" when we see how we use words in life.
- **Sensitivity to fine distinctions**: ordinary usage carries distinctions accumulated through long experience, which deserve attention before technical terms are invented.
- **Doing in saying**: speech is a social activity, not merely a description of the world.

## Influence

The current had a great impact on the philosophy of language, mind, action, and law, and Austin's ideas passed to Searle in *Speech Acts* (1969) and to pragmatic linguistics. Stanley Cavell in *Must We Mean What We Say?* (1969) offered a philosophical defense of the method.

## Limits and Criticism

Ernest Gellner attacked it in *Words and Things* (1959) as conservative and as turning philosophy into a description of the habits of the Oxford elite, which provoked wide controversy in the journal *Mind* until Ryle refused to publish a review of the book. Quine criticized the reliance on the distinction between meaning and fact, and others held that ordinary usage may contain inherited philosophical errors. The current declined in the 1970s in favor of systematic theories of semantics, such as those of Davidson and Kripke. Moreover, "ordinary language" varies with languages, communities, and classes, so there is no final authority in any language; hence it seemed to critics that what a philosopher takes to be an "ordinary saying" may reflect the habits of a particular social group.
