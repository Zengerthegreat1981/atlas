# Ethnography of Communication

## Definition

A branch of linguistic anthropology founded by Dell Hymes in the early 1960s with his programmatic essays "The Ethnography of Speaking" (1962) and "Toward Ethnographies of Communication" (1964), which studies the diversity of social and cultural patterns of language use across different speech communities, rather than the abstract structure of language alone as theoretical linguistics does.

## Historical Context

The field arose as a double reaction: on one side against the generative linguistics of Noam Chomsky, which reduced the object of linguistic research to the abstract "grammatical competence" of an idealised, homogeneous speaker; and on the other against traditional linguistic anthropology, which was content to document the grammar, phonology and vocabulary of languages without examining how they are used socially. Dell Hymes (1927–2009), influenced by the Sapir–Whorf tradition and by the interactional sociology of Erving Goffman, formulated an alternative project that treats "speaking" as a culturally organised activity governed by rules that can be studied ethnographically in the same way that rituals or kinship are studied. In the 1970s the field expanded with John Gumperz into the direction of "interactional sociolinguistics", and it later produced intersections with conversation analysis and discourse analysis.

## Main Ideas and Method

Unlike the generative approach, which focuses on "linguistic competence" (the abstract grammatical knowledge of a language), this school studies "communicative competence": the speaker's knowledge of when, how and with whom it is permissible to say what, that is, the socio-cultural rules that govern appropriate language use in different contexts. The school assumes that every language community (a "speech community") has its own norms of communication that deserve systematic ethnographic study as much as the structure of the language itself. Hymes developed a methodological framework known by the acronym SPEAKING for analysing the "speech event" ethnographically. Its letters stand for: Setting/Scene, Participants, Ends (purposes), Act sequence, Key (tone), Instrumentalities (channel and linguistic code), Norms of interaction and interpretation, and Genre. The framework is used to examine how communicative meaning takes shape through the interaction of these elements together within a single speech situation, such as a greeting, a trial or a sermon.

## Major Works and Representatives

Dell Hymes remains the founding name, with his article "On Communicative Competence" (1972) and his earlier article "Toward Ethnographies of Communication" (1964), which laid the methodological foundations of the field. Together with John Gumperz he compiled an influential collection titled *Directions in Sociolinguistics: The Ethnography of Communication* (1972), which included varied field studies of different speech communities around the world. Richard Bauman and Joel Sherzer's edited volume *Explorations in the Ethnography of Speaking* (1974) gathered further case studies and helped establish the approach.

## Impact and Criticism

The current developed out of the tradition of Sapir and Whorf in linguistic anthropology, shifting the focus from the influence of language structure on thought to the study of socio-cultural variation in language use. It has deeply influenced contemporary sociolinguistics, discourse analysis and conversation analysis, and the SPEAKING framework has become a standard teaching tool in sociolinguistics curricula. Critics have argued that the category of "speech community" may presuppose an exaggerated internal homogeneity that does not always reflect the real diversity and inequality within multilingual societies. Later researchers have also noted that the framework was developed mainly from North American case studies, and that it needs further comparative ethnographic testing in non-Western contexts.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The framework connects from a distance with the heritage of classical Arabic rhetoric, especially the science of semantics (*'ilm al-ma'ani*), which grew out of the theory of 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani (d. 471 AH / 1078 CE) and was later systematized by al-Sakkaki, and which was concerned with the correspondence of speech to the requirements of the situation. That is, how the appropriate linguistic style changes according to the social context, the addressee and the purpose. This question is close to the core of Hymes's "communicative competence", although the two methodological and historical frameworks differ markedly. The classical etiquette of disputation (*adab al-bahth wa-l-munazara*) and of gatherings also addressed kinds of "speech event" in the modern sense, such as the etiquette of gatherings, oratory and legal disputation. These established strict social norms for the sequence of speech acts, for who was permitted to intervene, and for the appropriate tone, although these norms were never formulated within a systematic ethnographic theoretical framework like Hymes's. Arabic rhetoric, by dividing discourse into the different requirements of the situation (the speech of kings differs from the speech of common people, and condolence differs from congratulation), offers an independent local formulation of the idea that suiting speech to its social context is a basic condition of eloquence, not a secondary linguistic ornament.
