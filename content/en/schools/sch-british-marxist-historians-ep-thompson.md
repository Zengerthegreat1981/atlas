# British Marxist Historians and E. P. Thompson

The British Marxist Historians Group (originally the Historians' Group of the Communist Party of Great Britain) was formed in 1946 by scholars committed to rewriting British history from the perspective of class struggle and materialist dialectics. Its members included E. P. Thompson, Eric Hobsbawm, Christopher Hill, Rodney Hilton, George Rudé, Victor Kiernan, and Dona Torr. Together, they developed the approach of **"history from below"**, shifting the historical gaze from monarchs, parliaments, and generals to the lived experiences, culture, and resistance of the working class and peasantry.

## The 1956 Break and Socialist Humanism

The Soviet Union's armed suppression of the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 precipitated a profound moral and intellectual crisis within the group. E. P. Thompson, John Saville, and Christopher Hill broke decisively with the Communist Party, protesting the stifling of inner-party democracy and the subordination of historical scholarship to Soviet orthodoxy. 

Following their departure, Thompson and Saville founded the independent socialist journal *The Reasoner* (later *The New Reasoner*), which merged in 1960 with the *Universities and Left Review* to establish the *New Left Review*. This intellectual rebellion gave birth to "Socialist Humanism"—a Marxist tendency that insisted on conscious human agency, moral choice, and cultural traditions, rejecting mechanical economic determinism and crude base-superstructure models.

## The Making of the English Working Class

Thompson's magnum opus, *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963), redefined the concept of social class across international historiography. Thompson rejected the orthodox sociological definition of class as an objective, static category defined solely by income or occupational measurement. Instead, he argued that class is a dynamic historical relationship:
> "Class happens when some men, as a result of common experiences (inherited or shared), feel and articulate the identity of their interests as between themselves, and as against other men whose interests are different from (and usually opposed to) theirs."

Focusing on the period between 1780 and 1832, Thompson argued that artisans, handloom weavers, and laborers actively forged their own class consciousness through popular religious traditions (such as Methodist chapel culture), radical political pamphlets, artisanal clubs, and Luddite machine-breaking. In a celebrated passage of the preface, Thompson defined his historical mission: to rescue the poor stockinger, the Luddite cropper, the "obsolete" handloom weaver, and the utopian artisan from "the enormous condescension of posterity."

## Methodological Innovations: History from Below

The British Marxist historians pioneered innovative research techniques to recover the history of non-elite populations who left few traditional written archives:
- **Interrogating Legal Records:** Historians read court records, assize rolls, prison calendars, and treason trials "against the grain" to identify illicit networks, black markets, and subversive sentiments.
- **Folklore and Popular Culture:** They analyzed street ballads, broadsheets, rioters' slogans, and popular customs, demonstrating that riots were often guided by a coherent "moral economy"—customary notions of fair prices and community justice—rather than blind economic desperation.

## Major Contributions across the Group

- **Christopher Hill and the English Revolution:** Hill reinterpreted the seventeenth-century English Civil War in works like *The World Turned Upside Down* (1972), uncovering the radical democratic and egalitarian visions of marginal groups—such as the Diggers, Levellers, and Ranters—whose revolutionary ideas challenged both the royalist court and parliamentary landowners.
- **Rodney Hilton and Medieval Peasantries:** In *Bond Men Made Free* (1973), Hilton argued that medieval peasants were not passive chattels but active historical agents with clear political goals, such as the abolition of serfdom and commutation of labor services during the Peasants' Revolt of 1381.
- **Eric Hobsbawm and Global Capitalism:** Hobsbawm explored pre-industrial social movements in *Primitive Rebels* (1959) and *Bandits* (1969), before producing his celebrated trilogy on the "long nineteenth century" (*The Age of Revolution*, *The Age of Capital*, and *The Age of Empire*), offering a comprehensive global synthesis of bourgeois transformation and proletarianization.

## The Polemic against Structural Marxism

In 1978, Thompson published *The Poverty of Theory*, launching a fierce polemic against the French structuralist Marxist philosopher Louis Althusser. Thompson attacked Althusser's conceptualization of history as a "process without a subject," governed entirely by self-reproducing structural apparatuses. Thompson vigorously defended the primacy of empirical historical evidence, historical craft, and real human agency against what he regarded as ungrounded theoretical system-building.

## Institutional Legacy and Global Impact

In 1952, members of the group founded *Past & Present*, which grew into one of the world's most prestigious academic history journals, providing an interdisciplinary venue for social history and long-term historical debates. The conceptual models developed by the British Marxist historians—most notably history from below and the moral economy—spread globally, influencing the Indian Subaltern Studies collective (Ranajit Guha, Dipesh Chakrabarty) and social history movements across the Americas, Europe, and Asia.
