# British Marxist Historians and E. P. Thompson

The British Marxist Historians Group (Communist Party Historians Group) was formed in 1946 as the academic wing of the British Communist Party. Its members included Eric Hobsbawm, Christopher Hill, Rodney Hilton, George Rudé, and E. P. Thompson. The group committed itself to rewriting the history of England from the perspective of class struggle, rather than the traditional narrative of kings and constitutions.

## Break with the Party in 1956

The Soviet Union's suppression of the Hungarian uprising in 1956 split the group. Thompson and a number of his colleagues left the Communist Party in protest, but they kept their Marxist commitment to historical analysis, freed from the official Soviet line. Thompson, together with others, founded the journal *The New Reasoner*, and later *New Left Review*. This gave rise to a current known as "Socialist Humanism," which focuses on human agency and consciousness within class analysis, rather than on mechanical economic determinism.

## The Making of the English Working Class

In his most famous work, *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963), Thompson put forward a central thesis: class is not a fixed, objective economic structure measured by income and occupation alone, but something that **"happens"** (something which happens) through a living historical experience, cultural interaction, and a gradually forming self-awareness. In the book he traces how the consciousness of the English working class took shape between 1780 and 1832 through early trade unions, radical religious movements (the Methodists), and popular traditions, rather than being imposed by economic structures alone, apart from the lived cultural experience.

## History from Below

The school developed a method known as **"history from below"**: writing about workers, craftsmen, peasants, and the marginalized as historical agents with their own consciousness and forms of resistance, rather than as passive victims of the major economic transformations driven by aristocrats and statesmen. This method relied on unconventional sources (court records, street songs, clandestine trade union publications) instead of relying on official state documents alone.

## Hobsbawm and Primitive Rebellion

Eric Hobsbawm contributed to this current with works on pre-political forms of popular resistance (his book *Primitive Rebels*, 1959), and later linked the history of the European working class to a global context in his trilogy on the "long nineteenth century" (*The Age of Revolution*, *The Age of Capital*, *The Age of Empire*), offering a broad-scale Marxist history that extends beyond England alone.

## The Dispute with Structural Marxism

Thompson later clashed with a structuralist version of Marxism developed by the French philosopher Louis Althusser, which subjects human action to objective economic structures that predetermine it. Thompson attacked this tendency fiercely in *The Poverty of Theory* (1978), defending the centrality of conscious human experience and historical agency against any structural determinism that empties history of its real agents.

## Global Impact

This school deeply influenced the development of the history of popular classes worldwide, including the Indian school of "Subaltern Studies," which borrowed the concept of resisting popular agency from Thompson and applied it to the context of British colonialism in India.

## Christopher Hill and the English Revolution

Christopher Hill, another member of the same group, contributed a re-reading of the English Revolution of the seventeenth century (*The World Turned Upside Down*, 1972) from a perspective that reveals the marginal radical popular movements (the Diggers and the Ranters) whose demands went beyond the moderate parliamentarians who dominated the traditional narrative of the revolution. He argued that the roots of modern British radical thought extend to these forgotten popular movements, not to the parliamentary elite alone.

## The Institutional Legacy: the Journal *Past & Present*

The group founded the journal *Past & Present* in 1952, which later became one of the most important historical periodicals in the world. It provided a permanent academic platform for publishing research in social history from a perspective that goes beyond traditional political history, thereby ensuring an institutional continuity for this intellectual current that outlived its individual founders.

## Rodney Hilton and the History of Rural Peasants

Rodney Hilton, also a member of the group, extended the logic of class history from the industrial working class to the peasants of medieval England. In his studies of the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, he showed that medieval peasant communities were not passive or wholly subjugated, as traditional history had portrayed them. They possessed class consciousness and a set of defined political demands (abolition of serfdom, reduction of taxes), expressed in organized revolts with a clear political logic, not merely the spontaneous and transient disorder that earlier historians had habitually described them as.
