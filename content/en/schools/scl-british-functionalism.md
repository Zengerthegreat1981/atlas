# British Functionalism

British functionalism is the social anthropology that took shape in Britain from the 1920s and dominated teaching and fieldwork into the middle of the century. It treated a society as a working arrangement of institutions and asked what part a custom, belief, or kinship rule played in keeping that arrangement going. Two programmes shared the name and often disagreed. Bronisław Malinowski explained institutions by the needs they met, including biological needs and the cultural needs derived from them. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown set the individual need aside and analysed social structure: the network of relations among persons and groups, and the contribution of each institution to the continuity of that network.

## Fieldwork as the method

Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922), based on long residence in the Trobriand Islands, became the model of intensive fieldwork. The ethnographer was expected to learn the language, live in the community, and report the ordinary round of life as well as ceremony. That standard distinguished the school from earlier armchair comparison and from expedition surveys that passed quickly through many places. Radcliffe-Brown's own fieldwork, among the Andaman Islanders and later in Australia, was shorter, but his teaching at Cape Town, Sydney, Chicago, Oxford, and elsewhere trained students to look for structural principles that could be compared across cases. The seminar and the monograph, rather than the universal evolutionary sequence, became the normal products of the discipline in Britain.

## What the monographs argued

The classic studies are arguments about order without, or alongside, the state. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) described segmentary lineages in which political allegiance shifts with the scale of a dispute. *African Political Systems* (1940), edited by Meyer Fortes and Evans-Pritchard, compared centralised and uncentralised polities and treated kinship and ritual as political organisation. Fortes's work on the Tallensi linked lineage, ancestorhood, and the life cycle. Malinowski's Trobriand books treated exchange, magic, law, and the family as institutions that meet needs and regulate conflict. The shared habit was synchronic. Societies were described as they functioned at the time of study. History, conquest, and the colonial administration that made much of the research possible were often kept in the background.

## Revision and decline

From the 1950s the programme was reworked by people trained inside it. Edmund Leach's *Political Systems of Highland Burma* (1954) treated social structure as a model people argue over, not as a stable machine. Max Gluckman and the Manchester department studied conflict, ritual rebellion, and situations that cross rural and urban settings. The publication of Malinowski's field diary in 1967 showed a private record sharply at odds with the public image of the sympathetic fieldworker and forced a reckoning with temperament, prejudice, and the conditions of colonial research. Critics also pointed to the thin treatment of women in many early monographs, to the neglect of change, and to the risk of explaining every custom by the contribution it was said to make to stability.

## What remained

By the 1970s, structuralism, Marxism, and interpretive anthropology had displaced functionalism as a general theory. The fieldwork standard did not disappear. Long-term residence, attention to how institutions fit together, and the comparison of political systems without assuming a state remained ordinary tools. Later work has put history and colonial power back into the regions the functionalists described, and has read the old monographs as documents of a particular theoretical moment as well as sources on the societies they studied.
