# Monetarism and the Chicago School

**Monetarism** is a school of macroeconomics associated mainly with the University of Chicago and the economist **Milton Friedman**. It rests on a central hypothesis: changes in the **money supply** are the principal driver of price fluctuations and nominal income in the long run. It holds that fiscal policy in the Keynesian sense is far less effective than the Keynesian school claimed, and that discretionary monetary policy is often a source of instability rather than stability.

## The quantity theory of money: a modern revival

Friedman reformulated the old **quantity theory of money**, whose roots go back to the Spanish School of Salamanca in the sixteenth century and to classical economics, in a modern empirical form. Inflation, in his phrase, is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon": growth in the money supply faster than growth in real output is the main cause of persistent inflation in the long run, regardless of the other aggregate-demand factors that Keynes emphasized.

## The monetary history of the United States: the Depression as a monetary error

In *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960* (1963), written with the economist Anna Schwartz, Friedman presented a detailed empirical argument that reinterpreted the Great Depression itself. On their reading it was not an inherent failure of the market economy, but largely the result of a mistaken monetary policy by the **Federal Reserve**, which allowed a sharp contraction of the money supply instead of expanding it to meet the banking crisis. This alternative reading undermined one of the strongest Keynesian historical arguments for the structural failure of free markets.

## The long-run Phillips curve: no permanent trade-off

Friedman mounted a decisive critique of the traditional Keynesian **Phillips curve** in his presidential address to the American Economic Association (December 1967, published 1968), independently of Edmund Phelps's similar argument, proposing the concept of the **natural rate of unemployment**. Any attempt to push unemployment below this rate through deliberate inflation succeeds only temporarily: individuals' expectations then adjust to the higher inflation, and unemployment returns to its natural rate with permanently higher inflation. In other words, there is no stable trade-off between inflation and unemployment in the long run. The stagflation that followed the oil shock of 1973 was widely taken as consistent with that critique of a stable Phillips curve. The oil shock was also a supply shock, so the episode did not isolate the expectations mechanism by itself, but it did strengthen monetarism's standing against the textbook trade-off.

## A fixed monetary rule instead of discretion

Friedman concluded in policy terms that central banks should commit to a **fixed monetary rule**, a constant and pre-announced growth rate of the money supply, instead of continuous discretionary intervention. Human judgment is prone to error, and lags make discretionary monetary policy a further source of disturbance rather than a tool of stability. The position reflects a deep skepticism about policymakers' ability to "fine-tune" the economy with precision.

## The broader Chicago School: free markets and empirical method

Monetarism was institutionally linked to a broader tradition in the economics department of the University of Chicago. Figures such as Ronald Coase and George Stigler belong to this wider tradition, which is not identical with monetarism. It is generally known for its systematic defense of the efficiency of free markets and its preference for market solutions over direct government regulation in many fields, together with a strict methodological commitment to testing economic hypotheses empirically. This empirical emphasis distinguished Friedman's approach from the purely theoretical abstraction of some rival schools.

## Political influence: Thatcher and Reagan

Monetarist ideas found direct political resonance in the early 1980s. In Britain, Margaret Thatcher's government wrote money-supply targets into the Medium Term Financial Strategy (1980). In the United States, the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker, appointed in 1979 and continued under Ronald Reagan, shifted operating procedures toward tighter control of monetary aggregates in October 1979. The Reagan administration's fiscal programme, built on tax cuts and a larger deficit, was not itself a monetarist rule. The money-target experiments ran into technical difficulties, as the relationship between the targeted aggregates and nominal income proved unstable, and later practice moved toward direct inflation targeting.

## Central bank independence: a lasting institutional demand

The monetarist critique of the Federal Reserve's historical errors helped build the case for shielding central banks from short-term political pressure, although Friedman himself preferred binding rules to independent discretion. The argument is that elected governments tend, for electoral reasons, to favor monetary expansion before elections, even if this harms stability in the long run. The case later influenced institutional reforms around the world that gave many central banks greater operational independence from the executive from the 1980s and 1990s onward.

## Partial retreat in the face of implementation difficulties

Despite monetarism's early theoretical and political success in the early 1980s, several central banks, including the U.S. Federal Reserve itself in the 1980s, gradually abandoned strict, literal targeting of money-supply growth. In practice it became clear that the relationship between monetary aggregates and inflation was less stable than the early theoretical formulation had assumed. This pushed monetary policymakers later toward direct inflation-targeting frameworks rather than fixed money-growth rules.
