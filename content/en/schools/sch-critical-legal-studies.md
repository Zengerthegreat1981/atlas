# Critical Legal Studies (CLS)

An intellectual movement that emerged in American law schools in the late 1970s (its founding conference was held in Madison, Wisconsin, in 1977), regarded as the most radical intellectual heir of **American legal realism**. If the realists doubted the capacity of rules to explain judicial decisions, CLS goes further, claiming that **law as a whole is a tool for concealing relations of political and economic power** behind a facade of formal neutrality.

## A jurisprudential precedent: critique of trickery and control of meaning in usul al-fiqh

Before CLS advanced its claim that law is disguised politics, Islamic jurisprudence had developed critical tools for exposing how formal wording can be used to achieve purposes contrary to the spirit of the Sharia. In his book *al-Furuq*, **al-Qarafi** discussed how rulings change with changing customs and interests while the text remains constant, warning against a jurisprudence that freezes verbal formulation apart from its purpose. More sharply, **Ibn Taymiyya** attacked the use of "legal stratagems" (such as the sale-and-purchase device that renders usury formally permissible) as an evasion of the Lawgiver's intent, carried out in the name of the legal letter itself. This structurally parallels, though the context and aim differ, the claim of CLS theorists that legal formalism may conceal a content contrary to the justice the rule is meant to serve. The essential difference is that the Islamic critique aims to restore the Lawgiver's purpose rather than deny that an objective standard of justice exists at all. CLS, by contrast, doubts even the possibility of such a standard outside relations of power.

## The central thesis: law is politics, not technical neutrality

CLS theorists reject the liberal idea that law is a neutral system of rules that objectively settles disputes. They hold that the legal rule itself **produces and reproduces existing balances of social and economic power**, and that the claim of legal neutrality (Legal Formalism) serves an ideological function: concealing law's political nature from the litigants and judges themselves.

## Leading figures

- **Duncan Kennedy**: Harvard professor, author of the thesis of **"fundamental contradiction"**, see **legal indeterminacy**. Kennedy holds that every legal rule conceals an unresolved tension between two opposing values (such as individual freedom versus collective obligation), and that a judge's choice of a particular solution is not a neutral logical deduction but an ideological decision disguised in technical language.
- **Roberto Unger**: also a Harvard professor. He gave the broadest theoretical formulation of the movement in *The Critical Legal Studies Movement* (article of 1983, published as a book in 1986), and pursued a wider project of "constructive politics" that seeks to imagine alternative legal institutions beyond the impasse between liberalism and classical socialism.
- **Mark Tushnet**: a historian of the movement itself. In his article "Critical Legal Studies: A Political History" (1991) he offered a critical reading of CLS's development from within, documenting how it turned from a collective project into multiple branches.

## The "Trashing" method and internal critique

CLS theorists employed an analytic technique that some called "Trashing": dismantling a particular legal argument to show that it rests on contradictory assumptions, which can be reversed to reach an opposite conclusion with equal logical consistency. The technique was applied intensively to contract and property law, where movement scholars showed that the two classical doctrines (absolute freedom of contract versus protection of the weaker party) coexist within the same body of law without a decisive criterion favoring one of them. The judicial outcome thus becomes hostage to a political choice rather than deductive logic. This approach exposed the movement to a repeated charge of legal nihilism, since its aim seemed to be demolition rather than construction. Its supporters replied that exposing contradiction is a necessary step preceding any genuine reform.

## Impact and later division

The movement divided in the late 1980s into branches that focused on axes adding a more specific identity dimension to the general critique: **critical race theory** (CRT), which focused on CLS's neglect of race in particular, and **feminist legal theory**, which focused on its neglect of gender. Both are regarded as critical continuations of CLS that correct what was seen as its shortcomings. The movement's influence also extended to **Third World Approaches to International Law** (TWAIL), which drew in part on CLS's critical tools to expose the colonial legacy embedded in the structure of international law itself. This showed that the method of doubting law's neutrality went beyond the bounds of domestic American law toward a wider global critique.

The most prominent criticism of CLS from outside the movement is that, despite the power of its deconstructive tools, it did not offer a coherent institutional alternative to replace what it tore down. This prompted some of its pioneers themselves, such as Unger, to make later attempts to formulate a constructive vision rather than confining themselves to critique.
