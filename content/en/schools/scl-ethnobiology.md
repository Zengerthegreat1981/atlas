# Ethnobiology

Ethnobiology is the study of how peoples understand, classify, and use the living world. It asks how different cultures name plants and animals, how they group them into categories, what they know about their behaviour and ecology, and how that knowledge guides hunting, farming, healing, and ritual. Ethnobiology treats folk knowledge of nature not as a crude approximation of science but as a sophisticated system in its own right — one that often encodes ecological information that Western science has only recently recognised.

## History

The roots of ethnobiology lie in nineteenth-century natural history and ethnography, when travellers and missionaries recorded indigenous plant and animal names. The term "ethnobiology" itself first appeared in the United States in 1935, in Edward Castetter's studies of the American Southwest, while the companion terms ethnobotany and ethnozoology were coined in the 1890s. The field's classic foundation, however, is Harold Conklin's *The Relation of Hanunóo Culture to the Plant World* (1954), a study of a Philippine people that documented a plant classification of extraordinary detail and showed that folk taxonomies are systematic, logical, and closely tied to practical life.

The 1960s and 1970s brought the cognitive turn. Brent Berlin, Dennis Breedlove, and Peter Raven's *Principles of Tzeltal Plant Classification* (1974) argued that folk classifications of plants and animals follow universal principles, with ranked categories — unique beginner, life-form, generic, specific, and varietal — that partly parallel scientific taxonomy. Berlin's synthesis, *Ethnobiological Classification* (1992), became the field's central theoretical statement and one of its most debated books, as critics questioned whether folk systems are truly universal or shaped by local ecology and culture.

Ethnobiology was institutionalised in the 1980s. The Society of Ethnobiology was founded in 1978, and its *Journal of Ethnobiology* began publication in 1981, giving the field a journal, a conference, and a professional identity. The International Society of Ethnobiology, founded in 1988, extended the field globally and linked it to conservation and indigenous rights. In the same period, Darrell Posey's work with the Kayapó of Brazil showed that indigenous ecological knowledge could guide the sustainable management of tropical forests, and E. N. Anderson's *Ecologies of the Heart* (1996) argued that traditional knowledge is rooted in emotion and moral attachment to place as much as in practical utility.

## Key Figures and Works

Conklin (1954) set the ethnographic standard, demonstrating that a small-scale society could possess a botanical knowledge more detailed than that of trained scientists. Berlin, Breedlove, and Raven (1974) and Berlin (1992) supplied the field's main theory of folk classification, arguing for universal cognitive principles in the naming of nature. Daniel Clement's "The Historical Foundations of Ethnobiology (1860–1899)" (1998) traced the field's nineteenth-century origins and the early history of its terms.

Roy Ellen's *The Cultural Relations of Classification* (1993) offered an important critique of Berlin's universalism, arguing that classification is shaped by social life, practical needs, and historical change, not only by cognition. Posey's Kayapó research, published through the 1980s, connected ethnobiology to conservation and to the politics of indigenous knowledge. Anderson (1996) brought the field's humanistic dimension to the fore, insisting that ecological knowledge is inseparable from love of place and from the moral order of a community.

## Method

Ethnobiological method combines linguistics, botany, and ethnography. Researchers elicit the names of plants and animals in the local language, record how they are grouped and distinguished, and collect voucher specimens identified by scientific specialists so that folk and scientific classifications can be compared. They document uses — food, medicine, construction, ritual — and the ecological knowledge that accompanies them: where a species grows, when it fruits, which animals eat it, and how it responds to fire or flood.

Free-listing, pile-sorting, and consensus analysis are standard tools for mapping shared knowledge and its variation within a community. Increasingly, research is participatory: communities set the questions, control the data, and decide how their knowledge is used. This reflects a hard lesson of the field's history — that knowledge collected without consent has been used for bioprospecting and commercial exploitation, with little benefit to its holders.

## Influence and Legacy

Ethnobiology transformed the study of classification, showing that folk taxonomies are orderly systems worthy of comparison with scientific ones, and it supplied anthropology with some of its best evidence about human cognition. Its practical influence has been greater still. Documented indigenous knowledge has informed conservation planning, the management of protected areas, and the recognition of indigenous land rights, because it demonstrates long-term, sustainable relationships with particular environments.

The field also reshaped debates about science itself. By showing that peoples without formal science possess detailed, testable ecological knowledge, ethnobiology challenged the monopoly of Western science on the understanding of nature and supported the idea of knowledge pluralism. Its contemporary agenda — biocultural diversity, the protection of traditional knowledge, and the documentation of languages and ecologies that are disappearing together — makes it one of anthropology's most engaged and most urgent fields.
