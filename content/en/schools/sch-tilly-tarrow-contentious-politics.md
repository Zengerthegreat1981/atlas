# Tilly & Tarrow's Contentious Politics & Social Movements

The framework of "contentious politics" developed by the American sociologist Charles Tilly (1929–2008) and the political scientist Sidney Tarrow, with Doug McAdam, which shifted the study of social movements from a focus on the "cause" of protest to the analysis of general dynamic "mechanisms" and "processes" that drive any collective political mobilization.

## The Central Idea

In *From Mobilization to Revolution* (1978), Charles Tilly argued that early theories of social movements (relative deprivation theory, collective behavior theory) erred in interpreting protest as a spontaneous expression of accumulated psychological discontent, proposing instead a "mobilization model" (close to the resource mobilization approach of McCarthy and Zald, 1977): collective action depends on organizational "resources" (leadership, funding, communication networks) and the political opportunities and threats facing a group, not on the intensity of grievance alone. Tilly and Tarrow later developed, in *Dynamics of Contention* (2001), a more general framework that looks for recurrent "mechanisms" (such as "brokerage", the linking of previously isolated groups into a single mobilization network, or "category formation") that appear across entirely different forms of collective action (revolutions, strikes, ordinary social movements), thereby unifying fields of research that had traditionally been studied separately.

## From Relative Deprivation to Resource Mobilization

Before Tilly, the study of social movements was dominated by psychosocial explanations linking protest directly to the accumulation of "relative deprivation" or collective discontent arising from a perceived gap between expectations and reality. Tilly regarded this approach as deficient because it does not explain why many documented cases of intense discontent do not turn into actual collective protest, while strong protest movements sometimes erupt in contexts in which objective discontent does not appear to be at its most intense. He instead emphasized that organization, leadership, funding, and pre-existing networks are the decisive variables explaining when, where, and why latent discontent turns into actual organized collective action. His approach is therefore often grouped with the American resource mobilization and political process tradition, in contrast to the European "new social movement theory," which focuses more on identity and cultural values than on organizational resources.

## Repertoires of Contention

Among Tilly's most important conceptual contributions is the idea of "repertoires of contention": in any given historical era and society, protest actors do not invent their forms of collective action from nothing each time, but draw them from a relatively limited "repertoire" of culturally familiar and historically available forms (the strike, the march, the sit-in, the petition), which they learn from earlier experiences and gradually adapt. Through his historical studies of France and Britain, Tilly argued that these repertoires change relatively slowly, and that each historical era (such as the transition from a local rural society to a national industrial society in the nineteenth century) is accompanied by a radical reshaping of the available forms of protest action (from local bread riots to the nationally organized labor strike), tied to the structure of political and organizational opportunities available in each era.

## Political Opportunities and Mobilization through Networks

Sidney Tarrow, in *Power in Movement* (1994), developed the concept of the "political opportunity structure" (a term introduced by Peter Eisinger in 1973) in greater detail: the relative openness or closure of the political system, the stability or instability of elite alignments, the availability of influential allies within the system, and the state's capacity and propensity for repression are structural factors that shape the opportunities for protest and its prospects, in addition to the strength of grievances. Tarrow also studied the phenomenon of "cycles of contention": escalating waves of protest that spread through imitation and emulation from one social sector to another (as in the protest wave of 1968), before gradually fading as the state absorbs some of the demands, repression intensifies, or the participants' energy is exhausted.

## Impact and Critique

This framework offered general analytical tools that influenced the study of social movements worldwide, across many cases, and the concepts of "political opportunities" and "repertoires of contention" became among the most widely used tools in the study of social movements and comparative politics. However, critics regarded this framework, despite its explanatory power, as at times inclined toward a general classificatory formulation that could be applied retroactively to almost any case (the danger of the "empty all-encompassing explanation"), questioning its actual predictive capacity compared with its descriptive power after events have occurred.
