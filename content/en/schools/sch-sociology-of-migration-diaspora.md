# Sociology of Migration, Asylum & Diaspora

A field that studies the integration of immigrants into receiving societies through differentiated paths rather than a single unified one, and analyzes the "diaspora" as a social formation that maintains its ties to the homeland across borders.

## The Central Idea

Alejandro Portes and his colleagues developed the theory of "segmented assimilation", building on the empirical portrait of post-1965 immigration in Portes and Rumbaut's *Immigrant America* (1990) and stating the theory in Portes and Zhou's 1993 article "The New Second Generation" (later elaborated in Portes and Rumbaut's *Legacies*, 2001). Contrary to the classical model, which assumed a single linear path of assimilation from the culture of origin toward the mainstream of the host society, it argued that the children of contemporary immigrants integrate into different "segments" of the host society. Some enter the white middle class; others, especially those facing discrimination and living in poor urban neighborhoods, risk assimilating "downward" into an impoverished underclass; a third path combines economic advancement with the deliberate preservation of the ethnic community's values and ties. Outcomes depend on factors such as the family's human capital, the family and community structure, and the context of reception (hostile, indifferent or welcoming).

William Safran, in "Diasporas in Modern Societies: Myths of Homeland and Return" (1991), proposed defining characteristics for the concept of "diaspora": a community dispersed from an original center to two or more peripheral places; it retains a collective memory, vision or myth about the homeland; it believes it is not, and perhaps cannot be, fully accepted by the host society; it regards the ancestral homeland as its true home to which it or its descendants might eventually return; it is committed to the maintenance or restoration of that homeland; and it continues to relate to the homeland in ways that shape its identity and solidarity.

## Historical Context

This field developed directly out of the profound American demographic transformations brought about by the Hart-Celler Immigration Act (1965), which abolished the old discriminatory system of ethnic quotas (which favored immigrants from northern and western Europe) and opened the door to large waves of immigration from Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East, radically changing the ethnic and demographic composition of American society as a whole within a relatively few decades. Alejandro Portes, born in Havana in 1944, became a prominent professor in the United States, teaching at universities including Johns Hopkins and Princeton. He studied Cuban and Mexican immigration, among other cases, combining fieldwork and interviews with quantitative analysis of immigrants' economic mobility and, with Rubén Rumbaut, of the second generation.

## Further Key Concepts

Portes wrote on "ethnic social capital": how cohesive ethnic networks (such as the Cuban community in Miami) can provide informal loans, job opportunities and support that facilitate economic integration for their members. The concept of the "ethnic enclave economy" was developed by Kenneth Wilson and Alejandro Portes in a 1980 article on Cuban immigrants in Miami, and was applied by Min Zhou, in *Chinatown: The Socioeconomic Potential of an Urban Enclave* (1992), to New York's Chinatown; it describes concentrations of immigrant-owned firms that employ co-ethnics and offer a path to economic mobility alongside the broader economy of the host society. Safran, a scholar of comparative politics, drew on a comparison with the Jewish diaspora, the classic reference case of the concept, and other cases such as the Armenian, Palestinian and Chinese diasporas.

## Critique

Some researchers criticized segmented assimilation theory on the grounds that it is difficult to predict in advance which path the children of any particular immigrant group will follow, and that its threefold classification (upward assimilation, downward assimilation, selective retention of ethnic identity) may oversimplify a far wider individual diversity within the same ethnic group, a diversity that is hard to study quantitatively with sufficient precision. Safran's criteria, modeled largely on the Jewish case, have also been debated: later scholars argued that they are too restrictive for many modern dispersed populations that do not orient toward return to a single homeland, and proposed looser definitions of diaspora.

## Impact

These two frameworks provided fundamental analytical tools that have been influential in contemporary sociology of international migration, helping to explain the considerable and tangible variation in the integration paths of different immigrant groups within the same host society. Segmented assimilation theory in particular has become a standard reference in research on second-generation immigrants, and Safran's article is among the most cited starting points in the diaspora literature.
