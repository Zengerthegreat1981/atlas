# Classical Political Economy

**Classical political economy** is the label historians of economic thought apply to the school that gave the study of wealth, growth and distribution a connected theoretical form. It ran from the publication of **Adam Smith**'s *The Wealth of Nations* in 1776 until the late nineteenth century, when the Marginal Revolution began replacing its theory of value. Smith, Ricardo, Say, Malthus and later John Stuart Mill did not separate the subject from moral and political questions; Smith held a chair of moral philosophy, and Mill's *Principles of Political Economy* (1848) still treated distribution as open to institutional choice. They shared one central question: **what determines the wealth of nations and its growth, and how is that wealth distributed among the three great classes: landowners, owners of capital, and workers?**

## From mercantilism to an independent science

The school arose in opposition to **mercantilism**, which tied a nation's wealth to the accumulation of precious metals through a trade surplus protected by the state with tariffs and monopolies. Smith turned this conception on its head: true wealth is not a hoard of gold but the **productive capacity** of a society, and this capacity grows through the **division of labour** and free exchange rather than through the accumulation of metal. This methodological shift is why 1776 is traditionally taken as the starting point for the history of modern economics.

## Value and distribution: the shared question

The founders were united by the question of **the theory of value**: what determines the natural price of a commodity? Smith proposed that labour was the measure of value in primitive societies, but in complex capitalist societies he retreated to a theory in which wages, rent and profit together form the components of the natural price. Ricardo later developed this question toward a more rigorous theory of labour value, while Malthus put forward his theory of population as a challenge to Smith's optimism about steady growth. Marx would later turn this same labour theory of value into a tool for criticizing capitalism itself, and this is regarded as the most radical branch to grow out of the school's trunk.

## The invisible hand and automatic equilibrium

In *The Wealth of Nations*, Smith introduced the image of the **invisible hand**: when each individual pursues his own interest within a competitive free market, the sum of these individual efforts tends — without deliberate central planning — toward a socially beneficial outcome. Jean-Baptiste Say completed this optimism by formulating **Say's law of markets**: supply creates its own demand, so a persistent general surplus of production cannot occur. This proposition would later become a principal target of Keynes's critique in the twentieth century.

## An early Arab parallel: Ibn Khaldun and the division of labour

Some historians of economic thought have noted a resemblance between Smith's idea of the division of labour as a source of wealth and what **Ibn Khaldun** put forward in the *Muqaddimah* (1377): that human labour is the actual source of gain and of civilization (*ʿumrān*), and that individuals cooperating on specialized functions raise their combined output above what each would produce working alone. This resemblance is usually cited as an independent intellectual precedent rather than as a direct, documented influence on Smith, since there is no documented evidence that Smith read the *Muqaddimah*.

## Decline before the Marginal Revolution

By 1870, Jevons, Menger and Walras had begun replacing the concept of labour value with the concept of **marginal utility**, shifting the focus of analysis from labour and classes to the decisions of the individual consumer. Yet the school's legacy — particularly Ricardo's theory of comparative advantage and its theory of class distribution — remained a basic reference on which neoclassical economics was built on one side and Marxist critique on the other; both resumed the same question from different angles.

## Methodology: natural laws and economic liberty

Thinkers of this school shared a deep methodological assumption, partly inherited from the French Physiocrats: that the economy is governed by **natural laws** similar to the laws of physics, discoverable through observation and logical inference, and that the best economic policy is the one that lets these natural laws operate with the least possible direct government intervention. This is the principle of *laissez-faire* (from the French *laissez faire, laissez passer*, "let do, let pass"), a phrase associated with the Physiocrats, not coined by Smith. It became a shorthand for one strand of the school, though members differed on how far it should go: Malthus defended the Corn Laws for part of his career, and Mill accepted a wider list of exceptions than the popular slogan suggests.

## The role of the state in classical thought: not a complete withdrawal

It is important to correct the claim that the classical call for economic freedom was a call for the complete absence of the state. Smith himself devoted lengthy sections of *The Wealth of Nations* to identifying necessary functions of the state that a free market cannot provide efficiently: national defence, the administration of justice through an independent judicial system, and the provision of public infrastructure (such as roads and basic education) that has high social benefit but is not commercially profitable enough to attract private investment. This makes the common image of Smith as the "father of the absolutely free market" a simplification that goes beyond the complexity of his actual position in the original text.

## Internal debate over labour value and utility

The classical school was not internally uniform in its views: its members differed on the question of value — is labour alone the source of value, or do scarcity and utility enter alongside it? Smith remained torn between several explanations, Ricardo tried to settle the matter in favour of labour with greater rigour, and Jean-Baptiste Say moved toward emphasizing utility as a parallel source of value. This internal disagreement paved the way for the school's later split between a neoclassical heir built on marginal utility and a Marxist heir that pushed the labour theory of value to its logical limits.
