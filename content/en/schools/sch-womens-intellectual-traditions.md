# Arab Women's Intellectual Traditions

An umbrella that gathers women's intellectual, spiritual and scholarly contributions to Arab and Islamic thought across the ages, from the early women mystics of the eighth century CE to the feminist Qur'anic interpreters of the twentieth century, passing through the women scholars who taught and transmitted knowledge in hadith and jurisprudence throughout medieval Islamic history.

## Key figures and trajectories

- **Rabi'a al-Adawiyya** (traditional dates c. 95–185 AH / c. 714–801 CE): the woman ascetic of Basra most often named in later Sufi writing. That writing credits her with teaching divine love (*mahabba*) as worship for its own sake, rather than from fear of punishment or hope of reward. Disinterested love became a recurring theme in later Sufi literature, including in Rumi. Almost everything told of her comes from hagiography written long after her death.
- **Women scholars in the medieval period**: Ibn Asakir (d. 571 AH / 1176 CE), in *Tarikh Dimashq*, included many women who studied, received an *ijaza* and authorized students, especially in hadith. A separate and later tradition, set down by Ibn Abi Zar' in *Rawd al-Qirtas* in the early fourteenth century, attributes to Fatima al-Fihri the founding of the Qarawiyyin mosque in Fez in Ramadan 245 AH / 859 CE. The mosque became an important place of teaching. Calling the foundation of 859 a university uses a later category, and the report itself is not contemporary.
- **Women poets in the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods**: women composed poetry and public speech before and after Islam. al-Khansa' is remembered for the elegies on her brothers. Hind bint Utba is remembered for speech and verse on both sides of her conversion.
- **Aisha Abd al-Rahman (Bint al-Shati')** (1913–1998): an Egyptian professor of Arabic and Qur'anic studies. In *al-Tafsir al-Bayani lil-Qur'an al-Karim* (Cairo: Dar al-Ma'arif, 1962–1969) she read selected suras through Arabic rhetoric and literary context.
- **Feminist readings of scripture and hadith**: Fatima Mernissi's *The Veil and the Male Elite* (French, 1987; English, 1991) studies hadiths used to restrict women and argues that several were weak or politically situated. It is not primarily a verse-by-verse rereading of the Qur'an. Amina Wadud's *Qur'an and Woman* (Kuala Lumpur, 1992; New York: Oxford University Press, 1999) does reread the Qur'an, and it locates patriarchal conclusions in the history of interpretation.

## Learning in scholarly circles

Muhammad Akram al-Nadwi, in *al-Muhaddithat*, reports thousands of women hadith scholars across the centuries, many of whom authorized male students. The count belongs to his biographical project and should not be treated as a census. One figure remembered in the Hanafi tradition is Fatima bint Muhammad al-Samarqandi, daughter of the jurist Ala' al-Din al-Samarqandi, who in the sixth century AH is said to have joined legal learning to the giving of fatwas. Al-Sakhawi (d. 902 AH / 1497 CE) gave the women of the ninth century AH a section of *al-Daw' al-Lami'*, more than a thousand biographies. Women's learning is a documented part of the biographical record. The record usually preserves a name in a chain of transmission, not a body of writing.

## The contemporary debate

Leila Ahmed, in *Women and Gender in Islam* (1992), argued that Abbasid legal discourse drew on wider Mediterranean patriarchal institutions, and that the Qur'an's ethical voice is more egalitarian than that later law. Critics have objected to the breadth of the contrast. Asma Barlas, in *Believing Women in Islam* (2002), argued for a non-patriarchal reading of the Qur'an. She writes as a Pakistani-American scholar of the text, not as a historian of the medieval isnad.

## Beyond the famous names

Research should not be limited to mystics and female exegetes. Women also had a presence in transmission of narrations, trade, endowment (*waqf*) and education, including endowments by women of ruling families to schools and *ribats* in Damascus and Aleppo during the Ayyubid and Mamluk periods. Biographical sources remain of differing purpose: they include women in chains of transmission (*isnad*) without recording their full thought.

## The common thread

What unites these traditions, distant in time, is one recurring question: how a woman can be an intellectual, spiritual and scholarly agent of full legitimacy within traditions that later history has often read through a male lens, whether through direct achievement (such as mysticism, teaching and scholarly authorization) or through the critical rereading of that history itself.

## Caveats

- The historical documentation of many of these figures (especially in the early medieval Islamic centuries) is indirect. It relies on biographical sources that recorded their lives within general collections of biographies rather than in independent works.
- This is an umbrella covering multiple fields that span more than twelve centuries; it is not a single homogeneous school.
