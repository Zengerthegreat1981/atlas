# International Law Theory

## Historical Context

**International law theory** examines the source of obligation of the rules that govern relations between states, and their nature. This question provoked philosophical debate from the earliest days of Islamic jurisprudence through the field of **siyar** (the jurisprudence of international relations in war and peace), which was systematized by Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani (d. 189 AH / 805 CE) in *al-Siyar al-Saghir* and *al-Siyar al-Kabir*. It addressed the rules of treaties (covenants and agreements), the inviolability of envoys and ambassadors, rules of safe conduct (individual and collective), and the limits on the use of force. Its starting point was that relations between the abode of Islam and other lands were governed by binding sharia rulings, not merely by bare political interest. In Western jurisprudence, **Francisco de Vitoria** in the sixteenth century, and then **Hugo Grotius** in *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* (1625), laid the foundations of what was later called the "law of nations" (natural law of nations). The field then moved, with the rise of the Westphalian state (1648), into a consensual system among equal sovereigns, and after the two World Wars into multilateral institutions (the League of Nations, then the United Nations Charter of 1945).

## Central Ideas

International law theory revolves around one fundamental question: **How does a law bind states that have no superior authority above them to enforce it by force?** The **natural law** school answers that obligation derives from universal principles of justice that precede the will of states. (This corresponds, in Islamic jurisprudence, to the fact that the rules of siyar are part of sharia, which binds regardless of the consent of the other party.) The **positivist** school, as represented by John Austin, answers that international law hardly deserves the name "law", given the absence of a central legislator and central sanction. The **consent-based (voluntarist)** school answers that the source of obligation is the consent of the states themselves, expressed through treaty and international custom. The **realist** school in international relations added a critical dimension by arguing that international law is a legal cover for actual balances of power, while the **constructivist** school later developed the idea that international norms are shaped through the interaction of shared identities and standards among states, not through material interest alone.

## Leading Thinkers

**Hugo Grotius** is regarded in the dominant Western narrative as the father of modern international law, although some of the questions he treated, such as the conduct of war and the protection of envoys, had been addressed centuries earlier in the Islamic jurisprudence of siyar. **Hans Kelsen** developed a rigorous positivist theory that subjects international and domestic law to a single normative hierarchy (the Grundnorm). **Hersch Lauterpacht** offered an intermediate view that combines positivism with a moral commitment to individual rights above state sovereignty. In recent decades, **Martti Koskenniemi** has become prominent for his structural critique of the contradictions in international legal discourse between formal legitimacy and actual justice, in his book *From Apology to Utopia* (1989). The **TWAIL** (Third World Approaches to International Law) school, with Antony Anghie among its prominent figures, has also emerged, exposing the colonial roots of many international law institutions that appear "neutral".

## Impact and Application

These theories shaped the interpretive framework of the work of the International Court of Justice, the Security Council, and regional human rights courts. They also influenced the codification of the law of treaties in the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969). They also formed a basis for the development of international criminal law after the Nuremberg and Tokyo trials, leading to the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court (adopted 1998; in force 2002).

## Critique

International law faces a dual critique. Realists argue that it is incapable of effectively restraining great powers when their interests conflict with it. The TWAIL school argues that even when it is applied, it often reflects the balances of old colonial power more than it achieves universal equality of justice between North and South.

## Siyar Compared with Grotius: Methodological Priority

A distinction deserves particular attention here. Whereas Grotius begins from the abstract rational idea of "natural law", which states are presumed to be bound by even if God did not exist (his famous hypothesis *etiamsi daremus*), the jurisprudence of siyar in al-Shaybani and al-Sarakhsi begins from the premise that the rules of international relations are part of detailed revealed sharia. In the Islamic framework, this gives them a normative coherence that does not require the separate rational justification that Grotius needed to justify an international law in post-Reformation Europe, where the single church authority had fragmented. Siyar also treated in detail matters that were codified in the West only much later, such as the prohibition on killing non-combatants, the prohibition on mutilating corpses, and the protection of the safe conduct granted to a foreign merchant (*musta'man*). These rules parallel in important respects what was later established in the Geneva Conventions for the protection of war victims (1949).

## The Tension between Sovereignty and Supranational Institutions

The deepest tension in contemporary international law theory is between the principle of absolute **state sovereignty** inherited from the Westphalian settlement, and the growth of supranational institutions (the Security Council, the International Criminal Court, the World Trade Organization) that hold powers which sometimes exceed the will of an individual state. Proponents of **global constitutionalism** see this development as a necessary step towards a fairer global governance. Advocates of national sovereignty and realists warn that this threatens the principle of equality between states and gives major powers the tools to impose their own interpretation of international law in the name of international legitimacy.
