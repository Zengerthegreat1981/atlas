# British Empiricism (umbrella)

A broad current in English, Scottish and Irish philosophy of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries that treats experience as the source, and the test, of our knowledge of the world. Its classic statement is John Locke's rejection of innate ideas: the mind begins as "white paper" and all its ideas come from sensation or from reflection on its own operations. The label "British empiricism", together with its contrast with "continental rationalism" (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz), is a nineteenth- and twentieth-century historiographical construction; the thinkers grouped under it disagreed sharply, and Berkeley and Hume in particular drew conclusions Locke would have rejected.

## Context

The century of the English Civil War (1642-1651), the Commonwealth, the Restoration (1660) and the Glorious Revolution (1688) was one of intense political and religious conflict, and questions about toleration, conscience and the limits of authority fed into epistemology. The new experimental science (Boyle, Newton, the Royal Society, founded 1660) supplied a model of knowledge built on observation. Francis Bacon (1561-1626) had urged in the *Novum Organum* (1620) a method of systematic induction from observation, and is conventionally treated as a forerunner.

## Leading figures

- **Francis Bacon** (1561-1626): *Novum Organum* (1620), a forerunner of the empirical method.
- **Thomas Hobbes** (1588-1679): *Leviathan* (1651); a materialist who held that thought derives from sense, though he is not usually counted a core empiricist.
- **John Locke** (1632-1704): *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689, dated 1690).
- **George Berkeley** (1685-1753): *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710) and *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous* (1713); denied the existence of matter independent of mind.
- **David Hume** (1711-1776): *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-1740), *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748).
- **Thomas Reid** (1710-1796): *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785); leading figure of the Scottish "common sense" reply to Hume.
- **Dugald Stewart** (1753-1828): *Elements of the Philosophy of the Human Mind* (1792-1827).
- **David Hartley** (1705-1757): *Observations on Man* (1749), an associationist psychology.
- **Adam Smith** (1723-1790): *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), part of the Scottish Enlightenment's empirical study of human nature.

## Central ideas

- **Experience as the source of ideas**: all ideas derive from sensation or reflection (Locke); there are no innate ideas or principles.
- **Association of ideas**: complex ideas are built from simpler ones, a theme developed by Locke, Hume and Hartley.
- **Causation**: for Hume, causal necessity is not observed in objects; what we observe is regular succession, and the idea of necessary connection arises from the mind's habit of expecting the effect.
- **Sentiment and reason in morals**: Hume held that "reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions" (*Treatise* 2.3.3), and that moral distinctions rest on sentiment; he also noted the difficulty of deriving "ought" from "is" (*Treatise* 3.1.1).
- **Political thought**: Locke's *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) and *Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689) grounded government in consent and defended limited toleration; Hobbes grounded it in a contract made from fear of the state of nature.
- **Religion**: positions ranged from Locke's rational Christianity and Berkeley's idealist defence of theism (he was an Anglican bishop) to Hume's sceptical *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (published 1779).

## Relation to other movements

- **Continental rationalism**: the conventional foil, with its stress on innate ideas and a priori reasoning; the contrast is a simplification.
- **Kant**: said that Hume interrupted his "dogmatic slumber"; the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) tried to answer Humean scepticism while limiting both rationalism and empiricism.
- **Utilitarianism**: Bentham and J. S. Mill developed an empirical ethics, with James Mill (*History of British India*, 1817) among the associationist and radical thinkers.
- **Logical positivism and analytic philosophy**: twentieth-century empiricists such as Russell and Ayer saw themselves as heirs of Locke, Berkeley and Hume.
- **The Enlightenment**: British empiricism, with Newtonian science, was a major source for the French and wider European Enlightenment.

## Limits and criticism

- Empiricism has difficulty accounting for necessary truths in mathematics and logic and for the status of general laws of nature; Hume's treatment of induction raised the problem that remains a central issue in philosophy of science.
- Hume's analysis of causation and the self led to a sceptical conclusion that many later philosophers, Kant and Reid among them, tried to resist.
- Critics from Kant and Hegel onward argued that experience already presupposes structure supplied by the mind.
- Scholars also dispute how far the "empiricist tradition" was ever a unified school.
