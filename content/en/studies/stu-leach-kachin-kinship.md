# Kachin Kinship and Political Organization: Leach on Fluid Kinship Systems

This entry concerns the kinship and marriage dimension of Edmund Leach's *Political Systems of Highland Burma* (1954), his study of Kachin society in the hills of northern Burma (Myanmar) and adjoining areas. Leach argued that Kachin kinship and marriage rules were not a self-contained structural system but were bound up with the rank and political competition he analyzed in terms of the gumsa and gumlao models. A related article, "The Structural Implications of Matrilateral Cross-Cousin Marriage" (1951), treated the same material theoretically.

## Context

Leach (1910-1989), trained first as an engineer and then in anthropology at the London School of Economics under Malinowski and Firth, carried out fieldwork in the Kachin Hills in 1939-40 and served in Burma during the war. Notes were lost in the war, and the book draws also on records and earlier literature. It appeared when British anthropology was dominated by structural-functionalist studies of African lineage systems, and it was a deliberate departure from the assumption that societies are stable, bounded systems.

## Kinship and Alliance

Kachin society was organized in patrilineal clans and lineages. Marriage was governed by a rule of preference for marriage with the mother's brother's daughter (matrilateral cross-cousin), and relations between lineages were expressed in the terms *mayu* (wife-givers) and *dama* (wife-takers). The relation is asymmetric: wife-givers are in a superior ritual position to wife-takers. Leach regarded this as an example of the kind of "generalized exchange" discussed by Lévi-Strauss in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship*, in which groups are linked in a circuit of marriage, and he explored what that implied in practice. In the gumsa hierarchy, chiefly lineages built up wealth and followers partly through such marriage alliances.

Leach's argument was that the circle of generalized exchange is an ideal not fully realized in practice: real marriages were irregular, the actual flow of wives could not be closed into a stable cycle among a few lineages, and the system did not by itself produce a determinate political order. Instead, kinship terminology and marriage rules provided a language in which people claimed rank, and the political order depended on the balance of power, economic resources and ambition of leaders.

## Gumsa, Gumlao and Shan Models

Leach identified two Kachin ideals of political order: gumsa, in which hereditary chiefs and a rank order of lineages are accepted, and gumlao, an egalitarian form that rejects chiefs. Communities could shift between them over time; in practice, gumsa chiefs tended to claim more and more Shan-like authority and so provoked rebellion, and gumlao communities tended to reintroduce rank. The neighbouring Shan, whose states had hereditary princes and Buddhist institutions, provided a third model. He argued that the "Kachin" category did not correspond to a single fixed social system, and that ethnic labels such as Kachin and Shan were used flexibly, since people could "become Shan" by adopting a Shan style.

## Reception and Critique

The work helped redirect British anthropology toward process, change and individual action, and it influenced later transactional approaches in political anthropology. Critics have argued that Leach's account of oscillation is a model rather than a demonstration (the historical evidence is thin and was partly reconstructed after the loss of his notes), that he underestimated the stability of Kachin institutions, that he over-read Kachin ideology as manipulation, and that the colonial context of his data was not fully considered. Later researchers, including Kachin writers and historians, have also questioned his picture of Kachin social organization. Feminist scholars note the limited attention to women. His later monograph *Pul Eliya* (1961) applied a comparable stress on practical land tenure and everyday arrangements, as against formal kinship rules, to a Sri Lankan village.
