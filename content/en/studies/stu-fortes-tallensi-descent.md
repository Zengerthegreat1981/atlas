# Tallensi Descent Systems: Fortes on Unilineal Descent Groups

Meyer Fortes (1906-1983), a South African-born British social anthropologist who trained in London under Charles Seligman and Bronislaw Malinowski and later held the chair of social anthropology at Cambridge, carried out fieldwork among the Tallensi (Talensi) of the Northern Territories of the Gold Coast (now northern Ghana) in 1934-37. His two monographs, *The Dynamics of Clanship Among the Tallensi* (1945) and *The Web of Kinship Among the Tallensi* (1949), became leading examples of the study of unilineal descent groups in British structural-functional anthropology.

## Context

The fieldwork was supported by the International African Institute and carried out under British colonial administration; Fortes also contributed with Evans-Pritchard to the edited volume *African Political Systems* (1940), whose introduction set out the contrast between societies with centralized states and stateless societies ordered by lineage structures. The Tallensi, like the Nuer studied by Evans-Pritchard, were presented as a case of the latter, although Fortes also gave an account of the roles of chiefs of the Namoos (a chiefly category with origins in neighbouring kingdoms) alongside those of the indigenous Tale clans.

## Social Organization

The Tallensi are farmers of the savanna, growing millet and sorghum and keeping livestock, in dispersed homestead settlements. Fortes described a society organized through patrilineal descent: maximal and minimal lineages, and clans, in which membership is traced through males. Several features recur in his analysis:

- **Lineage and the ritual order.** Descent groups are tied to ancestors who are objects of veneration through shrines and sacrifices. Fortes emphasized that the authority of elders over juniors is linked to their ritual closeness to the ancestors.
- **Earth cult.** The earth priest (*tendaana*) serves as custodian of the shrines of the earth, territory and the land's fertility, and holds ritual authority that is distinct from the secular power of chiefs. Fortes treated this duality as central to Tale political and religious order.
- **Segmentation.** Lineages divide and recombine at different levels. As with the Nuer, a segmentary principle structures relations among groups, though Fortes stressed that the Tallensi case differs from the Nuer in the role of ritual and of chieftaincy.
- **Complementary filiation.** In later essays (notably "The Structure of Unilineal Descent Groups," 1953) he argued that, in societies with unilineal descent, the tie to the parent who is not in the line of descent, such as the mother's kin, is a necessary complement and is itself a recognized relationship ("complementary filiation").

## Fortes on Kinship

Fortes developed the idea that kinship has a "domestic" domain, characterized by familial, personal ties, and a "politico-jural" domain, characterized by public rights and corporate groups, and he stressed that in societies such as the Tallensi the two interlock. He also insisted that kinship relations carry moral force, which he called the axiom of "prescriptive altruism" or amity (in *Kinship and the Social Order*, 1969). In *Oedipus and Job in West African Religion* (1959) he analyzed Tallensi ideas about ancestors and destiny, and used them to reflect on psychology and religion.

## Reception and Critique

Fortes' work, together with Evans-Pritchard's, formed the core of "African models" of descent and lineage theory. Later critics, notably Adam Kuper and others, argued that the model was too rigid and that the focus on descent obscured alliance, residence, politics and individual strategy. Edmund Leach and other scholars questioned whether descent groups were as central as claimed. Feminist and postcolonial writers have pointed to the limited attention given to women and to the colonial administrative setting. Despite these critiques, the Tallensi monographs remain classics of ethnographic detail.
