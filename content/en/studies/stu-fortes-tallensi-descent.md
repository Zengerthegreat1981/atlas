# Tallensi Descent Systems: Fortes on Unilineal Descent Groups

Meyer Fortes' ethnographic monographs documenting the Tallensi people of northern Gold Coast (Ghana), published from 1945 onward. Fortes' work established descent group theory as foundational to kinship anthropology, demonstrating how patrilineal descent groups organized political authority, land tenure, religious practice, and social relationships. His systematic documentation of how descent categories generated social organization became a classic example of structural-functional analysis and influenced kinship theory for decades.

## Publication Context

Fortes' major monographs were published from 1945 onward, synthesizing fieldwork conducted from 1934-1937 among the Tallensi. Fortes, trained at Cambridge and influenced by Radcliffe-Brown's structural-functional approach, had conducted intensive research among the Tallensi and had collaborated with Evans-Pritchard (who was studying neighboring Nuer peoples) on the edited volume *African Political Systems*. Fortes' monographs appeared in a context where descent group theory was being developed and refined by British social anthropologists. His detailed ethnographic documentation of how descent groups functioned became the canonical example of unilineal descent organization and significantly influenced the development of descent group theory in anthropology. The monographs were published by Oxford University Press and became widely assigned in kinship and social organization courses. Fortes' work, like Evans-Pritchard's, was conducted in colonial contexts (Gold Coast under British administration) and reflected the particular colonial moment and administrative concerns, though Fortes' ethnographic account emphasized indigenous categories and understandings rather than colonial perspectives. The work established Fortes as one of the most important kinship theorists of the twentieth century.

## Research Question & Approach

Fortes' central research questions were: How are Tallensi societies organized through kinship? How do descent groups function in maintaining social cohesion and political authority? How do religious beliefs and practices relate to kinship organization? His methodological approach combined extended fieldwork residence, learning of the Tallensi language, systematic genealogical documentation, and observation of ritual practices and political processes. Fortes systematically collected genealogies extending multiple generations, mapped descent groups' membership, documented rights and obligations associated with different kinship positions, and observed rituals, ceremonies, and secular social interactions. He paid particular attention to how descent determined land rights, inheritance of property, and succession to ritual and political positions. He also documented the ideology of kinship—how Tallensi themselves conceptualized descent, explained genealogical relationships, and justified social distinctions. Importantly, Fortes combined genealogical documentation with attention to ritual specialists (particularly earth priests) and how ritual authority related to descent group organization. His approach revealed that descent was not merely a conceptual category but was constitutive of multiple dimensions of social life: it determined access to land, determined whom one could marry, determined political authority, and determined participation in ritual.

## Ethnographic Setting

The Tallensi inhabited the savanna region of northern Gold Coast (Ghana), numbering approximately 35,000 persons. They practiced millet and sorghum agriculture supplemented by livestock herding and some hunting. Tallensi society was organized through patrilineal descent, with descent groups (lineages at different orders of segmentation) forming the basic units of political and economic organization. They practiced virilocal marriage (wives moved to their husbands' villages), and property (land, crops) was primarily controlled by men and passed patrilineally. Religious practice centered on the veneration of ancestors and on relationships to territorial earth spirits, with ritual authority concentrated in the earth priest (a position inherited patrilineally). Fortes' fieldwork occurred from 1934-1937, during the colonial period under British administration, when Gold Coast was transitioning toward independence. Colonial presence was significant but not overwhelming in the Tallensi hills, and Tallensi societies maintained considerable autonomy in local affairs. Fortes achieved reasonable access, learned the Tallensi language, and conducted systematic genealogical interviews. His work was facilitated by his established reputation as a serious scholar and his respectful engagement with Tallensi intellectuals and elders.

## Key Findings

Fortes documented that Tallensi society was organized through patrilineal descent groups of varying orders of segmentation. The smallest unit was the minimal lineage (composed of closely related agnates), which was nested in larger lineages, which were nested in clans. Each level of the descent hierarchy had particular functions: the minimal lineage was the primary unit for everyday social interaction and for managing land; larger lineages mediated relationships between minimal lineages and provided frameworks for organizing ritual and political affairs. Property (land, houses, granaries) was owned and controlled by descent groups, with individual members having use rights but the lineage retaining ultimate control. Inheritance of property passed patrilineally, with the senior man of the lineage being responsible for allocating land to junior members and for maintaining lineage property. Political authority was similarly organized through descent: each lineage segment had a senior elder (the oldest competent man), and lineages of different orders had corresponding leaders who made decisions affecting the broader group. Fortes found that Tallensi descent organization was fundamentally connected to religious practice: each descent group had a shrine, and relationships with ancestors were organized through descent group membership. The earth priest, who was the keeper of relationships to territorial earth spirits, was also a patrilineal heir—the ritual position passed from father to son. Fortes crucially documented that descent groups were not entirely fixed: marriages occasionally resulted in contested affiliation, genealogies were sometimes adjusted when relationships between groups changed, and individuals could sometimes be incorporated into descent groups other than their patrilineal group through adoption or other mechanisms. However, the ideology of patrilineal descent was very strong, and disputes over affiliation were resolved by reference to patrilineal principles.

## Ethnographic Examples

Fortes provides detailed case examples illustrating how descent operated in Tallensi society. He documents specific genealogies showing how descent groups segmented, how individuals were positioned within the lineage, and what rights and obligations attached to different positions. One example describes a dispute over land rights: two men from different minimal lineages claimed rights to the same piece of land; the dispute was resolved by reference to genealogy—the man whose lineage had been granted the land by the earth priest earlier had superior rights. The earth priest was consulted (he was the custodian of knowledge about territorial land rights), and his decision was generally accepted. Fortes also documents a succession dispute: an earth priest died, and question arose about who should succeed him. The position passed patrilineally, but there were multiple eligible candidates (multiple sons who could inherit). The candidates' relatives argued about whom the earth priest had designated as successor, and the matter was eventually resolved through discussion with other respected elders who knew the earth priest's intentions. Fortes also records genealogical narratives in which Tallensi explained their descent and justified their current social positions. These narratives articulated how descent groups had emerged from an apical ancestor, how they had segmented through his descendants' lines, and how contemporary groups maintained relationships of kinship despite their segmentation. Fortes notes that genealogies were not merely historical records but were actively used in contemporary social life to claim land rights, to justify political position, and to determine marriage eligibility.

## Theoretical Contribution

Fortes' work was enormously influential in establishing descent group theory as central to kinship anthropology. His systematic demonstration of how patrilineal descent determined land rights, political authority, ritual participation, and social relationships showed that descent groups were not merely kinship categories but were fundamental to understanding social organization. His analysis of lineage segmentation—the principle that lineages divided at different levels of genealogical distance—provided a framework for understanding how political units could scale from local communities to broader tribes. His analysis of the ideology of descent (how Tallensi themselves thought about and justified descent-based organization) influenced kinship theory toward understanding ideology as productive of social organization rather than merely as false consciousness. His demonstration that descent was connected to religious practice and that ritual specialists' authority derived from descent positions showed that kinship could not be analyzed in isolation but required understanding how it articulated with religion, politics, and economics. His work contributed to establishing the principle that in many non-industrial societies, kinship was the primary organizing principle of social life, and that understanding any aspect of social organization required understanding kinship.

## Methodological Legacy

Fortes pioneered genealogical methods that became standard in anthropological research. His systematic collection of genealogies, his precise documentation of genealogical relationships and the principles by which they were calculated, and his analysis of genealogical depth and branching established genealogy as a crucial research method. His method of analyzing genealogies not merely as historical records but as productive of contemporary social relationships—showing how people used genealogy to make claims and to justify social positions—influenced understanding of genealogy as an active social practice. His attention to terminology—recording not merely kinship terms but how people used and understood those terms—influenced the sophistication of kinship terminology analysis. His integration of genealogical documentation with observation of ritual and political processes showed how genealogy could be analyzed alongside social practice to understand how descent operated. His development of genealogical diagrams and his practice of presenting genealogies visually influenced how kinship knowledge was represented and communicated. His comparative analysis of different levels of descent organization showed how the same principles could organize groups at different scales, a finding that influenced subsequent comparative kinship analysis.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has identified important critiques of Fortes' work. Some scholars have questioned whether descent group theory, while powerful for organizing analytical understanding, actually captured the complexity of how Tallensi people understood and experienced kinship. Postcolonial scholars have questioned whether Fortes adequately contextualized Tallensi kinship within colonialism and whether colonial administration influenced the practices he documented. Feminist scholars have noted that Fortes' analysis centers on patrilineal descent and male authority, with less attention to women's roles in reproducing descent groups and less attention to women's own kinship perspectives. However, Fortes himself later produced work attending more explicitly to gender dimensions of kinship. Postcolonial Tallensi scholars and community members have also engaged with Fortes' ethnography, sometimes affirming his representations as generally accurate, other times noting the ways his analysis reflected particular moments and particular perspectives within Tallensi society. Despite these critiques, Fortes' work remains enormously valuable as a detailed ethnographic documentation of descent group organization and as an exemplary analysis of how kinship operates as a fundamental organizing principle. For contemporary students, the work exemplifies meticulous ethnographic documentation and shows how genealogy and kinship terminology can be systematically analyzed to understand social organization. The work remains a foundational text in kinship studies courses globally.
