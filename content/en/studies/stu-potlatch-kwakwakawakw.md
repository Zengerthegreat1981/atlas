# Potlatch Among Northwest Coast Peoples: Boas and Subsequent Studies

Franz Boas' pioneering ethnographic documentation of potlatch ceremonies among the Kwakwaka'wakw and other Northwest Coast peoples, conducted and published from 1897-1921. The potlatch—a ceremony in which wealthy individuals and lineages publicly destroyed property or distributed vast quantities of goods to establish and maintain rank and prestige—represented one of anthropology's most famous prestige economies and became central to debates about rational economic behavior and the diversity of human economic systems. Boas' meticulous documentation established the ethnographic baseline for understanding these ceremonies.

## Publication Context

Boas began his fieldwork with Northwest Coast peoples in 1886 and conducted research over decades, publishing detailed accounts from 1897 onward. His ethnographic research on potlatch ceremonies was conducted and published during a period when the potlatch was under intense pressure from the Canadian government: the Indian Act amendments of 1885 and 1921 actually prohibited the potlatch, making Boas' documentation of the ceremonies ethnographically urgent as a record of practices that were being legally suppressed. This context is crucial: Boas was not merely documenting "traditional" or unchanging practices but was recording ceremonies that were being performed despite colonial legal prohibition, and his work served as a record of the potlatch for Kwakwaka'wakw and other Northwest Coast peoples themselves. His publications appeared as government reports and academic journals, and his detailed documentation established the empirical baseline for subsequent scholarly engagement with Northwest Coast societies. Boas' work was foundational to anthropology as a discipline; his commitment to meticulous empirical documentation, his rejection of evolutionary racial frameworks, and his emphasis on understanding cultures in their own terms helped establish modern anthropological practice. His potlatch documentation became particularly famous due to later theoretical engagement by scholars like Marcel Mauss and Gregory Bateson, who used the potlatch as an exemplar of gift exchange economies.

## Research Question & Approach

Boas' research question was comprehensive: How were Northwest Coast societies organized, and what were the economic and social mechanisms through which status and hierarchy were established? Specifically, how did the potlatch ceremony function? What was the social significance of the destruction and distribution of property that characterized the ceremony? His methodological approach combined extensive fieldwork (he spent months among various Northwest Coast groups), interviews and collaboration with indigenous informants and experts, and collection of genealogical and historical data. Crucially, Boas worked with indigenous intellectuals and knowledge-keepers—particularly George Hunt, a Kwakwaka'wakw man, who collaborated with him for decades as a field research partner. Hunt's knowledge of Kwakwaka'wakw language, kinship, and ceremony was essential to Boas' ethnographic work. Boas systematically documented potlatch ceremonies through multiple methods: he attended actual potlatches when possible, he interviewed participants about ceremonies they had hosted or witnessed, he collected narratives about famous potlatches, and he documented the genealogical and rank systems that organized potlatch participation. His approach revealed that potlatches were not random or irrational destruction of property but carefully calculated ceremonies aimed at establishing and maintaining inherited rank and at managing relationships between ranked lineages. He documented how potlatch involvement was determined by one's position in a complex system of inherited titles and privileges.

## Ethnographic Setting

The Northwest Coast of North America—from the Tlingit territories in southern Alaska through Kwakwaka'wakw and other Coastal Salish peoples' territories in British Columbia to the Nuu-chah-nulth of Vancouver Island—was characterized by abundant marine resources (salmon, halibut, herring, sea mammals), dense temperate rainforests, and river systems that supported complex sedentary communities. These societies supported relatively large, dense populations through resource harvesting rather than agriculture. They developed distinctive cultures characterized by elaborate wooden architecture, monumental art (particularly the famous totem poles), complex kinship systems emphasizing inherited status and rank, and sophisticated maritime technology. Boas worked among multiple Northwest Coast groups, though his most intensive work was with the Kwakwaka'wakw (then called Kwakiutl) based in and around Fort Rupert and Alert Bay. His fieldwork occurred during the 1880s-1920s, a period of profound crisis for Northwest Coast societies: small-pox epidemics in the nineteenth century had devastated populations, colonial settlement was encroaching, the fur trade had disrupted traditional economies, and the Canadian government was actively attempting to assimilate indigenous peoples through residential schools and legal prohibition of ceremonies including the potlatch. Boas' fieldwork took place within this context of colonial domination and cultural suppression; the fact that Northwest Coast peoples continued to practice potlatches despite legal prohibition and social pressure testified to the ceremonies' enduring significance.

## Key Findings

Boas documented that the potlatch was a ceremony in which individuals (usually wealthy men, though women could also host potlatches) and their lineages distributed or destroyed vast quantities of property—blankets, copper objects, carved wooden items, food, and money—to other lineages. The distribution followed ranked hierarchies: the most prestigious recipients received the most valuable items. The ceremony affirmed the host's rank and the ranks of other attending lineages. Crucially, Boas found that the potlatch was understood as a kind of gift with obligations: recipients understood themselves as placed under obligation by the gift, and the ability to eventually reciprocate at an equally prestigious level demonstrated one's rank and capacity. The destruction of property—burning valuable blankets or sinking boxes of money into the sea—was understood as demonstrating the host's wealth and status; only a person with surplus resources could afford such destruction. The destruction also prevented rivals from acquiring those resources, making it a form of competitive display. Boas documented that potlatches were connected to significant life events and transitions: a chief might host a potlatch to mark his succession to a title, to initiate his son into high rank, to mourn a deceased relative, or to settle disputes. The ceremonies involved elaborate oratory, the recitation of genealogies and historical narratives, and the performance of dances and ceremonies. Boas found that participation in potlatches was structurally determined: rank and lineage membership determined one's position in the ceremony and the obligations one had. However, Boas also documented how individuals could manipulate the system: particularly ambitious men could work to accumulate wealth and host potlatches that elevated their rank; conversely, rivals could attempt to undermine someone's status by hosting larger potlatches or by contesting claims to titles.

## Ethnographic Examples

Boas records specific potlatches he attended or learned about from informants. He describes a potlatch hosted by a Kwakwaka'wakw chief marking the initiation of his son into a secret society. The ceremony lasted several days, involved the distribution of hundreds of blankets and other valuable items, included the performance of elaborate dances and the recitation of genealogical narratives establishing the host's and his son's rank, and culminated in a public feast with vast quantities of food. Boas records that certain positions in the ceremony had particular significance: the chief's rival received prominent gifts, marking the chief's dominance; other lineages and individuals received gifts scaled to their rank. Boas also documents a more competitive potlatch in which two chiefs attempted to outdo each other in generosity and display. Each hosted successive potlatches, each attempting to give more valuable items or to demonstrate greater wealth than his rival. The sequence of competitive potlatches lasted years, with the chiefs and their supporters intensely engaged in the competition. Boas records that the competition was understood as serious and as having real consequences for the chiefs' rank and authority. He also documents the practice of burning coppers—valuable objects that were understood as embodying wealth and prestige. In one case, Boas records a chief burning a copper in a competitive display, asserting that he was wasting the precious object to demonstrate his wealth and confidence. Boas notes that this act generated intense discussion; observers assessed whether the chief was genuinely wealthy enough to afford such display, or whether he was overstating his resources.

## Theoretical Contribution

Boas' documentation of the potlatch established the empirical foundation for diverse theoretical engagements with prestige economies. His meticulous documentation influenced anthropological understanding of gift exchange economies (later theorized by Mauss) and contributed to demonstrating the diversity of human economic systems. His analysis suggested that apparently irrational economic behavior—the destruction of property—could be understood as rational when interpreted within the cultural framework of rank competition and status display. His work contributed to arguments against evolutionary frameworks that posited simple barter as a universal stage in human economic development; Boas showed that sophisticated, complex economic systems operated through gift exchange and status competition. His documentation of how potlatches articulated with kinship and social organization suggested that economic systems could not be analyzed in isolation but required understanding how they related to kinship, rank, and political authority. His emphasis on the historical dimension of economic systems (documenting how potlatches had changed over time, particularly in response to colonial contact and the fur trade) pioneered historical economic anthropology. His demonstration of how indigenous peoples modified traditional practices in response to new opportunities (some people used money earned in the fur trade and from wage labor to purchase items distributed in potlatches, extending participation) showed that prestige economies were not static but adaptable institutions.

## Methodological Legacy

Boas pioneered crucial methodological innovations in ethnographic research. His collaboration with indigenous knowledge-keepers and research partners (particularly George Hunt) established the principle that ethnographic research required working with indigenous intellectuals and experts as partners, not merely as informants. His systematic documentation of kinship, rank, and genealogy became a model for subsequent kinship and political anthropology research. His comparative work across multiple Northwest Coast groups, examining how different societies organized potlatches and rank, pioneered comparative ethnography while remaining grounded in detailed documentation. His practice of conducting long-term fieldwork (his research spanned decades) demonstrated the value of sustained engagement with particular communities. His attention to historical change and his documentation of how ceremonies were being practiced despite colonial suppression pioneered what would later be called applied and advocacy anthropology. His practice of publishing detailed ethnographic monographs combining meticulous documentation with theoretical analysis established a model for ethnographic monography still influential. Boas' work also pioneered the use of indigenous collaborators' linguistic expertise: his work on Northwest Coast languages relied heavily on his consultants' knowledge, and this collaboration contributed to subsequent development of linguistic anthropology.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has identified important extensions and critiques of Boas' work. Historians have questioned whether Boas adequately contextualized the potlatch within colonialism and whether his representation of ceremonies conducted under legal prohibition captured how the potlatch was being transformed under colonial pressure. Feminist scholars have noted that Boas' analysis centers on male chiefs and male potlatch participants, with less attention to women's roles in resource accumulation and women's participation in ceremonies. Postcolonial indigenous scholars have criticized Boas (and subsequent anthropology) for producing documentation of indigenous practices that were then appropriated by colonial governments and used to suppress those very practices. However, they have also emphasized that Boas' detailed documentation has been valuable for indigenous communities seeking to revitalize potlatch practices and to understand their own histories. Indigenous historians and artists have drawn on Boas' ethnographic documentation to reconstruct potlatch practices and to assert indigenous cultural continuity and authority. The potlatch was legalized in Canada in 1951, and subsequent decades saw the resurgence of potlatches as assertions of indigenous cultural identity and sovereignty. Boas' work, despite its limitations, remains valuable as the most detailed ethnographic documentation of potlatch ceremonies at a particular historical moment and as a record that has been valuable to Northwest Coast peoples themselves. For contemporary students, the work exemplifies how ethnographic documentation can serve not merely academic purposes but can also serve the communities being studied, particularly when that documentation is conducted with respect and collaborative partnership.
