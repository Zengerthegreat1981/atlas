# Potlatch Among Northwest Coast Peoples: Boas and Subsequent Studies

The potlatch is a ceremonial institution of peoples of the Northwest Coast of North America, including the Kwakwaka'wakw (formerly called Kwakiutl), Tlingit, Haida and Nuu-chah-nulth. At potlatches hosts publicly validate rank, names and rights by giving feasts and distributing gifts to guests, who serve as witnesses. Franz Boas' work with the Kwakwaka'wakw, much of it with George Hunt, provided the main early ethnographic record, and the potlatch became a central case in the anthropology of exchange and prestige.

## Context

Boas began fieldwork on the Northwest Coast in 1886 and published extensively on the Kwakwaka'wakw, including *The Social Organization and the Secret Societies of the Kwakiutl Indians* (1897) and the large *Ethnology of the Kwakiutl* (published in 1921 in the Bureau of American Ethnology's annual report series), much of it based on texts written by Hunt. The potlatch was prohibited in Canada by an 1884 amendment to the Indian Act (in effect from 1885) and the ban was strengthened later; a well-known 1921 potlatch at Village Island led to arrests and to the confiscation of ceremonial regalia. The prohibition remained until the law was revised in 1951. These ceremonies were therefore documented, and also continued, under legal suppression, in a period of population loss from epidemic disease and of increased wage labour, which altered the scale and form of potlatching.

## What a Potlatch Is

Potlatches are held to mark events such as accession to a chiefly name or position, marriages, the validation of rights to crests, dances and territories, memorials, and the raising of memorial poles. Hosts feed guests, distribute gifts (in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries including blankets and other manufactured goods, as well as traditional items), and make speeches that recite histories and claims. Guests are seated by rank and, by receiving gifts and witnessing, acknowledge the claims being made. Among the Kwakwaka'wakw, rank was embodied in a limited number of inherited names and associated privileges, and the copper, a shield-shaped object with a named value, could be given, broken or "sold" as a statement of rank. Boas recorded forms of rivalry between chiefs in which valuable property was given away or destroyed, a feature that was later emphasized in comparative accounts.

## Interpretations

- **Prestige and rank.** Boas treated the potlatch as bound up with a hereditary rank system and an economy of credit-like obligations, in which the amounts given were expected to be returned with interest.
- **Competition and the "rivalry" model.** Ruth Benedict's *Patterns of Culture* (1934) presented Kwakiutl culture as characterized by a competitive drive for status, an interpretation that later scholars considered exaggerated.
- **Gift theory.** Marcel Mauss used Boas's material on the Kwakiutl, together with Tlingit and Haida data, as an example of a system of "total prestations" in which gifts create hierarchy and obligation (1925).
- **Historical critique.** Helen Codere (1950) argued that the intense rivalry and property destruction in the late nineteenth century reflected the effects of the fur trade, new wealth and population collapse rather than an ancient pattern. Later work by Sergei Kan (1989) on the Tlingit, by Wilson Duff, and by Indigenous scholars emphasizes the potlatch as a system of law, governance and memorial, and as the venue in which rights to land and names are validated before witnesses.

## Suppression and Revitalization

The criminalization of the potlatch is a central episode in the history of Canadian Indigenous policy; the 1921 arrests and the confiscation of regalia (some objects held for decades in museums) were followed by campaigns for repatriation, and after 1951 potlatching has been revived and continues as a means of cultural assertion and governance. Boas' records have been used by communities to support revitalization, though critics note that the ethnographic record reflects the period of crisis in which it was made and the collecting of material culture in that period.
