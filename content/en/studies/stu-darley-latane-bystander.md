# Darley & Latané Bystander Intervention and Diffusion of Responsibility Studies

A famous laboratory experimental study that demonstrated that the probability and speed of individuals' responding to help a person undergoing an emergency epileptic seizure decrease as the number of other witnesses the participant believes to be present increases.

## Context and experimental design

The study drew its inspiration from the murder of Kitty Genovese in 1964. Participants sat in individual rooms and communicated through an intercom system with other participants (pre-recorded audio). During the discussion, one of the confederate participants claimed to be suffering a sudden epileptic seizure and cried out for help; the independent variable was the number of people the participant believed to be in the discussion: the participant and the victim alone, the victim plus one other bystander, or the victim plus four others. In all, 72 students (59 women and 13 men) took part.

## Results and conclusions

When participants believed they were the sole witness, 85% reported the emergency and sought help before the seizure ended (mean latency: 52 seconds). When participants believed one other bystander was present, 62% sought help (mean latency: 93 seconds). When they believed four other bystanders were present, only 31% sought help (mean latency: 166 seconds).

Latané and Darley (1970) formalized these findings into a five-step cognitive decision model of bystander intervention:
1. **Noticing the event:** becoming aware that something unusual is occurring.
2. **Interpreting the event as an emergency:** recognizing the situation as requiring urgent intervention.
3. **Assuming personal responsibility:** deciding that intervention is one's personal duty rather than that of someone else.
4. **Deciding how to help:** identifying an appropriate form of assistance (direct or indirect).
5. **Implementing the decision:** taking concrete action despite potential costs.

The authors identified three primary psychological mechanisms that impede intervention along these stages: **diffusion of responsibility** (the subjective obligation to act is divided among all present, reducing individual urgency); **pluralistic ignorance** (bystanders look to the calm exterior of others to interpret an ambiguous situation, collectively concluding no emergency exists); and **evaluation apprehension** (fear of social embarrassment or negative judgment should the intervention prove unnecessary or awkward).

## Criticism and methodological debate

A meta-analysis (Fischer et al., 2011) of dozens of studies supported the bystander effect in general, but found that it is smaller in clearly dangerous emergencies involving a perpetrator, where bystanders were more likely to intervene. Video analyses of real public conflicts (Philpot et al., 2020) found that bystanders intervened in most of the incidents studied, suggesting that the effect in everyday settings is less strong than the laboratory work implies.

## Historical reconsideration of the Kitty Genovese case

The experiment was originally inspired by a famous press report in the New York Times claiming that 38 witnesses had observed the murder of Kitty Genovese in 1964 without anyone intervening or even calling the police. Later historical investigations (especially the 2007 article by Manning, Levine, and Collins) showed that the figure of 38 silent witnesses was greatly exaggerated by the press, and that some neighbors did try to intervene or alerted the police, which does not detract from the validity of the psychological phenomenon later demonstrated by Darley and Latané's laboratory experiments, but it is a reminder of the importance of not confusing the original inspiring incident (which was marred by exaggerations) with the independent experimental evidence that was later built apart from it.

## Other moderating variables of the phenomenon

Later research broadened the understanding of the phenomenon to include moderating factors: the bystander effect weakens when the witnesses know one another personally (not strangers), when one of those present has specialized training (such as a physician or paramedic), and when the situation is completely unambiguous, such as immediate danger to life, in place of an ambiguous situation that might be interpreted in multiple ways, which shows that "diffusion of responsibility" is not an inevitable law but a probabilistic tendency sensitive to the surrounding social context.
