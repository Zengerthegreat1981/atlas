# Bartlett's 'War of the Ghosts' and Reconstructive Memory Experiments

A classic study in which English participants were asked to read a legend from Native American folklore ('The War of the Ghosts') and recall it over successive intervals, revealing the systematic transformation of the story to match their familiar cultural schemas.

## Context and experimental design

Bartlett used two methods: "repeated reproduction" (the same participant retells the story at intervals ranging from 15 minutes to several years) and "serial reproduction" (the first participant passes the story to the second, like a whispered message). He chose a story containing magical and cultural elements foreign to English students in order to observe how they handled cognitive gaps.

## Results and conclusions

The results showed that the recalled stories became shorter and more concise, with incomprehensible elements dropped and terms modified to fit English culture (such as replacing 'canoe' with 'boat', and justifying mysterious events with rational causes). Bartlett concluded that memory is not a photographic recording but an active reconstruction driven by 'cognitive schemas' and the search for meaning.

## Critique and methodological controversy

Bartlett was criticized for informal procedures: testing intervals varied, instructions were loose, and he reported few statistics. Later attempts to replicate the repeated-reproduction method (Bergman & Roediger, 1999; Wynn & Logie, 1998) found that recall over time was often more accurate and less distorted than Bartlett's account suggests, although systematic changes such as omissions and rationalizations did occur. His concept of the schema nevertheless remained a point of departure for later work on constructive memory, including the research of Elizabeth Loftus.

## The types of distortion Bartlett documented

Bartlett described several kinds of change in the recalled story: **omission** of unfamiliar or irrelevant details and general simplification; **rationalization**, supplying reasons that make puzzling events seem sensible to the recaller; **transformation of familiar-sounding elements** into the recaller's own cultural terms; and the emergence of a **dominant theme** around which the remembered story is organized. The terms "levelling" and "sharpening," often attributed to him, come from later work by Allport and Postman (1947) on rumor.

## Impact on legal psychology and the reliability of testimony

Bartlett's emphasis on reconstruction anticipated later research, such as that of Elizabeth Loftus on eyewitness testimony and the misinformation effect (Loftus & Palmer, 1974), which has informed legal understanding of the limits of eyewitness memory. Loftus's experiments, however, used different methods and did not derive directly from the War of the Ghosts studies.
