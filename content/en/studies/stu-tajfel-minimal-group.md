# Tajfel's Studies of the Minimal Group Paradigm and Social Identity

A series of experiments that established the 'minimal group' paradigm, showing that randomly categorizing participants on the basis of trivial aesthetic preferences produces systematic discrimination and favoritism toward members of their own group at the expense of the other group.

## Context and Experimental Design

Adolescent boys (aged about 14 to 15, in Bristol, England) were divided into two groups on the basis of arbitrary and trivial criteria (such as over- or underestimating the number of dots in a display, or a stated preference for paintings by Klee versus Kandinsky). The participants were then asked to distribute real monetary rewards and points among anonymous individuals from the two groups using decision matrices designed to separate different allocation strategies. The participants never allocated to themselves and never met the others.

## Results and Conclusions

The participants regularly preferred to maximize the positive difference in favor of members of their own group even when it cost them a reduction in the absolute reward gained by their own group. Tajfel and colleagues concluded that intergroup bias does not require a history of conflict or real competition over resources, and can arise from mere categorization, which they interpreted in terms of a search for positive distinctiveness for the self.

## Criticism and Methodological Controversy

Some critics argued that the matrices used may have led participants to understand the experiment as a competitive game (Demand Characteristics); however, the basic in-group favoritism effect has been replicated many times with varied procedures and populations (a meta-analysis by Mullen, Brown and Smith, 1992, found a reliable effect), although its size and its explanation remain debated.

## Tajfel's Allocation Matrices

Tajfel designed precise decision matrices that allow the participant to choose between conflicting strategies: maximizing the joint profit of both groups, maximizing the absolute profit of his own group regardless of the other group, or maximizing the relative difference (Maximum Differentiation) between his group and the other group even if that reduces the absolute profit of his own group. Participants' choices reflected a mix of fairness, maximum joint profit and maximum differentiation, with maximum differentiation and in-group favoritism clearly evident, which led Tajfel to propose that the motive was not material profit in itself but relative advantage and positive distinctiveness from the out-group.

## Social Identity Theory

Henri Tajfel, in collaboration with John Turner, developed the findings of the minimal group paradigm to formulate "Social Identity Theory" in the 1970s (Tajfel and Turner, 1979), which assumes that part of an individual's self-concept derives from membership in social groups, and that individuals seek to achieve positive self-esteem by positively differentiating their group in comparison with other groups, even in the absence of any tangible material competition. Turner later developed this framework further in "Self-Categorization Theory," which elaborated the precise cognitive mechanisms of the process of social categorization itself.
