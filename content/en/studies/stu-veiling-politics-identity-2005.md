# An Anthropological Study of the Veil and Identity

This file covers an anthropological and political line of research that examines the Islamic veil as a symbol with many meanings: worship and religious identity for some veiled women, an imposed instrument of oppression for others, and the subject of repeated legislative intervention by the state in two opposite directions (imposition or prohibition).

## Definition

In this field, "the veil" refers to a Muslim woman covering her head and hair (and sometimes her face, in the forms of the niqab and the burqa), based on the verses of Surat al-Nur and Surat al-Ahzab that command believing women to draw their *khumur* over their *juyub* and to draw their *jalabib* around them. The field examines the gap between this textual basis and the diversity of its actual forms across cultures (the Indonesian colored veil, the Gulf abaya, the Iranian chador, and the Maghrebi *haik*).

## Historical Context

The veil has been the subject of recurring political controversy. From the forced unveiling campaigns in Iran under Reza Shah (1936) and in Tunisia after independence, to the compulsory hijab introduced in Iran after the 1979 revolution, and, in France, to the law banning conspicuous religious symbols including the headscarf in public schools (2004) and the law prohibiting face covering in public places (2010), debated in the name of secularism (*laicite*).

## Main Ideas and Method

Fadwa El Guindi, in *Veil: Modesty, Privacy and Resistance* (1999), challenged readings that reduce the veil to oppression alone. Drawing on Egyptian material, she argued that many women, including educated women, adopted the veil from the 1970s as an expression of Islamic identity and cultural assertion, and she placed it within a long history of dress, modesty and privacy in Arab culture. In the same year as the title of this entry, Saba Mahmood's *Politics of Piety* (2005), an ethnography of a women's mosque movement in Cairo, questioned liberal assumptions about agency and piety in related debates. At the same time, other studies documented clear cases of family or state coercion, which confirms the multiplicity of meanings according to context, rather than the existence of one fixed meaning for the practice.

## Impact and Criticism

This field pushed researchers beyond the binary of "liberation versus oppression" toward a contextual understanding of each case. Joan Wallach Scott, in *The Politics of the Veil* (2007), analyzes the French ban as a struggle over the meaning of secularism and integration, not merely a neutral dress law.

## The Jurisprudential Context

The precise legal boundary of the obligation (whether covering the face is obligatory) remains a historical jurisprudential disagreement among the schools of law, which this anthropological field has not resolved. The field describes the social diversity of the practice more than it decides the correctness of a particular jurisprudential interpretation.

## Methodological Note

This file presents a sociological description of the multiplicity of the meanings of the practice across contexts. It does not weigh in favor of a position supporting or opposing the veil. The aim is to document the actual diversity of women's lived experience, rather than reducing it to a single explanation.
