# Tongan Kinship and Social Hierarchy: Gifford on Pacific Rank Systems

Edward Winslow Gifford (1887-1959), an American anthropologist and ornithologist associated with the University of California, Berkeley, and the Bernice P. Bishop Museum in Honolulu, carried out research in Tonga in 1920-21 as part of the Bayard Dominick Expedition of the Bishop Museum. His monograph *Tongan Society* (Bishop Museum Bulletin 61, 1929) is a detailed account of the social organization, rank, kinship, political institutions, religion and material culture of Tonga, based on interviews with Tongan elders and on written sources. It remains a standard historical source on Tongan society, though it is a work of "reconstruction" of earlier institutions more than direct observation.

## Background

Tonga, an archipelago in western Polynesia, is a hereditary monarchy that was never formally colonized; it became a British protected state in 1900 and regained full independence in 1970. At the time of Gifford's visit, the kingdom was ruled by Queen Salote (who became queen in 1918), and the social order combined older rank institutions with church, government and a written constitution (1875). Gifford collected information from Tongan chiefs and informants about the situation before European contact and in the nineteenth century.

## Rank and Kinship

*Tongan Society* describes a society ordered by hereditary rank. At its apex stood the paramount titles: the sacred Tu'i Tonga and the later, more secular lines of the Tu'i Ha'atakalaua and the Tu'i Kanokupolu (from whom the present royal line derives), followed by noble title-holders (*hou'eiki*), their attendants (*matapule*), and commoners (*tu'a*). Kinship is organized through relatively flexible descent groups and households, and rank is traced genealogically. Later scholars (Elizabeth Bott, Adrienne Kaeppler, George Marcus) elaborated a distinctive feature of the Tongan system which Gifford described: the relative ranking of siblings and of the kin of a mother and a father. A sister outranks her brother, and her children outrank his; the father's sister (*mehekitanga*) holds a position of high authority over her brother's children, and her side of the family (the *fahu* relation) receives special honours at ceremonies such as weddings and funerals. This means that rank passes in complex ways through both male and female lines, although titles and political offices are mainly held by men.

## Contributions and Limits

Gifford's strength is the breadth of detail on genealogies, titles, ceremonial protocols (including the kava ceremony and first-fruits offerings), land and tribute, and the organization of political rank. He was concerned to record institutions of the past on the basis of oral testimony at a time when many were changing, and the book, together with his own notes, is used by Tongan scholars and historians. Subsequent research has corrected and extended aspects of his account, especially concerning the role of the female line and fahu relations (Bott 1982, Kaeppler 1971) and the dynamics of the chiefly political system (Marcus 1980). The work is primarily descriptive and organized by topic, and some authors note that its use of informants' accounts of the "old" system blends different periods.
