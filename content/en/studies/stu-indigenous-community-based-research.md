# Indigenous Community-Based Research: Collaborative Ethnography and Indigenous Methodologies

Indigenous community-based research refers to a body of practice and writing, developing especially from the 1990s onward, in which research with Indigenous peoples is shaped by the communities concerned: they help define the questions, take part in the work, and control how knowledge is used and shared. It grew out of Indigenous critiques of earlier research, including anthropology, as extractive and colonial, and it overlaps with collaborative ethnography, participatory action research and Indigenous methodologies.

## Background

Indigenous scholars and organizations argued that research had often been done on communities, for the benefit of outside academics, and that findings were frequently unreturned or used against community interests. A landmark text is Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (1999; 2nd edition 2012). Smith, a Maori scholar from Aotearoa New Zealand, analyzed research as bound up with colonialism and with Western ways of knowing, and set out an agenda of Indigenous research that includes decolonizing, healing, and self-determination. Related Maori approaches are known as kaupapa Maori research.

## Approaches

Several strands can be distinguished:

- **Indigenous research methodologies.** Authors such as Shawn Wilson (*Research Is Ceremony*, 2008, Opaskwayak Cree) argue for research frameworks grounded in Indigenous ontologies and relational accountability, in which the researcher is responsible to relationships with people, land and ancestors.
- **Data governance principles.** In Canada, First Nations organizations articulated the principles of Ownership, Control, Access and Possession (OCAP), set out in work such as Schnarch (2004), which assert community authority over data collected about them.
- **Collaborative ethnography.** Anthropologists such as Luke Eric Lassiter (2005) developed forms of ethnographic work in which consultants contribute to the design, interpretation and writing of texts, and in which co-authorship and review by communities are normal.
- **Community-based participatory research.** Particularly in health, education and environmental management, communities and academic partners jointly identify problems and use the results for local programs and policy.

## Principles in Practice

Common commitments include community consent and ongoing review, not only individual informed consent; return of results in forms useful to the community; training and employment of community members; recognition of Indigenous intellectual property and cultural protocols; and attention to who may hold and share particular knowledge. Many research ethics frameworks now include provisions of this kind, such as Canada's national research ethics policy chapter on research involving First Nations, Inuit and Metis peoples.

## Critique

Scholars note that "collaboration" and "partnership" can be used rhetorically without real sharing of power; that university timetables, funding rules and publication incentives constrain genuine community control; that communities are not homogeneous, so questions arise about who speaks for "the community"; and that Indigenous scholars themselves differ on whether Indigenous knowledge can be integrated with Western science or should be pursued separately. Some Indigenous authors call for research that goes beyond collaboration to Indigenous-led institutions and priorities.
