# Indigenous Community-Based Research: Collaborative Ethnography and Indigenous Methodologies

A body of work developed from the 1990s onward, documenting and theorizing collaborative ethnographic research conducted in partnership with indigenous communities. Rather than anthropologists conducting research "on" indigenous peoples, community-based research involved indigenous communities as partners, collaborators, and co-researchers, with research agendas determined jointly and research benefiting communities directly. The literature on indigenous community-based research emerged from critique of earlier anthropology's colonial legacies and represented a significant shift toward decolonial and community-accountable research practices.

## Publication Context

Indigenous community-based research emerged as a significant approach from the 1990s onward, as indigenous scholars (particularly Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies* published in 1999) theorized research approaches that centered indigenous control and community benefit. The work appeared in a context of growing indigenous movements globally, of increasing indigenous leadership in academic and research contexts, and of growing critique of anthropology's colonial history and ongoing power imbalances in research. Indigenous scholars and community leaders articulated that research had historically been extractive—that outside researchers had conducted research on indigenous communities, extracted knowledge and data, and published for academic audiences, with little benefit to the communities studied. Community-based research represented a commitment to different practices: research questions determined by communities, research processes accountable to communities, and research benefits accruing primarily to communities. The shift was significant and represented a fundamental transformation in anthropological and interdisciplinary research practice.

## Research Question & Approach

Community-based research asked: What research questions are important to indigenous communities themselves? How can research benefit communities? How can communities control research processes and outcomes? Rather than external researchers determining research agendas, community-based research involved communities identifying research priorities and shaping research design. The methodological approach involved collaboration, partnership, and shared decision-making. Research teams were often composed of community members and outside researchers working together. Research processes might involve community members as researchers, conducting interviews or ethnographic observation with their own communities. Knowledge produced through research was shared with communities first and was controlled by communities regarding publication and dissemination. Research benefits might include training community members in research methods, creating research reports useful to communities, supporting community advocacy or policy goals, or contributing to community cultural preservation and revitalization.

## Ethnographic Setting

Indigenous community-based research was conducted with diverse indigenous communities globally: Native American/American Indian communities in North America, First Nations in Canada, Aboriginal communities in Australia, indigenous communities in Latin America, and other indigenous groups. The research addressed diverse topics: cultural preservation, health disparities, environmental knowledge, educational outcomes, and other concerns communities identified as priorities. Research often was conducted in rural areas where indigenous communities maintained territorial presence, though increasingly also in urban areas where indigenous communities had relocated or where indigenous urban identities were being asserted. The ethnographic settings varied widely, reflecting the diversity of indigenous communities and their circumstances globally.

## Key Findings

Community-based research demonstrated that indigenous communities had valuable knowledge, priorities, and perspectives that should shape research agendas. The research revealed that indigenous communities often had conducted their own research on health, environment, education, and other domains; community-based research often built on and learned from indigenous knowledge systems and research traditions. The work revealed the importance of community control and accountability in research: when communities controlled research, they were able to ensure that research addressed their actual priorities and that research produced knowledge useful to communities. Community-based research also generated findings specific to particular communities: documenting indigenous knowledge about environmental management, revealing health disparities and their causes, documenting educational challenges and community strategies for addressing them. Beyond specific research findings, community-based research generated methodological insights about collaborative research: how to build genuine partnerships, how to address power imbalances, how to balance academic and community knowledge systems, and how to produce research that was rigorous by community standards as well as academic standards.

## Ethnographic Examples

Community-based research projects documented diverse collaborations. One project involved a First Nations community in Canada conducting research on health disparities. Community members and academic researchers worked together to design the research, with community members identifying health priorities and research questions. The research revealed that particular health conditions were disproportionately affecting community members and identified community-based factors (economic conditions, environmental exposures, access to services) contributing to disparities. The research was directed toward policy advocacy and toward community health program development. Another project involved an Aboriginal community in Australia conducting research on environmental knowledge and land management. Community elders and younger community members worked with outside researchers to document indigenous knowledge about land management and ecological relationships. The research produced materials usable by the community for teaching, for land management planning, and for asserting indigenous land rights. Another project involved a Latin American indigenous community conducting research on educational outcomes and on barriers to education. Community members researched their own community, identifying factors supporting and hindering educational success, and developed recommendations for addressing barriers.

## Theoretical Contribution

Community-based research contributed theoretical understanding that challenged anthropology's and research's traditional power relationships. The work demonstrated that indigenous communities had sophisticated knowledge systems and research traditions that deserved respect and partnership. It contributed to decolonial theory by showing how research could be conducted differently—not extractively but collaboratively, not for external researchers' careers but for community benefit. The work also contributed to understanding that research "quality" should be assessed not only by academic standards but by community standards: whether research addressed community priorities, whether it produced knowledge useful to communities, whether it was conducted ethically and respectfully.

## Methodological Legacy

Community-based research pioneered collaborative and participatory methodologies, establishing practices for genuine partnership in research. The work contributed to establishing ethical guidelines for research with indigenous communities, emphasizing community control, informed consent, and benefit-sharing. The work influenced broader anthropological and interdisciplinary practice toward greater attention to ethics and accountability.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has engaged with community-based research critically. Some scholars have noted that terms like "collaboration" and "partnership" could be used rhetorically without genuine power-sharing actually occurring. Others have noted that community-based research, while increasingly practiced, still faces institutional barriers within universities and funding agencies that prioritize academic over community concerns. Indigenous scholars have continued to develop and refine decolonial research methodologies, extending beyond mere collaboration to more radical critiques of academic knowledge systems. Despite these critiques and ongoing challenges, community-based research represents a significant shift in how anthropological and interdisciplinary research is conducted. For contemporary anthropologists, community-based research represents an ethical commitment and a practical approach to conducting research that benefits communities and that challenges anthropology's colonial legacies. The work exemplifies how anthropology can be reformed toward greater responsibility and accountability to the peoples and communities anthropologists work with.
