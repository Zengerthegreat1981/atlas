# Deep South

*Deep South: A Social Anthropological Study of Caste and Class* (1941), by Allison Davis, Burleigh B. Gardner, and Mary R. Gardner, is a landmark ethnographic study of race in the Jim Crow South. The book analyses the interplay of caste and social class in Natchez, Mississippi — a town the authors called "Old City" (in "Old County") — and it is considered a foundational text in the social-scientific study of racial discrimination in the United States and in the theory of race as caste. Its central argument is that the South's racial order was not simply a system of prejudice or of economic exploitation, but a caste system: a rigid hierarchy of ranks assigned at birth, maintained by law, custom, and violence, within which class distinctions operated as a secondary system. A Black professional might outrank a poor white person in education and income, the authors showed, yet still be required to show deference to that white person in public, because caste overrode class.

The study was carried out under the direction of W. Lloyd Warner, whose Yankee City project provided its model and whose interest in caste — developed during his fieldwork in Australia — shaped its questions. The fieldwork was conducted in the mid-1930s by a small interracial team, working in a society where racial boundaries were enforced by violence. Allison Davis, a Black anthropologist trained at Harvard, and his wife Elizabeth Stubbs Davis lived among the Black population of Natchez; Burleigh and Mary Gardner, a white couple also trained at Harvard, lived among the white population; and St. Clair Drake, a young Black researcher, worked among lower-class Black residents. Studying caste in Mississippi at that time carried real risks, especially for the Black researchers.

## Method

The method was participant observation of a kind that required the researchers to live inside the system they were studying. The Davises and the Gardners attended church services, joined clubs, visited homes, and observed the daily interactions — in shops, on the street, at work, and in court — through which caste was enforced. Because the researchers were themselves divided by the colour line, they could observe both sides of the system: the Davises had access to the Black community that no white researcher could have obtained, while the Gardners could observe white life from within. The team also collected statistics on income, occupation, church membership, and land tenure, and they interviewed hundreds of residents.

## Key Findings

The first finding is the distinction between caste and class itself. The authors argued that the fundamental division in Natchez was between white and Black people, a division that functioned as a caste system: white people received the larger share of economic and social rewards and regarded Black people as lesser, while Black people bore a larger share of onerous duties and were required to show deference. This division was maintained not only by law but by etiquette — forms of address, seating, and service — and by the threat of violence for those who violated it. The second finding is that class operated within each caste. The book examines socio-economic class among the white population in detail, showing a hierarchy from planters and professionals down to poor whites, and it devotes a chapter to class among the Black population, finding that similar stratifications and tensions existed there, including a Black elite of educated professionals and successful business owners who in some respects ranked socially above poor whites.

A third finding concerns the economic basis of the system. The authors showed that sharecropping and the tenant-landlord relationship functioned to maintain caste divisions: the economic dependence of Black tenants on white landowners reinforced the social hierarchy, and the legal and political systems — the courts, the police, and local government — enforced it. The book's analysis of how an economic system and a system of racial rank reinforce each other has been influential far beyond the study of the South.

## Reception and Influence

*Deep South* was published by the University of Chicago Press in 1941, following John Dollard's earlier study of caste and class in a Mississippi town. Gunnar Myrdal's *An American Dilemma* (1944) and St. Clair Drake and Horace Cayton's *Black Metropolis* (1945) drew on the same tradition of community study, and Drake, who had been a fieldworker on the project, went on to become a leading scholar of race in America.

The book's influence was renewed in the twenty-first century by Isabel Wilkerson's *Caste: The Origins of Our Discontents* (2020), which drew on the *Deep South* tradition to argue that the United States, like India and Nazi Germany, is organised by a caste system. Wilkerson's account of the Natchez researchers brought the study to a wide new audience. *Deep South* is now recognised as a classic of American social science, notable both for its analysis and for the circumstances of its production: a study of racial hierarchy carried out by an interracial team at a time of widespread segregation, including in much of American higher education. Its central insight — that race in America functions as a system of inherited rank, not merely as a set of attitudes — remains one of the most consequential ideas in the sociology of race.
