# Tok Pisin: A Classic Case Study in Creolization

Tok Pisin (from English "talk pidgin") is an official language of Papua New Guinea, with English and Hiri Motu. It is one of the three closely related English-lexifier languages of Melanesia, with Bislama in Vanuatu and Pijin in Solomon Islands. Because missions, newspapers, and administration wrote it down from an early period, its expansion is documented in texts as well as in modern grammars.

## Plantation origins

The language grew in the late nineteenth century out of Pacific pidgin English used by laborers from many islands who had no single mother tongue in common, on plantations in Queensland and Samoa and then in New Guinea. Germany declared a protectorate over northeastern New Guinea in 1884 and lost the colony in 1914; some German words remain in Tok Pisin from that period. English supplies most of the lexicon. Kuanua (Tolai), spoken around the Gazelle Peninsula of New Britain, is the substrate most often cited for grammar and for a block of vocabulary. Other local languages and, in a smaller way, Chinese-pidgin contact contributed items. Early plantation pidgin was narrower in function than present-day Tok Pisin. "Simplified to the extreme" overstates even that stage: pidgins still have stable conventions, but the early range of topics and constructions was limited.

## Expansion

Peter Mühlhäusler described **structural expansion** as the growth of vocabulary and grammar once a pidgin is used beyond the plantation. Forms that grammars of Tok Pisin now treat as basic include the plural marker *ol*, the transitive suffix *-im*, the future marker *bai*, the completive *pinis*, and a pronoun set that distinguishes inclusive *yumi* ("we, including you") from exclusive *mipela* ("we, not you"). That inclusive/exclusive contrast is characteristic of many Oceanic languages and is a standard exhibit of substrate influence. Expansion came through continued use among workers, missions, and colonial administration, before any large group of children had Tok Pisin as a first language.

## Nativization

From the mid-twentieth century, and increasingly with urbanization around Papua New Guinea's independence in 1975, children in some towns acquired Tok Pisin as a first language. In the usual terminology, nativization is what makes a creole. Much of the country still uses Tok Pisin as a second language and a lingua franca, so the language as a whole is often called an expanded pidgin, with creole varieties in some urban families. Those first-language varieties are not a separate language with a different name. Rural and urban speech differ, and urban Tok Pisin takes in more English. The record is unusually full; it does not capture every decade or every region equally.

## What the case can test

Comparisons of Tok Pisin with English and with Oceanic languages are used to weigh superstrate and substrate accounts of creole structure. The pronoun system and other grammatical patterns are hard to treat as English grammar with words removed. At the same time, much of the lexicon and some of the word order are English in origin. The case does not decide a single universal bioprogram for all creoles. It does show a contact language becoming a medium of politics, schooling, and home life while remaining in contact with its lexifier.
