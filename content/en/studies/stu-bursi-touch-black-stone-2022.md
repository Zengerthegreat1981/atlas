# Bursi (2022): Debating Touch in Early Islamic Pilgrimage — The Black Stone

A textual-historical study by Adam Bursi (`thk-adam-bursi`), published in 2022 in the journal *The Senses and Society* (vol. 17, no. 1) under the title "'You were not commanded to stroke it, but to pray nearby it': debating touch within early Islamic pilgrimage." It examines how early Muslim scholars of hadith and law debated the propriety of touching and kissing the Black Stone and other parts of the Ka'ba during circumambulation (*tawaf*), and what this debate shows about the place of touch within the sensory culture of early Islam.

## The Central Issue

The early sources preserve a well-known report attributed to the caliph 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, who is said to have addressed the Black Stone: "I know that you are a stone that neither harms nor benefits; had I not seen the Prophet kissing you, I would not have kissed you." The report was used in later discussion to guard against attributing intrinsic power to the stone, while still grounding the practice in the Prophet's example. Studies of early Islamic ritual note that sacred objects and places could raise worries about pagan associations, and that touch was therefore a point where the community's boundaries and the proper understanding of ritual were negotiated rather than settled from the outset. Bursi's article approaches these disputes as a case of sensory history, attending to which forms of touch were endorsed, restricted or questioned, and by whom.

## Position in the Field

This study brings an Islamic case into the discussion of touch and embodiment in pilgrimage (`sch-touch-embodiment-pilgrimage`), which Coleman and Elsner opened in a cross-religious comparison (`wrk-pilgrimage-past-present-coleman-elsner`). Unlike that contemporary and comparative work, it is a textual-historical study of the first Hijri centuries rather than a field ethnography. It also belongs to the wider turn toward sensory history in the study of early Islam, which is methodologically parallel to the study of religious smell in this section (`sch-religious-olfactory-studies-harvey`).

The study is a descriptive-analytical account of a historical debate within the Islamic heritage, not a normative judgement on the legitimacy of touch.
