# Asylum Anthropology & Borders

A subfield of social anthropology that studies national borders not as mere fixed geographic lines, but as **sites of living social and political action**. Borders are produced and reshaped daily through the practices of security states, and through the experiences of asylum seekers and undocumented migrants who cross them or are detained there.

## From the Border as Line to the Border as Practice

This field builds on a general theoretical shift in border studies. Borders are no longer understood only as fixed political lines on maps, but as **bordering**: a set of changing institutional and everyday practices. These include checkpoints, walls, digital surveillance technologies, and systems that classify asylum seekers as "legal" or "illegal." Together they continuously reproduce the border far from the official line drawn on the map.

## Land of Open Graves: Jason De León's Study

The American anthropologist Jason De León offered one of the most influential ethnographic works in this field, *The Land of Open Graves: Living and Dying on the Migrant Trail* (2015). It documents the suffering and death of undocumented migrants in the Sonoran Desert between Mexico and the American state of Arizona. De León combined traditional ethnography (participant observation in migrant shelters, and interviews with migrants before and after they crossed the border), archaeology (analysis of the material objects that migrants leave behind in the desert), and forensic science (study of the bodies in the desert environment). The result is a biting critique of the policy of **"Prevention Through Deterrence"** pursued by American authorities since the 1990s. This policy deliberately pushes migrants toward extremely dangerous desert routes in order to deter them. Field evidence documents that the policy kills large numbers of people without achieving its stated goal of effectively deterring migration.

## Institutional and Legal Dimension

This literature also examines the gap between the international legal framework for the right of asylum (the 1951 Geneva Convention) and the actual practice of national immigration systems. According to many researchers, these systems turn the asylum application process itself into a long and exhausting bureaucratic process of exclusion. It tests the asylum seeker's ability to prove a "well-founded fear" according to strict legal criteria, which may not reflect the complexity of their actual experience.

## Theoretical Significance

This field contributes to the critique of the sovereign nation-state as a neutral entity that manages its borders with purely administrative rationality. Instead, it reveals how border policies intersect with structural violence that directly and tangibly affects migrants' bodies and lives. It also shows how, in daily practice, "borders" turn into actual spaces of death rather than abstract sovereign symbols.
