# Samoan Kinship and Adolescence: Mead on Pacific Kinship Systems

Margaret Mead's *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928) reported her fieldwork in the Manu'a islands of American Samoa in 1925-26 and used the Samoan case to argue that the stresses of adolescence in the United States were shaped by culture rather than inevitable features of biology. The book became one of the most widely read works of anthropology, and was later at the centre of a prolonged controversy over its accuracy. Her more technical monograph *Social Organization of Manu'a* (1930) covered kinship, rank and political organization.

## Context

Mead (1901-1978) studied with Franz Boas and Ruth Benedict at Columbia University, and Boas, who was engaged in debates against biological determinism, encouraged a study of whether adolescent turmoil appeared in another culture. Mead, aged 24, lived in Manu'a, chiefly on the island of Ta'u, for about nine months in 1925-26. She concentrated on adolescent girls in three villages, among whom she studied a group of about 68 young women, and also gathered material on social organization and rank. The islands were administered by the United States Navy. She learned some Samoan, relying heavily on interviews and on living in a household.

## Samoan Social Organization

Samoan society is organized around extended families (*aiga*) headed by titled chiefs (*matai*), with bilateral (cognatic) kinship in which a person may claim membership in several descent groups. Rights to land and titles can be claimed through either parent, and individuals have some flexibility in residence and affiliation. Rank, titles and village councils (*fono*) structure public life. Mead's account of kinship and household organization is related to her arguments about adolescence, since children were cared for by many relatives, including older siblings, in households that included more than the parents.

## Argument of *Coming of Age in Samoa*

Mead argued that Samoan adolescent girls passed into adulthood with less conflict than their American counterparts. She attributed this to several features of Samoan life: children were raised in large households where relations with adults were diffused rather than concentrated in parents; sexual matters were treated more casually and premarital experimentation was tolerated; there were fewer sharp choices of occupation or beliefs; and rank and kinship provided clear places for young people. She concluded that "adolescence" as a period of stress was in large part a product of Western society. This was an influential argument for the role of culture in human development.

## The Mead-Freeman Controversy

In *Margaret Mead and Samoa* (1983), the New Zealand anthropologist Derek Freeman, who worked in Western Samoa from the 1940s, argued that Mead had misrepresented Samoan society, which he described as more competitive, concerned with virginity and prone to violence, and that her informants had misled her. The claim was widely publicized, and the dispute became a symbol of the nature-nurture debate. Responses by Lowell Holmes, who had restudied Ta'u in 1954, and by Paul Shankman (2009) concluded that Freeman's account was in important respects unfair and one-sided: Holmes had found much of Mead's account to be broadly accurate, though others identified weaknesses in her sampling, brevity of fieldwork and reliance on a small sample of girls. Freeman's own evidence also came from a different part of Samoa, a different period and a different focus. The dispute included a claim in the 1980s that a Samoan informant had said that she and a friend had joked with Mead, which is itself contested.

## Significance

Whatever the verdict on specific details, the book's broad argument that culture shapes the experience of adolescence is now widely accepted, and the controversy is used to teach about the use of evidence, the politics of anthropological representation and the limits of short-term fieldwork. The book has also been criticized for its popular style, its generalization from a small sample and its framing of Samoa as a contrast to American society.
