# Reinhart & Rogoff's Growth in a Time of Debt (2010)

A short paper published in 2010 by Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff in the *American Economic Review* (in its Papers and Proceedings issue, as a summary of a conference presentation). It claimed the existence of a critical threshold of the ratio of public debt to GDP above which economic growth slows sharply. It later became one of the most influential and politically cited economic papers of the decade after the global financial crisis, before its statistical foundation was severely shaken by the discovery of substantive methodological errors.

## The central claim: the 90% threshold

The authors drew on a broad historical debt database covering 44 countries over roughly two centuries; the growth comparison reported in the paper covered 20 advanced economies in the postwar period (1946-2009). They divided observations into four categories by the ratio of public debt to GDP (below 30%, between 30% and 60%, between 60% and 90%, and above 90%). They found that the average economic growth rate in the last category (debt exceeding 90% of output) was noticeably and surprisingly lower than in the other three categories, which appeared similar in their performance among themselves. This result was widely interpreted in political and media discourse as evidence of a sharp "critical threshold" near 90%, above which public debt turns from a gradual burden into a sharp and sudden brake on growth.

## Rapid political adoption

The result acquired exceptional political influence quickly, especially in Europe and the United States. It was repeatedly cited by policymakers and politicians (including European commissioners and American members of Congress) to justify fiscal austerity programmes and cuts in public spending in the aftermath of the 2008 crisis and the European sovereign debt crisis, as "scientific" evidence of the danger of exceeding a roughly specified critical debt threshold. Yet the original paper itself was a relatively short conference summary that had not yet undergone full peer review at its first publication.

## Discovery of errors: partial collapse of the statistical foundation

In 2013, Thomas Herndon, a graduate student at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, together with Michael Ash and Robert Pollin, while attempting to replicate the original results, discovered substantive methodological errors in the data spreadsheet (Excel) on which the original study relied: the unintentional exclusion of data from several countries (Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada and Denmark) due to an error in the range of an averaging formula, in addition to other methodological decisions on weighting observations that raised further questions. Herndon, Ash and Pollin showed that correcting these errors weakens the original relationship considerably: a negative association between very high debt and growth remains, but without any sharp "threshold" near 90% in particular, and with a slowdown that is far more gradual and much less severe than the original study suggested.

## The effect on the public debate on austerity

This discovery sparked wide media and academic controversy about the extent to which influential public policies had been based on a statistical result that did not survive independent re-examination. It led many to reconsider the empirical basis of the argument for strict fiscal austerity in that period. Reinhart and Rogoff themselves, however, defended the continued validity of the general direction of their result (a negative relationship between very high debt and growth) even after acknowledging the specific technical errors discovered in the original table.
