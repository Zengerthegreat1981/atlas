# Why We Post

A comparative ethnographic research project led by Daniel Miller at University College London (2012–2016, funded by the European Research Council), in which nine anthropologists carried out parallel fieldwork at nine sites to study how people use social media in their daily lives.

## Method

Each researcher lived in their assigned site for fifteen months, following a shared research framework so that the sites could be compared. This avoided relying on a single case (often American or European) and generalizing it to the world. The sites included an industrial town and a rural area in China, a town in northern Chile, a village in south India, a village in England, and locations in north-east Brazil, southern Italy, south-eastern Turkey and Trinidad.

## Central Finding

The project concluded that digital technology itself (a single platform such as Facebook) does not impose a uniform global behavior. Instead, it is culturally shaped in each context according to its particular social values. Uses of social media in the field sites differed considerably, not because of technical differences between platforms, but because of the local social and cultural contexts into which they were integrated. The team reported, for example, that migrant factory workers in industrial China used social media to sustain ties with home and to cope with the conditions of work, while in some Turkish and other settings concerns about family and community reputation, particularly of women, shaped how people used it.

## Impact

This project helped establish the method of multi-site comparative ethnography in digital anthropology, and offered an empirical alternative to the quick generalizations common in popular discourse about the "effect of social media" worldwide. The project's findings were published in a series of books issued by UCL Press and made available open access, including the comparative volume *How the World Changed Social Media* (2016).

Each member of the team also published a separate case study of their own site alongside the comparative book, so that readers can follow the detailed ethnographic findings for each country individually. This reinforced the methodological transparency of the project and allowed other researchers to review the evidence for each case in detail.

A later volume, *Visualising Facebook* (Miller and Sinanan, 2017), examined the visual content of posts across the field sites, adding a comparative analysis of images to the ethnographic evidence for cultural diversity in the use of the same platform.
