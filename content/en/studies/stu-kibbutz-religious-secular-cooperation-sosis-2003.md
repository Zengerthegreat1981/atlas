# Costly Signalling and Cooperation in Religious and Secular Communities (Sosis and Bressler, 2003)

A comparative quantitative study by Richard Sosis and Eric Bressler, published in *Cross-Cultural Research* in 2003, that tested the costly signalling hypothesis of religion. The authors compared the longevity of utopian communes in the United States, mostly founded in the nineteenth century, some religious and some secular, and asked whether the number of costly requirements imposed on members predicted how long a commune survived. The entry's slug refers to kibbutzim because the same research programme also produced a companion study of Israeli kibbutzim (Sosis and Ruffle, 2003), summarised below.

## Method and Result

Using historical data on a large sample of communes, Sosis and Bressler found that religious communes survived longer than secular ones. Among religious communes, longevity was associated with the number of costly requirements imposed on members, such as dietary restrictions, restrictions on communication with outsiders, and distinctive dress or sexual practices; among secular communes no comparable relationship appeared. The authors read this as support for the hypothesis that costly commitments function as signals of commitment that sustain cooperation. Because the study measures organisational survival rather than cooperation directly, the link between the two is inferential.

## The Kibbutz Companion Study

Sosis and Ruffle (2003, *Current Anthropology*) studied religious and secular kibbutzim in Israel using behavioural economic games. They reported that members of religious kibbutzim cooperated more in a common-pool resource game than members of secular kibbutzim, and that among religious men, frequency of communal synagogue attendance was associated with greater cooperation. These results are often cited alongside the commune-longevity study, though the two use different data and methods.

## The Mechanism of Costly Signalling

Costly signalling theory, derived from evolutionary biology (notably Amotz Zahavi's "handicap principle"), holds that ritual commitments that are apparently costly, such as time spent on repeated prayers or restrictions on diet and dress, can function as a signal that is hard to counterfeit of a person's commitment to the group, because someone who is not committed will not usually bear such costs without an immediate benefit. The theory offers a proposed solution to an evolutionary puzzle: how cooperative trust arises among unrelated individuals in large groups, where direct verification of each person's intentions is difficult.

## Methodological Objections

The central difficulty is that religious and secular communities were not assigned their status at random. In the commune data, religious groups may have differed from secular ones in leadership, shared origin, kinship ties, economic organization or the kind of people they attracted, and any of these could affect survival. Statistical controls can address only what the historical record measures. A count of costly requirements also treats unlike demands as comparable and may miss how strictly they were observed; surviving records may be fuller for longer-lived groups. Demanding rules could reflect already high commitment as well as produce it. The finding that costs predicted longevity only among religious communes also needs an additional explanation, such as the sacred meaning attached to religious demands, which signalling theory alone does not supply.

In the kibbutz study, members may have selected into religious communities because they were already inclined to cooperate. Synagogue attendance is associated with sex and religious role, so comparing men and women does not isolate ritual from other differences, including collective study or social contact. A one-time economic game measures a narrow behaviour among fellow members and need not generalize to daily life or to outsiders. These points limit causal conclusions without showing the reported associations to be false.

## Impact and Caution

The studies have been widely cited in the evolutionary anthropology and cognitive science of religion as examples of testing evolutionary hypotheses about religion with comparative and behavioural data. They are correlational, and religious and secular communes differed in other respects, so the findings are generally treated as supportive rather than conclusive.
