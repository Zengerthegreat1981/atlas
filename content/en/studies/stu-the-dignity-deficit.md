# The Dignity Deficit

"The Dignity Deficit" is the title under which this entry gathers sociological work on dignity, respect and class. No single published study under that title has been confirmed for this entry, so it describes the established research it refers to.

## The Research Tradition

Sociologists have long argued that inequality involves a struggle over respect and moral worth as well as over income. Richard Sennett and Jonathan Cobb's *The Hidden Injuries of Class* (1972), based on interviews with working-class men in Boston, described how people who felt unable to command respect through occupation or education experienced their position as a personal failing and developed a strong concern for self-respect and independence. Michèle Lamont's *The Dignity of Working Men: Morality and the Boundaries of Race, Class, and Immigration* (2000), based on interviews with white and Black working-class men in the United States and France, showed how they defined their worth through moral standards such as discipline, responsibility and honesty, and drew boundaries against those they saw as lacking them. Arlie Russell Hochschild's *Strangers in Their Own Land* (2016) described a "deep story" among conservative white Louisianans, who felt that others were "cutting in line" ahead of them and that liberals disdained them.

## Why It Matters

This literature shifts attention from the distribution of money to the distribution of recognition. It suggests that economic decline, stigma and a meritocratic culture that attributes outcomes to individual effort can leave people with a damaged sense of worth, with consequences for politics, family life and health. The evidence in these studies comes largely from interviews in specific groups and places, so their findings describe patterns of meaning and not population averages.

## An Essay of the Same Name

A work bearing this title is Arthur C. Brooks's essay "The Dignity Deficit: Reclaiming Americans' Sense of Purpose", published in *Foreign Affairs* in March/April 2017, when Brooks was president of the American Enterprise Institute. It is a policy essay, not an empirical sociological study, and it is distinct from the research tradition summarised above. Brooks argues that a sense of dignity depends on being needed by others. He presents the deficit as especially acute among working-class men, many of them white and living in rural and exurban parts of the United States, and connects their alienation with support for Donald Trump in the 2016 election. In his account, the War on Poverty fell short because it did little to make people needed, and the remedy is not simply for government to devise more ways of helping them; policy should be judged by whether it enables people to contribute. His diagnosis and proposals reflect a particular political position and should be weighed as argument.
