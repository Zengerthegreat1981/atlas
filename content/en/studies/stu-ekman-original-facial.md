# Ekman's Original Cross-Cultural Facial Expression Studies

Are the expressions on our faces — the smile of happiness, the widened eyes of fear, the curled lip of disgust — the same everywhere on earth, or does each culture learn its own way of showing emotion? For most of the twentieth century, anthropologists assumed the answer was culture: facial expressions were thought to be learned conventions, as arbitrary as language. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the American psychologist Paul Ekman set out to test that assumption, and his field studies among isolated peoples in Papua New Guinea produced one of the most famous — and most contested — findings in the science of emotion: that a small set of basic emotions is recognised from the face in every culture tested, including cultures with almost no contact with the outside world.

## The Researcher and the Question

Paul Ekman, a clinical psychologist then at the University of California, San Francisco, came to the question through the work of Silvan Tomkins, who had argued that a small number of innate affects were the primary human motives, each with its own facial pattern, and through Charles Darwin, whose book The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872) had proposed that emotional expressions are evolved, inherited and shared across humanity. Ekman's question was therefore a direct challenge to the dominant view: if expressions are learned, then people who have never seen a Western face should not be able to read one; if they are innate, they should.

Ekman's principal collaborator was Wallace V. Friesen, with whom he would later develop the Facial Action Coding System. The fieldwork took place between 1967 and 1971, and it had two stages. First, in 1969, Ekman, Sorenson and Friesen showed photographs of posed emotional faces to observers in five literate cultures — the United States, Brazil, Chile, Argentina and Japan — and found high agreement about which emotion each face showed. But that result could be explained by shared exposure to Western media. The decisive test required people who had never seen a film, a television programme or a magazine: the South Fore people of the Eastern Highlands of Papua New Guinea, a preliterate group with little contact with the Western world, and, in related work, the Dani of what was then West Irian.

## Method: Stories Instead of Words

The method had to be redesigned for participants who could not read and who had no words for the researchers' emotion categories. Ekman and Friesen borrowed a procedure that avoided emotion vocabulary altogether. A translator read each Fore participant a short, culturally appropriate story — his friend has come and he is happy; his child has died and he is sad; he is about to fight and he is angry — and then asked the participant to point to the photograph, from a small set, that matched the story. The photographs showed posed expressions of the six emotions Ekman took to be basic: happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise and disgust. In a reverse test, Fore participants were asked to pose the expressions described in the stories, and their faces were photographed and shown to American students, who identified them at similarly high rates.

The results, published in Ekman and Friesen's 1971 paper "Constants Across Cultures in the Face and Emotion" in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, were striking. Fore adults and children matched the photographs to the stories at rates far above chance for most emotions (for example happiness, anger, disgust, and sadness). The one systematic failure was instructive: Fore adults could not reliably distinguish fear from surprise. Ekman read the overall pattern as support for his "neurocultural" theory: a universal facial affect programme, inherited and wired into the nervous system, produces the expressions, while culturally learned "display rules" govern when and how strongly they are shown.

## Key Findings

The first finding was universality of recognition: the same facial configurations were associated with the same emotions in every culture tested, including one with minimal exposure to Western media. For Ekman, this was evidence that the six basic emotions are biologically built in, each with a distinct universal signal, a characteristic physiology and an evolved function — a claim he formalised in his 1992 paper "An Argument for Basic Emotions."

## Reception, Criticism and Influence

The studies made Ekman famous and helped establish the modern science of facial expression. Their most important institutional legacy is the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), developed by Ekman and Friesen in 1978, which decomposes any facial movement into anatomically defined "action units" and which became a standard measurement tool in research on emotion, psychiatry, and the study of deception.

But the universality claim has been contested from the start, and the contest is itself part of the studies' significance. James A. Russell, in a 1994 review in Psychological Bulletin, argued that the apparent cross-cultural consensus was largely an artefact of the forced-choice method: when participants must choose from a small set of emotion words or photographs, agreement is inflated, because the method prevents them from offering alternative interpretations. Lisa Feldman Barrett's research programme, and the Himba study by Gendron and colleagues (2014) — in which members of a remote Namibian group sorted facial expressions in ways that did not match Western emotion categories — suggested that the same face can be read very differently when the forced-choice frame is removed.

## Why It Matters

Ekman's original studies matter for three reasons. Scientifically, they turned an old philosophical question — are emotions natural or cultural? — into an empirical programme, and they gave that programme a method and a measurement system that are still in use. The Fore studies are the reference point against which every later claim about emotional universality is measured, including the Himba study that challenges them. That is why they belong in this Atlas as a pair: the claim and its counter-claim, held together, are the content.
