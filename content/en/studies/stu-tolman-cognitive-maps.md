# Edward Tolman's Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men Study

A seminal program of experimental investigations in animal learning conducted by Edward Chace Tolman and colleagues at the University of California, Berkeley. By demonstrating the phenomenon of "latent learning" in complex spatial mazes, Tolman challenged Clark Hull's and John B. Watson's orthodox stimulus-response (S-R) behaviorism, proposing that organisms acquire internal, field-like spatial representations—termed "cognitive maps"—rather than mechanical chains of motor reflexes.

## Context and Experimental Design

During the 1920s and 1930s, mainstream American behaviorism asserted that all learning consisted of strengthening connections between environmental stimuli and peripheral motor responses through drive reduction or contiguous reinforcement. Tolman advocated for a "purposive behaviorism" that recognized cognitive, molar-level intervening variables between stimulus input and behavioral output.

In their classic 1930 experiment, Tolman and Charles H. Honzik tested 36 male rats in a complex 14-unit T-maze containing multiple cul-de-sacs (blind alleys). The rats were assigned to one of three conditions:
1. **Regularly Rewarded Group (HR - Hungry Rewarded, n = 14):** Received food in the goal box at the end of each daily trial throughout the experiment.
2. **Regularly Non-Rewarded Group (HNR - Hungry Non-Rewarded, n = 10):** Explored the maze once daily but found no food in the goal box, being removed after a set period.
3. **Delayed-Reward Group (HNR-R - Hungry Non-Rewarded then Rewarded, n = 12):** Received no food reward during Days 1 through 10, exploring the maze without apparent incentive. Beginning on Day 11, food was placed in the goal box for every subsequent daily run.

The experimenters recorded the number of errors (entering a blind alley) and the time required to navigate from the start box to the goal box on each daily run.

## Results and Latent Learning

The behavioral curves revealed a striking divergence across groups:
- The **Regularly Rewarded group** exhibited the standard, gradual reduction in errors and running time predicted by reinforcement theories.
- The **Non-Rewarded group** showed only marginal reductions in errors over the first ten days, wandering through the maze alleys at a leisurely pace.
- On Day 11, food was introduced to the **Delayed-Reward group**. On Day 12—after a single rewarded trial—their error scores dropped precipitously, immediately matching and in some trials exceeding the performance of the group that had been rewarded continuously for eleven days.

Tolman reasoned that the rats in the delayed-reward condition had acquired extensive knowledge about the spatial layout of the maze during their unrewarded explorations (latent learning), but had no motivation to translate this learning into rapid performance until food was introduced. This decisively separated **learning** (the internal acquisition of cognitive structure) from **performance** (the overt behavioral execution driven by motivational incentives).

## Cognitive Maps and Spatial Orientation

Synthesizing decades of experimental work in his landmark 1948 paper "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men," Tolman posited that the brain does not operate like an old-fashioned telephone switchboard connecting incoming stimuli to outgoing motor responses. Instead, incoming impulses are worked over and elaborated in a central control room into a tentative, cognitive map of the environment, indicating routes, paths, and environmental relationships.

Tolman supported this map concept through subsequent spatial shortcut experiments (e.g., Tolman, Ritchie, & Kalish, 1946). When rats trained on a winding, roundabout path found their familiar route blocked and were presented with a radiating array of novel alternative paths (a "sunburst maze"), the animals preferentially selected the direct path that pointed geographically toward the hidden food location. This demonstrated flexible place learning rather than rigid motor response learning (e.g., repeating a specific sequence of left-right muscle turns).

## Modern Neurobiological Validation

Tolman's cognitive map hypothesis anticipated the cognitive revolution by two decades and received physical neurobiological validation decades later. In 1971, John O'Keefe and Jonathan Dostrovsky discovered "place cells" in the CA1 and CA3 subfields of the mammalian hippocampus—neurons that fire selectively when an animal occupies specific spatial locations in an environment. In 1978, O'Keefe and Lynn Nadel published *The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map*, providing a detailed neural architecture for Tolman's psychological construct. The subsequent discovery of grid cells in the entorhinal cortex by Edvard and May-Britt Moser (awarded the 2014 Nobel Prize alongside O'Keefe) completed the cellular confirmation of an internal metric mapping system in the mammalian brain.
