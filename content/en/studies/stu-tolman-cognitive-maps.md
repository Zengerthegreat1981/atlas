# Edward Tolman's Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men Study

A historic line of experiments that anticipated the cognitive turn in psychology; Tolman argued that rats in mazes do not merely learn mechanical stimulus-response (S-R) links through reinforcement, but build an 'internal cognitive map' of the place through 'latent learning' without direct reward.

## The three-group maze experiment

The rats that received no reward for several days showed an immediate and striking jump in maze-running speed once food was introduced after the tenth day, indicating that they had been learning about the maze during the unrewarded trials.

## Challenge to strict stimulus-response behaviorism

Tolman's work pressed behaviorism to acknowledge intermediate processes and representations between the external stimulus and the behavioral response.

## Replication status

The 'cognitive map' hypothesis has been replicated behaviorally across decades of spatial-learning studies, and it received neural support after his death (1959), when John O'Keefe and Jonathan Dostrovsky reported 'place cells' in the rat hippocampus in 1971, which was later interpreted as a possible physiological basis for the idea of a cognitive map (O'Keefe shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this line of work).

## Detailed maps versus motor chains
Tolman argued that rats do not merely learn a rigid motor chain of successive turns (left, then right, then left), but build a comprehensive spatial representation resembling a mental map of the environment as a whole. This conclusion was supported by other experiments (Tolman, Ritchie and Kalish, 1946) in which rats trained on a roundabout route chose a direct path in the right direction when it was opened, a behavior hard to explain with a simple stimulus-response chain but consistent with a flexible internal spatial representation.

## The experimental design in detail
Tolman and his colleague Charles Honzik divided rats into three groups that ran a complex maze daily for weeks (the 1930 study): one group received a food reward at the end of the maze from the first day, a second group received no reward at all throughout the experiment, and a third group received no reward for the first ten days and was rewarded from the eleventh day. The always-rewarded group showed regular gradual improvement, as reinforcement-based theory predicts, while the third group showed little improvement during the first ten days but, once the reward was introduced, improved abruptly and soon performed about as well as the group rewarded all along.
