# A Multidisciplinary Study of Ramadan Fasting

This file addresses a line of research that combines nutritional physiology and religious anthropology to examine the fasting of the month of Ramadan (the fourth pillar of Islam) from two complementary angles: its effect on the body, and its social and spiritual meaning for those who fast. It is descriptive and is not medical advice.

## Definition

Fasting in Ramadan is an annual obligation on every adult Muslim who is able. It requires abstaining from food, drink and sexual intercourse from dawn until sunset throughout the ninth month of the lunar Hijri calendar. It is based on the Qur'anic verse in Surat al-Baqara: "O you who believe, fasting has been prescribed for you as it was prescribed for those before you, that you may become righteous" (Qur'an 2:183).

## Historical Context

Because the calendar is lunar, the month of Ramadan moves through the seasons of the solar year over a cycle of about thirty-three years. The length of the daily fast therefore varies with season and latitude, from roughly eleven or twelve hours in winter in many regions to well over sixteen hours in summer at high latitudes. This created a specific research area on how the human body adapts to fasting of variable duration and season.

## Main Ideas and Method

Physiological research on Ramadan has examined body weight, blood glucose, blood lipids and sleep and circadian rhythms. A widely cited overview is Trepanowski and Bloomer (2010, *Nutrition Journal*), which reviewed the health literature on religious fasting in general, including Ramadan, and noted that results vary with study design, diet and the timing of meals. Variation is linked in part to the pre-dawn (*suhur*) and breaking-fast (*iftar*) meals and to the kind of food consumed at them. In parallel, anthropological studies such as André Möller's ethnography *Ramadan in Java* (2005) show that changes in the rhythm of sleep, work and family gatherings during the month (staying up after the *tarawih* prayers, collective iftar meals) are socially and religiously important in their own right.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

In its jurisprudential aspect, this line of research draws on a vast exegetical and legal heritage concerning the rules of fasting (exemptions for illness and travel, and the question of determining the true dawn). This makes the study necessarily multidisciplinary: jurisprudential, physiological and anthropological at once.

## Impact and Critique

The generalization of results remains limited by variations in climate and day length among Muslim countries, and by the diversity of study populations and designs. Medical and jurisprudential rulings on exemptions from the fast are therefore made case by case, in light of individual circumstances, rather than from a single universal rule.
