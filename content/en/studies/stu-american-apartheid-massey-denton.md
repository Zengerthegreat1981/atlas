# American Apartheid

A comprehensive statistical-sociological study by Douglas Massey and Nancy Denton (published 1993), which documented with unprecedented quantitative precision the persistence of actual racial residential segregation in American cities decades after the abolition of official legal segregation. It identified this residential segregation, and not cultural factors alone, as the fundamental structural cause of the formation of the poor Black urban "underclass."

## Method and Content

Massey and Denton analyzed detailed census data across several decades, using precise statistical indices to measure "residential segregation" (such as the Dissimilarity Index). They documented that the major American cities remained residentially segregated to a very high degree between Blacks and whites even after the abolition of official racist laws, through continuing institutional mechanisms (discrimination in mortgage lending, the practices of real estate agencies, government housing policies). They argued that this continuing residential segregation is the decisive structural mechanism (and not supposed cultural factors, as the Moynihan report partly suggested) that concentrates Black poverty geographically in neighborhoods entirely isolated from job opportunities, good education and useful social networks. They argued that segregation interacts with rising poverty to produce concentrated poverty, which compounds disadvantage across generations.

## Context and Extensions

The study came in the wake of the Kerner Commission report (1968), which warned that America was moving toward "two societies, one black, one white, separate and unequal." A quarter century later it offered precise quantitative evidence that this residential separation had not receded as much as hoped despite anti-discrimination laws (such as the Fair Housing Act of 1968). Massey and Denton distinguished five dimensions of residential segregation (evenness, exposure, concentration, centralization and clustering) and introduced the concept of "hypersegregation" for metropolitan areas where Black residents were segregated on most of these dimensions at once. They found such hypersegregation in several large northern cities (including Chicago, Detroit and Newark), whose levels of segregation were as high as or higher than in many southern cities that had formerly had legal segregation. The book complemented William Julius Wilson's study *The Truly Disadvantaged* (1987), which focused more on the economic factors in the formation of the underclass, while Massey and Denton argued that residential segregation was an independent causal mechanism in its own right, and that Wilson's emphasis on class and deindustrialization underplayed it.

## Impact

This study provided influential systematic statistical evidence for the argument that continuing racial residential segregation is a central structural mechanism behind geographically concentrated Black urban poverty, though the relative weight of segregation and of economic restructuring remains debated. It became a basic reference in the sociology of race, the city and poverty, and informed later debates on combating housing discrimination and promoting mixed-income and racially integrated housing.
