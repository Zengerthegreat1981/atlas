# Hagen Ceremonial Exchange: Women and Men in Melanesia

Marilyn Strathern (born 1941) carried out fieldwork among the Hagen of the Western Highlands of Papua New Guinea from 1964-65, often alongside her husband Andrew Strathern, whose own work on the moka is treated in a separate entry. Her early study *Women in Between* (1972) examined Hagen women's roles in a male-dominated exchange system, and her later, theoretical *The Gender of the Gift: Problems with Women and Problems with Society in Melanesia* (University of California Press, 1988) reconsidered anthropological accounts of gender and exchange using Melanesian material, with the Hagen as an important case.

## Ethnographic Context

The Hagen are a Highland people whose prestige economy centres on the **moka**, a delayed, competitive exchange of pigs and valuables between men (see the Moka entry). Women raise pigs and cultivate sweet potato, but the large public transactions are conducted mainly by men, who also claim credit for them. Strathern's early work asked how Hagen women stand in relation to this world; she described them as "in between", linking men of different clans as wives, sisters and mothers.

## The Argument of *The Gender of the Gift*

- **Critique of Western assumptions.** Strathern argued that anthropological analyses of Melanesian societies, including feminist ones, had imported Western ideas: the individual as owner, society as a collection of individuals, and gender as inequality between male and female "interests". She proposed instead a contrast between a *gift economy*, where persons and things are bound up in relations, and a *commodity economy*, where things are alienable and persons are autonomous.
- **The dividual person.** Melanesian persons, she argued, are not conceived as bounded individuals but as "dividual" or composite, constituted by relations and detachable parts; a gift carries part of the giver and creates a relation, so that giving does not simply transfer an object between separate owners. The term "dividual" was borrowed from work on India by McKim Marriott and applied by Strathern to Melanesia.
- **Gender.** Male and female are treated as elements that can be combined or separated in persons and acts; exchange events make relations visible, and gender is a way of making them so. Gender relations are not reducible to the domination of one sex over another, though the book does not deny inequalities.

## Relation to Gift Theory

Strathern re-reads the legacy of Marcel Mauss, who wrote of obligations to give, receive and return, and of Malinowski's Kula work, arguing that these analyses still assumed a person who has property and exercises choice. In her account, gift-based societies conceive of persons and things differently, so that "the gift" is a relation that shapes the persons involved.

## Reception and Debate

The book was highly influential in anthropology, feminist theory and the study of Melanesia. It has also been criticized: for the difficulty of its prose; for treating "Melanesia" as a unity and using a stylized model of the person; and for the use of the term "dividual," which some regard as an analytic device. Mark Mosko developed the idea of the "partible" person, while Edward LiPuma argued that persons combine dividual and individual aspects. Feminist critics disputed whether it understated women's experience of inequality.
