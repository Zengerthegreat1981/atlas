# Everything in Its Path

An ethnographic and legal study by Kai Erikson (published 1976), which documented the social (and not only material) destruction caused by the collapse of a coal-waste dam in the Buffalo Creek valley in West Virginia in 1972. The flood submerged adjoining mining villages entirely within minutes.

## Method and Content

Erikson was engaged as a sociological consultant by the lawyers acting for the survivors in their lawsuit against the Pittston Coal Company. His research drew on interviews with survivors and on observation of the valley, documenting how the disaster destroyed not only houses and material property but the whole valley "community" as a living social fabric. The close neighborly networks and mutual trust built over generations in small adjoining mining communities were suddenly scattered when survivors were resettled in scattered temporary housing far from one another. Erikson distinguished between "individual trauma" (direct personal psychological harm) and "collective trauma" (the tearing of the shared social fabric itself, which had provided meaning and mutual support). He argued that collective trauma, the loss of the "community" itself as a living framework of social support, may be more painful and longer-lasting than direct material harm, and that the resettlement of survivors in scattered temporary housing aggravated this social destruction instead of easing it.

## Impact

This study is a classic of the sociology of disasters and gave the influential concept of "collective trauma" a prominent place in the field, becoming a widely used analytical tool in studying how societies respond to natural and technological disasters. It also fed into later discussion of post-disaster rehousing and the value of preserving existing community networks.

## Context and Background

The disaster occurred on 26 February 1972, when a slag-heap dam owned by the Pittston Coal Company collapsed in the Buffalo Creek valley in West Virginia. It released a wave of water and coal waste that submerged 16 adjoining mining villages within a few minutes, killing 125 people and displacing thousands of survivors. Contrary to the common image of mining communities as poor, socially disintegrated areas, these communities were densely tied neighborhood networks formed over generations of shared work in the mines, intermarriage and daily mutual support. It was this living social bond whose sudden disintegration Erikson documented. The survivors' suit was settled out of court, with financial compensation for the survivors. But Erikson emphasized that the most precious thing he documented was not the material loss but the loss, impossible to compensate financially, of an entire social fabric.
