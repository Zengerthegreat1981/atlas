# Street Corner Society

## Historical Context and Author

William Foote Whyte published *Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum* in 1943, after three and a half years of fieldwork in Boston's North End. The district, which Whyte called Cornerville, was an Italian immigrant neighbourhood that outsiders — social workers, journalists, city officials — described as disorganised, a slum of poverty and crime. Whyte, a young Harvard Junior Fellow who later took his doctorate at the University of Chicago, set out to test that description from inside. The book appeared in the middle of the Second World War, when American sociology was turning toward survey research and quantitative methods. Whyte (1914–2000) came to the North End as a young Harvard fellow in 1936 and lived there for three and a half years. A social worker introduced him to "Doc," a young Italian-American who became his guide and closest friend. Whyte studied Italian, bowled with the corner gangs, and attended weddings and funerals until he was accepted as a regular.

## Core Argumentative Structure

The book's thesis is that Cornerville is not disorganised at all: it has a complex, coherent social structure that outsiders cannot see because they judge it by middle-class standards. Whyte maps the neighbourhood's organisation through its groups. The corner boys — young men who hang out on street corners, loyal to their gang and its leader — and the college boys — young men pursuing education and upward mobility — are not random collections of individuals but structured groups with hierarchies, norms, and obligations. Above them stand the rackets and the political machine, which link the neighbourhood to the wider city: the racketeer provides jobs, loans, and protection, and the politician trades favours for votes. The conclusion is that the slum is organised, but organised differently.

## Method and Key Concepts

Whyte's method is participant observation, carried out over a long period and at close range. He lived in the neighbourhood, learned the language, and took part in daily life — bowling, drinking, attending meetings — while keeping detailed field notes. His approach shares the Chicago School's insistence that the sociologist study the world from the point of view of its inhabitants. He was candid about the method's costs: the danger of "going native," the ethical problem of observing illegal activity, and the difficulty of writing honestly about people who had trusted him. In later editions he added appendices describing how the research was done. The book's central contribution is the concept of informal social organisation: groups without formal rules, charters, or offices can still be highly structured. The corner gang has a hierarchy — a leader, lieutenants, followers — maintained by obligations, favours, and reputation rather than by written rules. The rackets, meanwhile, function as a parallel system of welfare and order.

## Evidence and Findings

The evidence is the texture of daily life, recorded in field notes over three and a half years. Whyte documents the bowling scores that reveal the gang's hierarchy — bowling performance tracked a man's rank in the group. He describes the rituals of borrowing and lending, the code of loyalty that forbids informing, and the elaborate etiquette of the street corner. He traces the political machine through the ward boss who trades favours for votes, and the rackets through the numbers game that funds them. He shows how the corner boys' apparent idleness is a structured social life with its own rules, and how the college boys' pursuit of education puts them at odds with the neighbourhood's values. The findings are not statistics but patterns, demonstrated through cases that accumulate into a picture of a working social order.

## Influence and Continuing Relevance

*Street Corner Society* is a classic text of urban ethnography and a model for qualitative sociology. It established participant observation as a legitimate method for studying groups that surveys cannot reach, and it influenced generations of ethnographers — from Elliot Liebow's *Tally's Corner* (1967) to Elijah Anderson's *Streetwise* (1990) and Sudhir Venkatesh's *Gang Leader for a Day* (2008). Its argument that apparently disorganised communities have their own order shaped the sociology of deviance, urban sociology, and the study of gangs. It also influenced the sociology of organisations: Whyte's analysis of informal hierarchy anticipated later work on how organisations really work, as opposed to how their charts say they work. Whyte himself went on to a career in industrial relations at Cornell University and to participatory action research, including a study of the Mondragon cooperatives written with Kathleen King Whyte (*Making Mondragón*, 1988). Its analysis of gangs, informal economies, and political machines still speaks to debates about policing and urban poverty.

## Critical Assessments and Limitations

Critics note that Whyte's picture of Cornerville is overwhelmingly male: the corner boys and the racketeers are men, and women's lives appear mainly at the margins. The book's focus on the neighbourhood's internal order can also underplay the forces that produced its poverty — discrimination, the housing market, the city's neglect. Some have questioned whether Whyte's closeness to Doc compromised his judgement, making him an advocate for his informants rather than an observer of them. In 1992 Marianne Boelen's restudy of Cornerville questioned parts of Whyte's account, and Whyte published a reply in the same journal. And the book's claim that the slum is organised, while true, risks romanticising hardship — order is not the same as justice. The book also raises ethical questions that have only grown sharper: Whyte observed illegal activity and had to decide what to record and publish, and he protected informants by changing names, but the neighbourhood was identifiable and Doc's identity became known. Modern ethnography has developed stricter rules about consent and confidentiality, and some of Whyte's choices would not be accepted today. Defenders reply that Whyte never claimed Cornerville was just, only that it was not chaos — and that this distinction was exactly what the sociology of his day needed to learn.
