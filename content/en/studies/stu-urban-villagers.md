# Urban Villagers

*The Urban Villagers: Group and Class in the Life of Italian-Americans* (1962), by the sociologist Herbert J. Gans, is one of the most influential community studies in American sociology. Gans lived for eight months in Boston's West End, a working-class Italian-American neighbourhood, just before it was demolished under urban renewal. He found not the disorganised slum the planners described, but a close-knit community with a rich social life — and his book became a classic argument against the destruction of working-class neighbourhoods in the name of progress.

## The Setting

The West End of the late 1950s was a dense district of tenements, small shops, and crowded streets near downtown Boston, home to several thousand Italian-American families. To the city's planners it was a slum: old buildings, low rents, and a population they regarded as a problem. Under the federal urban renewal programme the entire district was condemned, and in 1958 the bulldozers arrived. The residents were scattered across the city, and the West End was rebuilt as a complex of high-rise apartment towers. Gans arrived in time to observe the neighbourhood in its last months.

## Method

Gans's method was participant observation. He rented a room in the West End and lived there from 1957 to 1958, spending his days on the streets, in the shops and bars, and in residents' homes. He joined the local organisations, attended meetings, and talked with people about their families, their work, and their feelings about the coming demolition. He also interviewed residents systematically and kept detailed field notes. Gans was frank about his position as an outsider — a middle-class researcher living among working-class people — and about the fact that his study covered a community under sentence of destruction, which coloured everything he observed.

## Key Findings

Gans's central finding was that the West End was not disorganised at all. Its social life was organised around the peer group and the extended family, and its residents had developed a way of life that worked for them. People spent much of their time on the street and in public places, maintaining a constant round of casual sociability that outsiders mistook for idleness. The family was the centre of life, but the peer group — the circle of friends of the same sex and generation — was nearly as important, and it shaped behaviour from childhood to old age. Residents valued loyalty, generosity, and the ability to get along, and they distrusted formal organisations and middle-class reformers.

The planners' mistake, Gans argued, was to judge the West End by middle-class standards. They saw old buildings and concluded that the community was pathological; they did not see the social networks that made the neighbourhood function. Gans coined the term "urban village" for such a place: a community that combined the density and diversity of the city with the intimacy and mutual support of a village. The demolition, he showed, did not merely destroy housing. It destroyed a social system, and many former residents grieved for the lost neighbourhood for years afterwards — a finding confirmed by Marc Fried's later study of the psychological costs of relocation.

Fried's follow-up research gave Gans's argument its most human evidence. Interviewing former West Enders after the demolition, Fried found grief comparable to bereavement: people mourned not only their homes but the web of relationships that had made those homes meaningful. The street corner, the corner shop, and the neighbour's kitchen had been the infrastructure of their social lives, and no new apartment could replace them. This finding travelled well beyond sociology. It has been cited in planning debates ever since, whenever officials propose to clear a neighbourhood, and it remains one of the strongest empirical warnings against treating housing as though it were only shelter.

## Reception and Influence

*The Urban Villagers* became a landmark in urban sociology and in the critique of urban renewal. It was read alongside Jane Jacobs's *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961) as evidence that planners had misunderstood the neighbourhoods they were destroying, and it helped turn professional opinion against large-scale clearance. The concept of the "urban village" entered the language of sociology and planning, and the book remains a standard text in courses on urban sociology, community studies, and ethnographic method. Gans himself went on to study the Levittown suburbs, and the two books together established him as the leading American ethnographer of community life. More than sixty years after the West End was demolished, *The Urban Villagers* is still cited whenever a city proposes to clear a neighbourhood in the name of renewal.
