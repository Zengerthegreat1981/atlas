# The Unwinding

*The Unwinding: An Inner History of the New America* (2013), by the journalist George Packer, is a work of narrative journalism on the United States between 1978 and 2012, told through the lives of ordinary Americans and the institutions that failed them. Packer's title describes his thesis: over three decades, the bonds that once held American society together — stable industrial employment, strong communities, shared norms of fairness, and a functioning politics — came undone, and what replaced them was a country split between winners who adapted to the new economy and losers who were left behind. The book follows several main subjects in depth, interspersed with shorter biographical sketches of public figures, to show how large historical forces — deindustrialisation, the rise of finance, the housing bubble, and the growing power of money in politics — were experienced by individuals.

The principal subjects are Tammy Thomas, a Black factory worker in Youngstown, Ohio, whose working life is shaped by the decline of Youngstown's industrial base and who becomes a community organiser; Dean Price, a North Carolina tobacco farmer's son who becomes a biodiesel entrepreneur and an advocate of a new green economy; Jeff Connaughton, a Washington lobbyist and congressional aide who moves between idealism and the lure of organised money; Peter Thiel, the Silicon Valley billionaire and co-founder of PayPal, who arrives at a radical libertarian vision of the future; and a set of people caught in the distressed housing market of Tampa, Florida, during the subprime mortgage crisis. Around them, Packer places brief portraits of figures including the rapper Jay-Z, the politician Newt Gingrich, the Walmart founder Sam Walton, and the financier Robert Rubin, showing how each embodied or exploited the unwinding in a different way.

## Method

Packer's method is long-form narrative journalism informed by history. He spent years reporting and interviewing, returning repeatedly to his subjects as their circumstances changed, and he built the book from their own words, court records, company histories, and the public record. The structure is borrowed from John Dos Passos's *U.S.A.* trilogy of the 1930s, which alternated fictional narratives with biographies of real historical figures and newsreel fragments; Packer uses the same alternation of deep profiles and short sketches, with occasional passages of reportage, to give the book the feel of a collective biography of a country. The book is not a work of academic sociology, and Packer makes no claim to a representative sample. Its authority rests instead on the depth and duration of its observation and on the way its individual stories converge on the same pattern of institutional decay.

## Key Findings

The book's first finding is that deindustrialisation destroyed more than jobs. In Youngstown, the closure of the mills and factories removed the institutions — unions, churches, neighbourhood associations — that had organised working-class life, and Tammy Thomas's story shows how a community built around a single industry unravels when the industry leaves. The second is that the new economy rewarded a narrow set of skills and connections. Thiel's success in Silicon Valley and Connaughton's career in Washington illustrate a world in which wealth and influence concentrate among those who can navigate finance, technology, and politics, while the people who made things or served customers are left with low-wage service work.

A third finding concerns housing and debt. In Tampa, Packer follows families who bought homes with subprime mortgages, watched their values collapse, and then lost them to foreclosure, showing how the financial system turned shelter into a speculative asset and then into a source of ruin. A fourth is the corruption of politics. Connaughton's account of lobbying and campaign finance shows how money came to dominate Washington, so that the institutions meant to respond to the unwinding were captured by the interests that benefited from it. Finally, Packer finds that the unwinding produced not only material hardship but a loss of shared meaning: his subjects describe a country in which the old story of American progress no longer fits their experience, and in which anger, nostalgia, and a search for scapegoats have taken its place.

## Reception and Influence

*The Unwinding* won the 2013 National Book Award for Nonfiction. It was a *New York Times* bestseller and was widely praised for its reporting and its structure, and many reviewers noted that the book, published three years before the 2016 presidential election, seemed to anticipate the anger and disaffection that shaped it.

The book's influence lies in the way it gave a name and a narrative to a process that sociologists had described in more abstract terms — the hollowing out of the middle class, the decline of social capital documented by Robert Putnam in *Bowling Alone* (2000), and the rise of precarious work. By staying close to individual lives, Packer made those processes visible and human, and the book has become a standard text for readers trying to understand the social roots of American political polarisation. Its limitation, noted by some critics, is the same as its strength: it is a work of interpretation rather than measurement, and its claims about the nation as a whole rest on the persuasive power of a small number of stories rather than on systematic evidence.
