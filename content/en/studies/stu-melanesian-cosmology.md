# Melanesian Cosmology and Exchange Systems: Wagner on Person, Relation, and Meaning

Roy Wagner's ethnographic work among the Usen Barok of Papua New Guinea, published from 1981 onward, offering innovative analysis of Melanesian cosmology, personhood, and exchange systems. Wagner's work pioneered what he called "fractalist" approaches to understanding Melanesian societies, arguing that Melanesian social organization operated through principles fundamentally different from Western assumptions about persons and relationships. His work demonstrated that understanding Melanesian societies required radically rethinking anthropological categories and concepts.

## Publication Context

Wagner's major works were published from 1981 onward, synthesizing fieldwork conducted from 1967-1975 among the Usen Barok in Papua New Guinea. Wagner, trained at Columbia University under Morton Fried, had developed distinctive theoretical approaches to understanding non-Western peoples and cultures. His work appeared in a context where anthropologists were increasingly questioning whether anthropological concepts developed in the West could adequately capture non-Western understanding. Wagner's work was radical in arguing that anthropological concepts themselves needed to be invented for different cultural contexts; he could not simply apply Western concepts to understand Melanesian societies but needed to develop new theoretical frameworks appropriate to Melanesian logic. His work became influential in Melanesian anthropology and contributed to developing what some scholars call the "new Melanesian ethnography," characterized by attention to indigenous conceptualizations and by reflexivity about anthropological concepts.

## Research Question & Approach

Wagner's central research questions concerned how Melanesian peoples understood persons, relationships, and exchange. Specifically, how did Melanesian conceptions of personhood differ from Western concepts? How did Melanesian cosmologies work? How did exchange systems relate to understandings of persons and relationships? Rather than assuming that anthropological categories like "individual," "society," or "exchange" would adequately describe Melanesian realities, Wagner asked what categories would be appropriate for understanding Melanesian conceptions. His methodological approach combined intensive fieldwork, engagement with Melanesian philosophical concepts, and willingness to radically rethink anthropological frameworks. He conducted participant observation, interviewed community members about their understanding of fundamental concepts, and developed theoretical frameworks that could capture Melanesian logic without reducing it to Western categories. His approach was notably reflexive: he continually questioned whether his own interpretive frameworks were adequate or whether he needed to invent new categories to understand Melanesian thought.

## Ethnographic Setting

The Usen Barok inhabited the Rai Coast region of Papua New Guinea, a coastal and riverine region with tropical forests and limited population density. They engaged in horticulture, fishing, and trading, participating in regional exchange networks that connected them to neighboring groups. Usen Barok society was organized through kinship and through exchange relationships. They maintained cosmologies incorporating belief in spiritual beings, understanding of land and resources as embedded in spiritual relationships, and concepts of personhood that differed from Western individualism. Wagner conducted fieldwork in the 1970s, during Papua New Guinea's transition toward independence and in a context where Western colonial presence was significant but where Usen Barok communities maintained substantial autonomy and cultural distinctiveness. Wagner lived in a village setting and participated in daily life and community practices.

## Key Findings

Wagner argued that Usen Barok (and more broadly, Melanesian) conceptions of personhood differed fundamentally from Western concepts. Where Western thought tends to view persons as bounded individuals, Usen Barok conceived of persons as fundamentally relational—persons were constituted through relationships with others. Personhood was distributed and potentially mutable; a person could include elements of other persons, and persons could be divided or recombined. Exchange practices reflected this relational understanding of persons: exchange was not merely transfer of objects between separate individuals but was a way of creating and maintaining relationships and of constituting persons. Wagner documented that Usen Barok cosmology involved understanding of spiritual beings and forces that were not clearly distinguished from the human or natural realm. Land, resources, persons, and spirits were all interconnected in a cosmological system where boundaries were fluid. He documented practices of exchange, ritual, and kinship that expressed these cosmological understandings. Wagner also argued that Western anthropologists, in using their own concepts to interpret Melanesian societies, were inevitably distorting those societies through imposition of inappropriate categories. He advocated for anthropologists to invent new concepts specifically to understand particular cultures, rather than forcing cultures into pre-existing categories.

## Ethnographic Examples

Wagner provides descriptions of Usen Barok ritual practices and social relationships that exemplify his theoretical arguments. He documents exchange practices and how they related to understandings of persons and relationships. He describes ceremonies and how ceremonial activities expressed cosmological principles. While Wagner's writing style is notably theoretical and abstract (reflecting his philosophical approach to anthropology), he provides sufficient ethnographic illustration of his arguments about how Melanesian peoples understood fundamental aspects of existence differently from Western frameworks.

## Theoretical Contribution

Wagner's theoretical contributions were radical and philosophical. His argument that anthropologists needed to invent new concepts appropriate to particular cultures rather than applying universal categories contributed to debates about whether anthropological concepts could adequately capture non-Western realities. His work contributed to fractalist and other sophisticated theoretical frameworks for understanding Melanesian societies. His demonstration that personhood was not a universal category but was culturally constructed influenced broader anthropological understanding of how persons were constituted. His work contributed to questioning anthropological assumptions about what were fundamental and universal categories (persons, societies, exchange) and to recognizing that these categories themselves were culturally specific.

## Methodological Legacy

Wagner pioneered reflexive theoretical approaches to anthropology, questioning whether existing concepts could adequately understand non-Western peoples and advocating for anthropologists to develop new frameworks appropriate to particular societies. His approach influenced subsequent anthropological theory toward greater reflexivity about categories and concepts. His work demonstrated that theoretical innovation in anthropology needed to be grounded in ethnographic engagement.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has engaged with Wagner's work critically. Some scholars have found his theoretical abstractions difficult to translate or apply, questioning whether his concepts successfully captured Melanesian realities or whether they represented a kind of theoretical obfuscation. Others have praised his work for taking seriously non-Western epistemologies and for questioning anthropological universalism. Melanesian scholars and postcolonial critics have engaged with Wagner's work, sometimes finding his approach valuable for taking indigenous thought seriously, other times questioning whether his theoretical frameworks remain anthropological impositions. Despite these debates, Wagner's work remains influential in Melanesian anthropology and in theoretical anthropology more broadly. For contemporary students, his work exemplifies how anthropology can engage seriously with radically different ways of thinking and how anthropological theory needs to be responsive to ethnographic particularities.
