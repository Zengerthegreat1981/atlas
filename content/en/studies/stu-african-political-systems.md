# African Political Systems: Evans-Pritchard and Fortes on Nuer and Tallensi

A landmark edited volume that synthesized ethnographic studies of African political organization, published in 1940. Evans-Pritchard contributed ethnographic material on the Nuer of Sudan, while Meyer Fortes documented Tallensi political organization in Gold Coast (Ghana). The volume introduced the concept of "segmentary lineage systems" to explain how acephalous (headless) societies maintained political order without centralized authority, revolutionizing anthropological understandings of social organization and political authority.

## Publication Context

*African Political Systems* was published in 1940 by Oxford University Press as a collaborative edited volume bringing together ethnographic studies of diverse African political organizations. Evans-Pritchard, based at Oxford, and Fortes, then at Cambridge, collaborated to assemble contributions analyzing societies ranging from the Nuer and Tallensi (acephalous lineage-based systems) to the Bemba (chiefdoms) to the Zulu (kingdoms). The volume emerged from a context in which British anthropology was developing functionalist approaches to social organization, but it also reflected the specific colonial moment: these studies were conducted during the 1930s in territories under British colonial administration (Sudan for the Nuer, Gold Coast for the Tallensi), and the volume's theoretical innovations cannot be separated from their relationship to colonialism and administration. The editors' introductory essay explicitly framed the volume as addressing colonial administrators' practical questions about how "tribal" societies governed themselves—a framing that reflected anthropology's entanglement with empire even as it contributed to more sophisticated understandings of non-Western political forms. The volume became canonical in political anthropology and influenced administrative approaches in colonial territories.

## Research Question & Approach

The fundamental question Evans-Pritchard and Fortes posed was: How do societies without chiefs, kings, or centralized bureaucracies maintain internal order, resolve disputes, and constitute themselves as political units? This question directly challenged the then-prevailing assumption that politics required hierarchical authority structures. Their methodological approach involved intensive ethnographic investigation of how kinship systems generated political organization, how disputes were resolved, how territorial identity was constituted, and how inter-community relations were managed. Evans-Pritchard, working among the Nuer (Sudan) from 1930-1936, conducted meticulous documentation of pastoral practices, kinship genealogies, settlement patterns, and the practice of self-help justice (the feud system). Fortes, working among the Tallensi (Gold Coast) from 1934-1937, documented how descent groups organized political authority through senior elders and how ritual specialists (earth priests) embodied territorial authority. Both combined genealogical documentation with observation of political processes: how lineages segmented or reunited based on external pressures, how disputes escalated through levels of kinship organization, and how religious specialists maintained political authority despite lacking coercive power. The approach revealed that kinship was fundamentally political—descent group organization was simultaneously familial, economic, and political.

## Ethnographic Setting

The Nuer inhabited the swamps and grasslands of the southern Sudan (now South Sudan), practicing transhumant pastoralism based on cattle herding and seasonal movement. They numbered approximately 200,000-300,000 dispersed across a vast territory, organized into autonomous territorial sections without formal political hierarchy. Evans-Pritchard's fieldwork occurred during the 1930s, when British colonial administration was establishing itself in the region but Nuer communities retained substantial autonomy. The Tallensi occupied the savanna region of northern Gold Coast (Ghana), numbering around 35,000, practicing millet agriculture and livestock herding. Fortes' fieldwork coincided with colonial consolidation but before administrative integration into the colonial state structure. Both ethnographers faced significant challenges. Evans-Pritchard, working with limited interpreter support among pastoral peoples with whom he could not maintain continuous residence, developed innovative methods for reconstructing political processes through genealogical interviews and historical narratives. The Nuer initially regarded him with suspicion; gaining sufficient rapport for detailed political information required extended patience and demonstrated interest in Nuer concerns. Fortes achieved more continuous residence among the Tallensi and participated more regularly in community life, though as an outsider, particularly during ritual and political contexts where his presence was restricted. Both ethnographers worked in contexts where colonialism was reshaping political authority structures, complicating their documentation of "traditional" systems.

## Key Findings

Evans-Pritchard's analysis of Nuer political organization demonstrated that Nuer identity was constructed through overlapping segmentary lineage divisions rather than through territorial chiefs or centralized institutions. The Nuer conceptualized their political world through descent—they could recite genealogies linking them to apical ancestors and through those genealogies identified with patrilineal groups of varying scales. These groups (lineages of different orders) segmented: a man might identify first as a member of a particular household, then a clan section, then a larger tribe-like unit, depending on context. Crucially, Evans-Pritchard discovered that Nuer political identity was contextual and relational: two distant lineages would recognize their common patrilineal ancestry (and thus unity) only in opposition to a still more distant group. This "segmentary opposition" principle meant that Nuer society lacked permanent hierarchies but generated flexible political units for specific purposes. Disputes were resolved through self-help justice—if one lineage member was injured, his lineage collectively pursued compensation through the feud system, but compensation could also be negotiated through cattle payment mediated by respected elders. Fortes' analysis of Tallensi political organization demonstrated that while they lacked chiefs, political authority derived from religious specialists—the earth priest (*tenugan*) embodied the authority of the earth and conducted sacrifices on behalf of the territorial community. Authority was simultaneously mystical (derived from earth/ancestors) and practical (concentrated in senior lineage elders who made substantive decisions). Unlike the Nuer's fluid segmentary system, Tallensi political units were more territorially bounded, with earth priests anchoring territorial identity. Both studies revealed that politics in acephalous systems operated through kinship, ritual, and age hierarchies rather than through formal institutions—yet these systems effectively generated political order, maintained territorial boundaries, and resolved disputes.

## Ethnographic Examples

Evans-Pritchard documented specific conflicts and their resolution to illustrate the Nuer self-help justice system. He recorded a case in which a young Nuer man killed another in a fight; the victim's lineage demanded compensation from the killer's lineage, and the lineage elders negotiated payment of forty cattle to settle the matter. Importantly, Evans-Pritchard noted that Nuer themselves understood and debated the appropriate compensation—they did not view the system as automatic but as requiring judgment and negotiation. He describes another case in which a Nuer man injured a member of a distant lineage; because the lineages were not closely related, the injured man's lineage mobilized for warfare, yet settlement negotiations involved respected men from intermediate lineages who recognized common ancestry with both disputing parties. Evans-Pritchard explicitly recorded Nuer terminology for these political processes and their metacommentary on them. Fortes documented Tallensi ritual practices centered on the earth priest: he recorded the earth priest performing sacrifices before planting and harvest, in response to social conflicts believed to anger the earth, and during initiation ceremonies. One passage describes a divination session in which an earth priest diagnosed illness in a young man as resulting from his grandfather's buried corpse being disturbed; the priest conducted a ritual reburial, and the young man's health improved—a sequence that Fortes interprets as demonstrating how ritual specialists maintained authority through perceived efficacy and how ritual articulated with social structure (the young man's kinship position in relation to his grandfather). Fortes also records genealogical materials showing how Tallensi plotted their descent, frequently revising genealogies to reflect contemporary political alliances—suggesting that kinship was actively constructed through political processes.

## Theoretical Contribution

*African Political Systems* introduced the concept of segmentary lineage systems to anthropological theory, fundamentally shifting understandings of how non-state societies organized politically. The volume challenged the assumption that politics necessarily required centralized authority, demonstrating that acephalous systems maintained order through kinship organization, reciprocal obligation, and ritual authority. Evans-Pritchard's segmentary opposition model became enormously influential: the principle that political units defined themselves relationally—that Nuer identity was context-dependent rather than fixed—suggested that political organization was more flexible and contextual than fixed institutional arrangements. This insight reverberated through subsequent political anthropology scholarship on factions, fluid groups, and the performative constitution of political community. Fortes' analysis of how ritual and kinship constituted authority without formal hierarchy suggested that anthropologists must examine how power and authority operate through non-institutional channels—through age hierarchies, ritual expertise, and genealogical standing. Together, the volume's contributions demonstrated that kinship was fundamentally political: descent-group organization generated political units, determined authority positions, and shaped conflict resolution. This integration of kinship and political anthropology influenced decades of subsequent scholarship. The volume also contributed to developing functionalist approaches to social analysis by showing how different social institutions (kinship, ritual, economics) articulated to maintain social order. The political anthropology field, as a disciplinary subdomain, arguably originated with this volume's theoretical synthesis.

## Methodological Legacy

Evans-Pritchard and Fortes pioneered methodological approaches to studying stateless societies that became influential across anthropology. Evans-Pritchard's development of genealogical methods—using genealogy as an entry point to understand political organization—became standard practice in kinship and political anthropology. His systematic documentation of how genealogies structured territorial identity and dispute resolution demonstrated that genealogy was not merely kinship trivia but fundamental to understanding political processes. Fortes' careful documentation of ritual specialists' roles and authority structures pioneered methods for understanding how non-institutional power operated, influencing subsequent scholarship on priesthoods, shamanism, and ritual authority generally. Both ethnographers developed interview-based methods for reconstructing political processes when direct observation was impossible, particularly important for understanding historical changes and large-scale political movements that unfold over years. Their work established that effective ethnographic study of political systems required understanding of kinship, since kinship generated political categories and determined dispute resolution. This methodological integration subsequently influenced the field toward seeing societies holistically rather than in isolated domains. Additionally, the volume's comparative framework—bringing together diverse African societies to identify common principles—represented a disciplinary shift toward systematic comparison while remaining grounded in thick ethnographic description, rather than comparative abstraction separated from evidence.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has identified significant critiques of *African Political Systems*. Postcolonial anthropologists have questioned whether Evans-Pritchard and Fortes adequately contextualized their studies within colonial power relations—whether their representations of "traditional" political systems reflected pre-colonial organization or colonial-era realities shaped by colonial administrative presence and pressure. The segmentary opposition model has been criticized as overly neat, insufficiently accounting for the role of trade, religious networks, and other cross-cutting ties that complicated simple lineage-based segments. Feminist scholars have critiqued the volume's inattention to women's political roles, particularly in systems where women's control of agricultural production and women's associations wielded significant political influence. The volume's relative emphasis on formal political institutions (lineage structures, dispute resolution) over everyday political contestation and individual agency has also been questioned. Additionally, the theoretical prestige of the segmentary opposition model led to its misapplication to other societies where lineage organization operated differently. Despite these critiques, the volume retains enormous value as a foundational anthropological text that demonstrated sophisticated political organization in the absence of state structures, with methodological approaches still applied in ethnographic research. The genealogical methods developed and the insight that kinship constitutes political organization remain central to anthropological practice. For contemporary students, the volume exemplifies how intensive ethnographic documentation can generate broad theoretical insights, and the critiques of the volume provide instructive lessons about how anthropological analysis is shaped by historical moment and the anthropologists' own assumptions and blind spots.
