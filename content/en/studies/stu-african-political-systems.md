# African Political Systems: Fortes and Evans-Pritchard on Stateless and Centralized Societies

*African Political Systems* (1940), edited by Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard, is a landmark collection of ethnographic essays on indigenous African political organization. It is best known for setting out, in a comparative frame, how societies without a centralized government could nonetheless maintain order, and it is widely regarded as a founding text of British political anthropology.

## Publication Context

The volume was published in 1940 by Oxford University Press for the International African Institute. Its contributors, several of them trained or influenced by Malinowski or Radcliffe-Brown, wrote about societies they had studied in the field in the 1930s: Max Gluckman on the Zulu, Isaac Schapera on the Ngwato, Audrey Richards on the Bemba, K. Oberg on the Ankole kingdom, S. F. Nadel on the Kede of northern Nigeria, Günter Wagner on the Bantu of Kavirondo, Fortes on the Tallensi, and Evans-Pritchard on the Nuer. Much of this fieldwork took place in territories under British colonial administration, and the editors expressed the hope that the book would be of use to those who administered African peoples. Later critics have taken this as a sign of the discipline's entanglement with empire.

## Argument

In the introduction, Fortes and Evans-Pritchard divided the societies surveyed into two broad types. In the first, such as the Zulu, Ngwato, Bemba, Ankole, and Kede, there were centralized authorities, administrative machinery, and judicial institutions, so that cleavages of wealth, privilege, and status corresponded to the distribution of power. In the second, such as the Nuer, the Tallensi, and the Bantu of Kavirondo (notably the Logoli), there was no such government, and order depended on the balance between kinship groups and local communities. The introduction argued that in the second type political relations are expressed in the idiom of kinship and descent, and that the structure of lineages provides a framework for territorial groups. The volume's lasting contribution was to show that the absence of a state was not the absence of political order.

## The Nuer

In his chapter on the Nuer of the southern Sudan (now South Sudan), and more fully in *The Nuer* (1940), Evans-Pritchard described a cattle-keeping people whose territorial divisions, from the tribe down to the village, were organized by what he called segmentary opposition. Sections of a tribe were united in conflict with other tribes, but opposed to one another within the tribe, and the scale of loyalty depended on the context of a dispute. Evans-Pritchard presented this as a kind of balanced opposition that held the political system together without any central authority. Disputes, including homicide, were typically settled through payment of cattle, often mediated by a ritual figure called the leopard-skin chief, who had no power to enforce a decision. Evans-Pritchard's fieldwork among the Nuer took place in short periods between 1930 and 1936, under difficult conditions and under the pressure of British military operations in the region.

## The Tallensi

Fortes worked among the Tallensi of northern Ghana (then the Northern Territories of the Gold Coast) between 1934 and 1937. His chapter and later books, notably *The Dynamics of Clanship among the Tallensi* (1945) and *The Web of Kinship among the Tallensi* (1949), analyzed a society of patrilineal clans and lineages in which political and ritual authority were separated and linked. Alongside chiefs, whose office was in important part a product of colonial administration, the earth priests (tendaana) held ritual authority over land, linked to the ancestors and the earth. Fortes argued that the segmentary lineage structure was fundamental, but that ritual ties and ties of residence and kinship through women also played essential roles, so that the Tallensi did not conform neatly to the Nuer model.

## Impact and Method

The volume helped to define political anthropology as a field and encouraged the systematic comparison of African political systems on the basis of detailed fieldwork. The concept of the segmentary lineage system became one of the most influential models in the study of kinship and politics, and was applied, sometimes uncritically, to societies in New Guinea, the Middle East, and elsewhere. Methodologically, the contributors relied on long-term participant observation, on genealogies as a way of recording social structure, and on a comparative, structural-functionalist framework influenced by Radcliffe-Brown.

## Critique and Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has criticized the volume on several grounds. Its typology treated societies as stable, bounded systems and gave little attention to history, to the effects of colonial rule, or to conflict and change. Marshall Sahlins (1961) argued that segmentary lineage organization was better understood as a means of predatory expansion than as a mechanism of equilibrium, and Adam Kuper (1982) argued that the model of lineage theory was applied more rigidly than the evidence warranted. Feminist and other scholars have noted the volume's limited attention to women's political roles and to everyday political contestation. Despite these critiques, *African Political Systems* remains a foundational text, and its central insight, that order can be sustained by kinship, ritual, and balanced opposition rather than by a state, continues to shape debate in political anthropology.
