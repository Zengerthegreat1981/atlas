# Sidewalk

An ethnographic study by the sociologist Mitchell Duneier, based on several years of fieldwork (1992–1996) among Black street vendors of used books and magazines, many of them homeless, on Sixth Avenue in Greenwich Village, New York. Published in 1999, it won the C. Wright Mills Award of the Society for the Study of Social Problems.

## Method and Content

Duneier spent extended time on the sidewalk with the vendors, and briefly worked alongside them selling, documenting how these men, many of whom had been homeless and some of whom had histories of addiction or prison, built an informal social order of their own: tacit rules for claiming places on the sidewalk, cooperation among vendors, and the informal monitoring that Jane Jacobs called "eyes on the street". The book challenges the "broken windows" thesis of James Q. Wilson and George Kelling (1982), which treated visible signs of disorder such as panhandling and street vending as a prelude to crime and a justification for strict order-maintenance policing. Duneier argued that the vendors acted as a source of informal order on the block rather than disorder, and he examined how policies and local complaints affected them. With Harvey Molotch he also analysed how everyday interaction between residents and the poor can produce what they called "interactional vandalism".

## Context

Duneier, a sociologist at Princeton University, had earlier written *Slim's Table* (1992), an ethnography of Black working-class men in a Chicago cafeteria. *Sidewalk* continues the tradition of American urban ethnography associated with Elliot Liebow's *Tally's Corner* (1967), and it is notable for its methodological transparency. The book includes a long methodological appendix, discusses the ethics of fieldwork with marginalised people, and was accompanied by photographs by Ovie Carter. Duneier also describes how he checked his accounts, including by sharing drafts with participants.

## Impact and Critique

The book became a widely taught example of ethnography in public space and offered an influential critique of zero-tolerance approaches to street disorder. It also prompted methodological discussion about verification and ethics. Loïc Wacquant (2002) criticised Duneier, together with Elijah Anderson and Katherine Newman, for what he saw as moralistic framing and insufficient attention to the role of the state in shaping the lives of the urban poor, and others have asked how far a study of one block's vendors can generalise.
