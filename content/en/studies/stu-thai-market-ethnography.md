# Thai Market Ethnography: Ethnographies of Exchange, Gender, and Urban Economics

Anthropologists and other social scientists have studied Thai markets and trading as sites of economic exchange, gender and ethnicity. This entry gives a short overview of the main themes rather than the findings of one study. The literature is smaller than that on markets in Indonesia or Morocco, and much of it appears within broader work on Chinese-Thai commerce, gender and household economy, and economic change.

## Background

Markets and petty trade have long been important in Thai economic life. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries much of the commerce between rural producers and urban consumers was carried out by Chinese immigrants and their Thai-born descendants, a history analyzed by G. William Skinner in *Chinese Society in Thailand* (1957), who traced the gradual assimilation of the Chinese into Thai society over generations. John Embree's well-known description of Thailand as a "loosely structured social system" (1950), based on rural observations, drew attention to the flexible character of Thai social relations, and it influenced later discussions of trading relationships.

## Gender and Trade

Research on women in Southeast Asia, including the collection edited by Penny Van Esterik, *Women of Southeast Asia* (1982), documented the prominence of women in marketing and in household financial management in Thailand and neighbouring societies. Women are often vendors of food, vegetables and household goods and manage family finances, which is sometimes contrasted with a male-dominated religious and political public sphere. Scholars have argued that this gives women economic influence but does not necessarily translate into equal status or power in other domains.

## Comparative Frameworks

Clifford Geertz's studies of markets in Indonesia and Morocco (*Peddlers and Princes*, 1963, and the bazaar essay of 1979) and the literature on the "moral economy of trade" (Evers and Schrader, 1994), which looks at how ethnicity, trust and reputation structure market relations, have provided frameworks for studying trading communities in Southeast Asia. Common themes include the role of credit and personal relationships (such as long-term ties between buyers and sellers), the role of ethnic networks in commerce, and the effects of supermarkets, tourism and national and global markets on small traders.

## Critique

Scholars caution against generalizing about "Thai markets" as a single institution, since urban, rural, tourist and border markets differ greatly. An emphasis on women's trading can obscure the insecurity and low returns of informal work, and claims about the flexibility of Thai society have been criticized for exaggerating the contrast with more "tightly structured" societies.
