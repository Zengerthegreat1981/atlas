# Solow's Residual in Growth Accounting (1957)

**Robert Solow** published his landmark paper "Technical Change and the Aggregate Production Function" in 1957 in the *Review of Economics and Statistics*. It offered a simple quantitative accounting framework for decomposing the sources of economic output growth, which later became the foundation of the whole field of growth accounting in applied macroeconomics.

## Research design: an aggregate production function

Solow used a simple aggregate production function that relates total output to capital and labour. He assumed competitive factor markets, so that each factor is paid its marginal product and income shares can stand in for output elasticities, and applied this framework to annual data for the US private non-farm economy from 1909 to 1949. His aim was to decompose the growth of output per worker into two components: the part resulting from the increase in capital available per worker, and the remaining part not explained by the accumulation of directly measurable factors of production.

## The surprising result: the dominance of the unexplained residual

Solow found that the larger part of the growth in gross output per man-hour during the period (about seven eighths by his estimate, against about one eighth attributable to capital accumulation) was attributable to this "residual", which capital accumulation alone does not explain. He interpreted it as an indicator of the effect of technical progress in its broad sense: improvements in production methods, organization and accumulated technical knowledge over time, rather than merely a quantitative increase in the capital available per worker. This residual later became known as "Solow's residual" or "total factor productivity" (TFP), a standard measure of the unexplained part of growth; because it is a residual it also absorbs measurement error and omitted factors, so it is not a pure measure of technology.

## The methodological impact on growth accounting

This paper established the field of "growth accounting" as a standard analytical tool, which development economists, central banks and international institutions still use to decompose the sources of economic growth in any country into the contributions of capital, labour and total productivity. This allows systematic comparisons between economies that grow at seemingly similar rates for structurally very different reasons (pure capital accumulation versus genuine technical improvement).

## Later criticism and development

The model was later subjected to several methodological criticisms, most prominently the Cambridge capital controversy over how to measure aggregate capital itself. It was also noted that the "residual" may in fact hide neglected factors such as improvements in the quality of capital and labour themselves, rather than pure technical progress in the narrow sense. This led to the later development of more detailed growth accounting models that separate the quality of factors of production from their raw quantity. Nevertheless, Solow's basic framework, with its relatively simple accounting logic, has remained the standard reference on which most later analyses are built despite all these modifications.

## Presence in the debate on Arab growth

This growth accounting framework was later invoked in analyses of Arab economic growth experiences. In particular, it featured in the discussion of the difference between growth driven mainly by physical capital accumulation and labour (as in some Gulf oil economies) and growth driven by genuine improvement in total factor productivity. This distinction was regarded as an important indicator of the sustainability of economic growth, away from reliance on rent flows or the accumulation of imported capital alone.
