# Herndon, Ash and Pollin's Replication Study (2013)

A short but highly influential academic study, prepared by the graduate student Thomas Herndon under the supervision of his professors Michael Ash and Robert Pollin at the University of Massachusetts Amherst. It revealed fundamental methodological errors in the influential 2010 study by Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff on the relationship between public debt and economic growth. It is one of the best-known cases of "replication" in the modern history of economics.

## Context: A Coursework Exercise Turns into a Discovery

The work began as a routine coursework exercise for Herndon, a student in an applied statistics course, who was asked to try to reproduce the results of an influential economics paper as part of the course requirements. Herndon chose the Reinhart and Rogoff study precisely because of its enormous political influence. However, he repeatedly failed to obtain the same result from the publicly available data, which led him to request the original spreadsheet data directly from the authors. He obtained it after several attempts, and the episode fed a wider debate about data-sharing and transparency in policy-relevant economic research.

## The Errors Uncovered

On examining the original spreadsheet, Herndon, Ash and Pollin separated three problems. First, a formula averaged the wrong block of rows and dropped five countries from the high-debt mean: Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada and Denmark. Their data were in the file. Herndon, Ash and Pollin attributed about 0.3 percentage points of the published gap to this coding error alone. Second, early postwar observations with high debt and, in several cases, solid growth were absent for Australia (1946–1950), Canada (1946–1950) and New Zealand (1946–1949), without a reason stated in the paper. New Zealand's one remaining high-debt year in the original average had sharply negative growth, often cited as about −7.6 percent. Third, each country counted as one observation, however many years it contributed, so that single New Zealand year weighed as much as the United Kingdom's long high-debt record.

## The result after correction

Putting the three corrections together, Herndon, Ash and Pollin found average real growth in the above-90-percent category of about 2.2 percent a year, against the roughly −0.1 percent in Reinhart and Rogoff's published table. Growth was still lower than in the lower-debt bins, and it still tended to be weaker at higher debt ratios. What did not survive was the sharp break at 90 percent that had been widely quoted. Because the spreadsheet mistake was only a small part of the revision, descriptions that treat the whole 2.2-point shift as an Excel error overstate that one mistake. The selective years and the country weighting did more of the work. Reinhart and Rogoff accepted the coding error and argued that a negative debt-growth association remained in other specifications, including their separate work on historical episodes. The 2013 exercise did not show that debt is irrelevant to growth. It showed that the particular 90-percent cliff was not a robust summary of the spreadsheet.

## Political and Academic Impact

The study caused a wide media and political stir when released as a working paper in April 2013 (the peer-reviewed version appeared in 2014). It was regarded as evidence that an academic argument widely used to justify influential fiscal austerity policies around the world had not been as well-founded as it appeared. This prompted a broader debate about the importance of transparency in sharing research data, and the need to subject politically influential papers to independent review and replication attempts before relying on them to frame wide-reaching public policies.
