# Manufacturing Consent: Changes in the Labor Process under Monopoly Capitalism

An ethnographic study by Michael Burawoy, who worked as a machine operator for about ten months in 1974–1975 in the engine division of a South Chicago factory (called "Allied Corporation" in the book), the same plant in which Donald Roy had worked as a machine operator some thirty years earlier. Comparing his findings with Roy's, Burawoy argued that the organisation of the shop floor had changed and that workers' participation in shop-floor games helped to secure their consent to the labour process.

## Method and Content

Burawoy's fieldwork took place in 1974–1975. Roy had studied the same plant, under the pseudonym "Geer," in 1944–1945, which gave Burawoy an unusual chance to compare two periods of one workplace. He found that the game of "making out", in which operators try to reach production quotas under informal rules, was still central to shop-floor life. His interpretation, however, differed from Roy's. Games of this kind are not only a defence by workers against management; by absorbing workers' attention and giving them a sense of achievement, they also lead them to cooperate with the rules of the production process and obscure the extraction of surplus value from their labour.

## Theoretical Context

The book is a response to Harry Braverman's *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974), which argued that capitalist management steadily deskills work and strips workers of control. Burawoy asked instead why workers cooperate, drawing on Antonio Gramsci's idea of hegemony — rule that rests on consent as well as coercion — and applying it to the factory floor rather than to national politics. He distinguished earlier "despotic" regimes of production, in which discipline rested on the threat of dismissal under market competition, from the "hegemonic" regime that emerged in the twentieth century, in which collective bargaining, internal labour markets and grievance procedures gave workers some rights and tied their interests to the continuity of the firm. (The later phrase "hegemonic despotism" belongs to Burawoy's subsequent book *The Politics of Production*, 1985, and refers to a different argument about global capitalism.)

## Impact and Critique

Manufacturing Consent became a central work of labour process theory and of Marxist industrial sociology, shifting the question from why workers resist to why they cooperate with a system that exploits them. Burawoy later developed the "extended case method" in *Ethnography Unbound* (1991). Critics have pointed out that the argument rests on a single plant and a single occupational group, that it gives limited attention to gender, race and workers' resistance, and that "consent" may be better explained by economic necessity than by the games themselves.
