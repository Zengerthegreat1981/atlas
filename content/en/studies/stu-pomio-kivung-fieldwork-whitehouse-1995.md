# Whitehouse's Foundational Fieldwork: The Pomio Kivung Movement in Papua New Guinea (1995)

Harvey Whitehouse (`thk-harvey-whitehouse`) carried out ethnographic fieldwork in the late 1980s, through participant observation rather than distant quantitative survey, among members of the Pomio Kivung movement. This was a local new religious movement in East New Britain Province of Papua New Guinea, of a kind often labelled a "cargo cult" in the Melanesian literature (a label many anthropologists regard as problematic). The results of this work were published in his first book, *Inside the Cult: Religious Innovation and Transmission in Papua New Guinea* (1995). Some aspects were later elaborated in an article titled "Rites of Terror: Emotion, Metaphor and Memory in Melanesian Initiation Cults" (1996).

## The Field Observation: A Ritual Schism within the Movement Itself

Whitehouse described two contrasting patterns of religious practice in the same region. The main Pomio Kivung movement was dominated by routinised, frequently repeated ritual and verbal teaching, which he later called the "doctrinal" mode. A splinter group, by contrast, developed rarely performed but emotionally intense and sensory rites, which he later called the "imagistic" mode. He argued that participants in the splinter group remembered the rites as vivid individual episodes, while the doctrinal teaching of the main movement was absorbed as a general body of knowledge.

## From Field Observation to General Theory

This specific field contrast, between two phases within a single movement rather than between two different religions, formed the first empirical core on which the general theory of "modes of religiosity" (`con-modes-of-religiosity-theory-whitehouse`) was later built. Whitehouse developed it in later books such as *Arguments and Icons* (2000) and *Modes of Religiosity* (2004), and later helped lead work on large historical datasets (the Seshat: Global History Databank). This distinguishes Whitehouse from colleagues in the cognitive science of religion (Boyer and Barrett), who drew their theories mainly on laboratory experimentation in cognitive psychology. It is one of the clearer examples in this section of a theory that arose first from long-term ethnography and was only later tested with quantitative data.
