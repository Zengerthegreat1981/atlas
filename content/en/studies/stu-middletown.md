# Middletown

*Middletown: A Study in Modern American Culture* (1929), by the husband-and-wife sociologists Robert Staughton Lynd and Helen Merrell Lynd, is a classic community study in American sociology. The Lynds set out to study a single ordinary American city in exhaustive detail, as an anthropologist might study a village, in order to understand how modern American culture was changing. They chose Muncie, Indiana — a city of roughly 35,000 to 38,000 people in the 1920s, predominantly white and Protestant, with a small Black population and relatively few foreign-born residents — and renamed it "Middletown" to suggest its supposedly typical, middle-of-the-road character. Over about eighteen months of fieldwork beginning in January 1924, they and a small staff examined work, family, schooling, religion, leisure and local government. The result was a bestseller and a model for later community studies.

The study was commissioned by the Institute of Social and Religious Research, an organisation funded by John D. Rockefeller Jr., which had originally wanted a survey of religious life in a small American town. Robert Lynd, who had trained at Union Theological Seminary and worked in publishing, and Helen Lynd widened the brief into a study of the whole community. Their guiding question was how the town had changed over the previous thirty-five years, from roughly 1890 to 1925, a period in which Muncie had been transformed by the gas boom, the growth of manufacturing (notably the Ball brothers' glass works) and then the automobile industry. They organised their material around six basic activities, borrowed from anthropology: getting a living, making a home, training the young, using leisure, engaging in religious practices, and engaging in community activities.

## Method

The Lynds' method combined ethnography, surveys and documentary research. They lived in Muncie, attended church services, club meetings and school events, interviewed residents, and collected statistics on wages, housing, church membership, school attendance and consumption, along with newspapers and other local documents.

The method had a conspicuous limitation that later scholars have stressed: the Lynds studied only the white population of Muncie. They explained that they excluded the town's Black residents (and, to a large extent, the foreign-born) in order to study a relatively homogeneous population, a choice that reflected both the racial assumptions of the time and the study's design. The Lynds returned to Muncie in 1935 for a follow-up study, published as *Middletown in Transition: A Study in Cultural Conflicts* (1937), which examined the effects of the Great Depression. In the late 1970s a team led by Theodore Caplow, funded by the National Science Foundation, conducted a third study (Middletown III, including *Middletown Families*, 1982), and Ball State University's Center for Middletown Studies in Muncie now coordinates continuing research, including *The Other Side of Middletown* (2004), a study of the city's African American community.

## Key Findings

The central finding of the 1929 study was that class was the great dividing line in Middletown. The Lynds divided the population into a working class and a business class and showed that this division shaped nearly everything else: where people lived, what churches they attended, how they spent their leisure, and what they expected for their children. Work itself had changed profoundly: in 1890 many men worked in small shops or on farms, whereas by 1925 more worked in factories on schedules set by machines and managers, and the Lynds documented the effects of shift work, the decline of craft skills and the rise of a managerial class.

A second finding concerned the family and the young. The Lynds argued that industrialisation had changed domestic life: women were buying goods that had once been made at home and more of them worked for pay, children stayed in school longer and spent more time outside the home, and schools, employers and the mass media took over functions the family had once performed. A third finding was the persistence of traditional values alongside rapid material change. Despite the arrival of automobiles, radios and new consumer goods, the Lynds found that Middletown's moral and religious outlook had changed less than its habits: the town remained devout, conservative and suspicious of radical ideas, and its business class set the tone for public life.

## Reception and Influence

*Middletown* was an immediate popular and academic success, and it remains a widely cited work in American sociology. It established the community study as a central sociological genre and influenced later work such as W. Lloyd Warner's Yankee City studies, William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943) and Herbert Gans's *The Urban Villagers* (1962), and it brought the anthropological method of studying a whole community to the study of modern societies.

The study has also drawn sustained criticism. Scholars have questioned whether any single town could be "typical" of America, pointed out that the choice of a white, Protestant, Midwestern town built a narrow definition of Americanness into the study, and noted that the Lynds' own political sympathies shaped their account of the business class. Later Middletown research, including Middletown III and the work collected in *The Other Side of Middletown*, has corrected the record by studying the Black community the Lynds excluded and by examining how Muncie changed in the later twentieth century.
