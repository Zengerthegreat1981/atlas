# Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight

**"Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight"** is an essay by **Clifford Geertz**, first published in the journal *Daedalus* in **1972** and reprinted the following year in *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), where it became one of the best-known pieces of interpretive anthropology. Its subject is the cockfight of Bali — an illegal, bloody, and passionately followed contest between fighting cocks — and its argument is that the cockfight is a text in which the Balinese tell themselves a story about themselves. In Geertz's reading, the cockfight is not a sport, a pastime, or a primitive survival: it is a dramatisation of status, masculinity, and social hierarchy, and to understand it is to understand Balinese society.

Geertz arrived in Bali in 1958 with his wife, Hildred, and the essay opens with one of the best-known anecdotes in modern anthropology. The couple were watching a cockfight in a village when a police raid sent the crowd fleeing; the Geertzes, like everyone else, ran, and ended up taking refuge in a villager's compound. Having shared the villagers' flight, they were afterwards treated as people to be taken into account rather than as invisible outsiders, and the village's acceptance of them dates from this episode. The story is more than a charming opening: it is Geertz's statement of method. Understanding, he suggests, comes not from detached observation but from shared experience — from being, however briefly, inside the event rather than outside it.

## The Cockfight and Its Rules

The essay describes the cockfight in exact detail. A fight is staged in a ring, before a crowd of men, between two cocks fitted with steel spurs. The birds are owned by individuals, but the fight is not between birds: it is between their owners, and through the owners between the kin groups, villages, and status ranks they represent. Betting is the heart of the event, and Geertz distinguishes two kinds of bet. The centre bet, between the owners of the two cocks, is large and even; the side bets, made by individuals in the crowd, are generally smaller, are offered at odds, and are strongly influenced by the size of the centre bet. A man bets on the cock of his kinsman or his ally, and the pattern of side bets maps the social structure of the village: who is allied with whom, who is opposed to whom, who is rising and who is falling.

The fight itself is brief and violent, and the losing cock is usually killed. But the money, Geertz argues, is not the point. The sums wagered can be large, but the stakes that matter most are symbolic. What is at stake is prestige: the public affirmation of a man's standing, his courage, his loyalty to his group. The cockfight is a public stage on which the hierarchy of the village is displayed, contested, and reaffirmed.

## Deep Play and the Text of Culture

The essay's title comes from Jeremy Bentham, who used the term "deep play" for a game in which the stakes are so high that it is irrational to play at all. Geertz's point is that the Balinese cockfight is deep play in exactly this sense: from the standpoint of material interest, it makes no sense to risk so much for so little, and yet men play, passionately and repeatedly. The explanation is that the game is not about money but about meaning. The cockfight is a "sentimental education": a dramatisation of the themes that organise Balinese life — status rivalry, masculine pride, the tension between solidarity and competition — in a form that can be watched, felt, and understood.

This is the essay's famous methodological claim: that culture is a text, and that the anthropologist's task is to read it. Geertz compares the cockfight to a work of literature, in which a society's deepest concerns are given aesthetic form, and argues that it can be "read" in much the way a Western reader reads a great novel or play. The cockfight, he writes, is "a story they tell themselves about themselves." The comparison has been enormously influential: it licensed a generation of anthropologists to treat rituals, performances, and public events as texts to be interpreted, and, together with Geertz's essay on "thick description" (the detailed, layered account of an event's meanings), it shaped the method of interpretive anthropology.

## Reception and Influence

"Deep Play" became one of the most widely assigned essays in anthropology and a model of the interpretive essay. It has been praised for its literary brilliance, its analytical clarity, and its demonstration that a single event, closely observed, can illuminate a whole society. It also shaped the study of play, gambling, and sport, and its concept of deep play has been taken up in sociology, economics, and cultural studies.

It has also been criticised, and the criticisms are part of its fame. William Roseberry (1982) argued that the textual reading detaches the cockfight from Balinese history, political economy and social differences, and Paul Shankman (1984) questioned the explanatory value of Geertz's interpretive programme as a whole. Others have noted that the essay says almost nothing about Balinese women, who are absent from the cockfight and from the analysis, and that its picture of Bali is static, with little attention to history or to the Indonesian state. The essay retains its standing and the essay is now read not only as an interpretation of Bali but as a document in the history of anthropology itself.
