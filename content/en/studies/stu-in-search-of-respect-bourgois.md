# In Search of Respect: Selling Crack in El Barrio

A long-term ethnographic study by the anthropologist Philippe Bourgois, who actually lived for years in the East Harlem neighborhood ("El Barrio") of New York, directly engaged in the lives of local crack dealers.

## Method and Content

Bourgois and his family lived in the neighborhood for several years in the late 1980s, and he spent time daily with a group of Puerto Rican crack dealers and their families. He documented how the violent informal economy of drug selling, despite its grave risks, represents an alternative form of "entrepreneurship" that gives these young men a sense of "respect" and masculine dignity of which they were deprived in the formal legal labor market available to them: low-paid, low-status service jobs that require daily submission they feel humiliates their manhood. Drawing on concepts such as "structural violence" (introduced by Johan Galtung) and Pierre Bourdieu's "symbolic violence", he argued that historical structures of racial and economic discrimination (the closing of New York's factories, racial discrimination in hiring) produce conditions that make the violent drug economy an understandable choice within a very limited range of options.

## Impact

Through its sustained ethnographic immersion in a violent economy, the study made an influential theoretical contribution linking the analysis of macro-level "structural violence" with finely observed individual lived experience, and it became a basic reference in the anthropology and sociology of American urban poverty, race and the informal economy.

## The Contradiction Between Two Cultures and Work Ethics

Among the study's most prominent problems is its documentation of the experience of some crack dealers when they tried to move into formal legal jobs: they found themselves unable to bear the daily submission required (taking orders from supervisors who were younger or less "respected" on the street, and strict time discipline) despite their theoretical willingness to do honest work, which led many to return to the street economy that gives them greater autonomy and masculine dignity despite its grave risks. Bourgois showed that this contradiction is not an individual moral failure but the product of a structural collision between a street subculture that adapted to historical economic exclusion and the demands of a modern service labor market that does not respect their basic dignity.

Bourgois was personally exposed to real physical dangers during his fieldwork, and faced difficult ethical dilemmas about the limits of a researcher's participation in a criminal environment without actually becoming implicated in it, dilemmas he discussed openly in a lengthy methodological appendix to the book.

Bourgois later co-authored, with the photographer Jeff Schonberg, *Righteous Dopefiend* (2009), an ethnography of homeless heroin injectors in San Francisco, using a similar method.
