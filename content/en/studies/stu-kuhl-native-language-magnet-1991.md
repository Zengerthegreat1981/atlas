# Kuhl's Study of the Native Language Perceptual Magnet Effect (1991)

The psycholinguist Patricia Kuhl published an influential study in 1991 in *Perception & Psychophysics* under the title "Human Adults and Human Infants Show a 'Perceptual Magnet Effect' for the Prototypes of Speech Categories, Monkeys Do Not." It provided experimental evidence that adults and six-month-old infants, but not monkeys, show this effect for speech categories; a follow-up study (Kuhl et al., 1992) showed that early exposure to the native language reshapes the infant's perceptual sound map in a manner biased toward the sounds of that particular language, even before the infant utters a single actual word.

## The Theoretical Idea: The Prototype as a Perceptual Magnet

Building on the literature on prototype theory in cognitive psychology, Kuhl proposed that every sound category in a given language (such as the vowel /i/ in English) has a central "prototype" representing the clearest and best pronunciation of that sound in that language. This prototype functions as a perceptual "magnet" that attracts neighboring sounds physically toward itself, so that sounds close to the prototype are perceived as more similar to one another than sounds at the same objective acoustic distance but far from that prototype (or close to a boundary between two categories).

## Method: From the 1991 Study to the Cross-Language Follow-Up

The 1991 study tested listeners on variants of the vowel /i/, with a prototype (a highly rated, good exemplar) and a non-prototype, using a head-turn procedure: the listener (or infant) learns to turn toward a reward when a repeating background sound changes, which makes it possible to measure discrimination. Adults and six-month-old American infants were tested, and rhesus monkeys were tested for comparison. The 1992 follow-up used six-month-old infants from two linguistic environments (American English and Swedish) and vowel prototypes from each language (English /i/ and Swedish /y/), comparing discrimination around each prototype.

## Result: An Early, Language-Specific Perceptual Bias

In the 1991 study, adults and infants showed poorer discrimination of variants around the prototype than around the non-prototype, whereas monkeys did not. In the 1992 follow-up, American infants showed this broader generalization around the English prototype, while Swedish infants showed it around the Swedish prototype instead. This demonstrates that this perceptual "magnetic effect" is not a uniform general physiological property shared by all humans regardless of language. Rather, it takes shape very early (by six months of age, before any word is spoken) according to the specific native language surrounding the infant.

## A Bridge to Studies of Perceptual Narrowing

This result converges with and complements what was later documented in studies of perceptual narrowing (such as the study by Werker and Tees). Kuhl proposed that the "perceptual magnet" mechanism is one possible explanation of how this narrowing occurs. Rather than the infant losing its sensitivity to foreign sounds at random, its perceptual system reorganizes its sound map around the prototypes of its native language in particular. Its ability to discriminate sounds that are functionally important in its language increases, while its sensitivity to distinctions that do not serve this reorganized map declines.

## The Study's Importance for Understanding Later Second-Language Learning

This study provides an explanatory framework for the difficulty of learning new foreign sounds later in life. When a firmly established map of perceptual "magnets" forms around the sounds of the native language in very early childhood, the sounds of a new foreign language are later perceived through the lens of this already existing map. They are thus absorbed or "pulled" toward the nearest prototype in the native language, rather than being perceived with their original precision in the new language.
