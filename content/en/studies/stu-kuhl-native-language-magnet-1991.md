# Kuhl's Study of the Native Language Perceptual Magnet Effect (1991)

Patricia K. Kuhl's 1991 paper in *Perception & Psychophysics*, "Human Adults and Human Infants Show a 'Perceptual Magnet Effect' for the Prototypes of Speech Categories, Monkeys Do Not," reported that adults and six-month-old infants, but not rhesus monkeys, treat a good exemplar of a vowel as a center of perceptual generalization. A follow-up in *Science* (Kuhl, Williams, Lacerda, Stevens, and Lindblom, 1992) reported that this pattern already differs for American and Swedish infants, before the infants produce words.

## The Theoretical Idea: The Prototype as a Perceptual Magnet

Drawing on prototype effects in perception, Kuhl proposed that a speech category such as English /i/ has a best exemplar, a prototype. Variants near that exemplar are harder to tell apart than variants spaced equally far apart in acoustic terms but lying nearer a poor exemplar. She called the shrinkage of perceptual distance around the prototype a perceptual magnet.

## Method: From the 1991 Study to the Cross-Language Follow-Up

The 1991 paper reports experiments on variants of the vowel /i/. Adults first rated many synthesized tokens for how good an /i/ each was. Discrimination was then tested around a high-rated prototype and around a poor exemplar. For infants the measure was a head-turn conditioning task: the infant is trained to turn toward a reward when a repeating background sound changes. Monkeys were tested with a comparable operant procedure. The 1992 study tested six-month-olds in American English and Swedish homes on English /i/ and Swedish /y/.

## Result: An Early, Language-Specific Perceptual Bias

Adults and American infants generalized more around the prototype than around the non-prototype; the monkeys did not. In the 1992 follow-up, American infants showed the broader generalization around English /i/, and Swedish infants around Swedish /y/. Kuhl read that cross-language difference as evidence that the effect is already tuned by the ambient language at six months.

## A Bridge to Studies of Perceptual Narrowing

The result sits beside earlier work on perceptual narrowing. Werker and Tees (1984) found that infants' discrimination of non-native consonant contrasts declines over the second half of the first year. Kuhl suggested that reorganization around native prototypes is one mechanism of that narrowing. The two findings are not the same experiment: the magnet studies used vowels and a prototype-versus-non-prototype comparison at six months, while Werker and Tees tracked consonant contrasts across age.

## The Study's Importance for Understanding Later Second-Language Learning

Kuhl linked the early native map to later difficulty with new phonetic categories: a foreign sound may be heard as an instance of the nearest native category. That link is a hypothesis about second-language speech, not a result measured in the 1991 or 1992 infant experiments themselves.

## Prototypes, statistics, and criticism

The 1992 result treats the relevant prototype as language-specific and therefore shaped by what the infant has heard. It does not, by itself, show whether that center is the statistical mean of heard tokens or some other best exemplar. Later work on distributional learning, including Maye, Werker, and Gerken (2002), showed that infants can form phonetic categories from the frequencies of sounds in a short training set, which supplies one experience-based route to category structure. Kuhl's later "native language magnet theory expanded" (Kuhl et al., 2008) incorporates that distributional learning.

The magnet effect itself has been disputed. Lotto, Kluender, and Holt (1998) argued that poorer discrimination near a category's best exemplar can be predicted from general discriminability within the category, without a special magnet that pulls neighbors inward. Subsequent studies have not all reproduced the original prototype-versus-non-prototype difference. The cross-language difference at six months remains an important finding; the metaphor of a magnet, and the claim that it is species-specific in the strong form of the 1991 monkey comparison, are less secure.
