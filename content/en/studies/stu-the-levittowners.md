# The Levittowners

*The Levittowners: Ways of Life and Politics in a New Suburban Community* (1967), by the sociologist Herbert J. Gans, is the classic ethnographic study of American suburban life. Gans set out to test the sweeping criticisms of the suburbs that dominated the 1950s — that they bred conformity, emptiness, and a rootless mass culture — and found something very different. The Levittowners he lived among were ordinary, pragmatic people building a community from scratch, and his book reshaped how sociologists think about suburbs, class, and community.

## The Setting

Levittown, New Jersey — today Willingboro Township — was one of the great postwar planned suburbs built by the developer William Levitt. Thousands of nearly identical houses went up on former farmland, and thousands of young families, many of them veterans, moved in within a few years. By the late 1950s Levittown was the very symbol of the new American suburb: mass-produced housing, car-dependent streets, and a population that was young, white, and lower-middle-class. Critics of the era treated such places as proof that mass society was swallowing individuality.

## Method

Gans's method was participant observation of the most literal kind. In 1958 he and his wife bought a house in Levittown and lived there for two years, joining the local organisations, attending meetings, and talking with neighbours about their lives, their children, and their politics. He also surveyed residents and kept detailed field notes. Gans was open about his role as a researcher, and about the limits of studying a single community, but his long residence gave him access to everyday life that no questionnaire could match. The study was funded and published at a time when urban sociology was still dominated by studies of the inner city, and a suburb seemed an unlikely place to do serious fieldwork.

## Key Findings

Gans's central finding was that Levittown was not the conformist wasteland of popular criticism. Residents did not lose their individuality when they moved to the suburbs; they brought their class backgrounds, ethnic ties, and habits with them, and the community was more diverse than its identical houses suggested. Neighbourliness was real but selective: people formed close ties with a few neighbours of similar background and kept others at a friendly distance. Organisations — the PTA, the synagogue and church groups, the civic associations — mattered, but they were vehicles for practical concerns such as schools, roads, and taxes rather than instruments of social control.

Class differences persisted in the suburb. The lower-middle-class and working-class families who made up most of Levittown cared above all about homeownership, security, and their children's futures, and they judged the suburb by how well it served those ends. Gans also found that residents were politically active and pragmatic, not apathetic. Local politics revolved around services and taxes, and residents organised effectively when their interests were at stake. Perhaps most importantly, Gans argued that the suburbs did not create a new kind of person. They created a new setting in which familiar American class cultures continued, adapted to new conditions.

Gans was also candid about what his study could not show. Levittown's population was almost entirely white, a result of the discriminatory sales practices that shaped so many postwar suburbs, and Black families were effectively excluded from the community he studied. Gans acknowledged this homogeneity as a limit on his findings: his Levittowners were a cross-section of white working- and lower-middle-class America, not of America as a whole. Later scholars have taken up this limitation, using Gans's methods to study more diverse suburbs and to show how race, as well as class, shaped suburban life. The book's argument about the persistence of class culture has survived that criticism, but its picture of the suburb is now read alongside studies of the suburbs from which Black and Hispanic families were shut out.

## Reception and Influence

*The Levittowners* became a landmark in urban sociology and suburban studies. It undercut the "suburban myth" — the idea, promoted by writers such as William H. Whyte and David Riesman, that suburban life produced a homogeneous mass culture — and it helped establish the participant-observation study of ordinary communities as a central sociological method. A second edition, published in 1982, added a new introduction in which Gans reflected on how Levittown and its reputation had changed. The book remains a standard reference in courses on urban sociology and community studies, and its argument that place matters less than the class and culture people carry into it continues to shape research on suburbs, neighbourhoods, and community life.
