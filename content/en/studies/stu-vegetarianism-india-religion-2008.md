# A Field Study of Religious Vegetarianism in Hindu and Buddhist Contexts

This file is an overview of an anthropological and historical research line that examines the roots of religious vegetarianism in India and its contemporary practices among Hindus, Buddhists and Jains, and the varying degrees of its observance across communities and social classes.

## Definition

Religious vegetarianism in the Indian context is not merely a healthy dietary system, but a moral-religious commitment rooted in the principle of non-violence (*ahimsa*) toward every living being. This commitment reaches its greatest strictness among the Jains (many Jains avoid root vegetables, to avoid harming the many small organisms in the soil), and varies among Hindus according to region, sect and caste. Buddhist practice differs: Theravada monastic discipline traditionally permits eating meat offered as alms if the monk has not seen, heard or suspected that the animal was killed for him, while many Mahayana texts and East Asian monastic traditions encourage or require vegetarianism.

## Historical Context

The principle of *ahimsa* appears in the early Upanishadic texts (first millennium BCE), and became a central doctrine in Jainism and, in a different form, in Buddhism, both of which arose in the ascetic (*shramana*) milieu critical of Vedic animal sacrifice. The emperor Ashoka (third century BCE), who supported Buddhism, announced limits on animal slaughter in his rock and pillar edicts.

## Main Ideas and Method

Survey research, such as the national survey analysed by Yogendra Yadav and Sanjay Kumar (*The Hindu*, 14 August 2006), has found that a minority of Indians, roughly a third in that survey, report being vegetarian. This contrasts with the common image of India as a vegetarian country. Vegetarianism is more common in parts of the north and west, and among some caste and religious communities, and less common in the east and south and among Muslims and Christians. Survey figures vary with question wording and date, so they should be treated as approximate.

## Impact and Critique

These studies revealed that religious vegetarianism often intersects with a caste hierarchy (the higher castes have historically been the most committed to it as a sign of social purity), which makes it a subject of sociological critique rather than a neutral moral virtue.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Islam presents a different model for dealing with meat: meat is generally permitted, subject to rules on lawful animals and on the manner of slaughter (*dhabiha*, including the invocation of God's name), so that *halal* regulates how animals may be killed and eaten rather than forbidding meat, unlike the logic of *ahimsa*, which seeks to avoid taking life in the first place. This difference benefits the comparative study of the philosophies of religious food across traditions.
