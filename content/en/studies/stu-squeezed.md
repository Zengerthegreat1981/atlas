# Squeezed

*Squeezed: Why Our Families Can't Afford America* (2018), by the journalist Alissa Quart, is a work of reportage on the American middle class in the years after the 2008 financial crisis. Quart, who is executive director of the Economic Hardship Reporting Project, interviewed families across the United States whose incomes were too high for most public assistance yet too low to pay for housing, child care, health care and education without strain. Her central claim is that middle-class life has become structurally unaffordable, that the shortfall is bridged by debt, second jobs and constant improvisation, and that the resulting insecurity is a condition produced by an economy that has shifted risk from employers and the state onto families, not a personal failing.

The book is journalism rather than academic sociology, but it is widely read in sociology courses on inequality, work and family because it describes in concrete cases how structural pressures reach households.

## Method

*Squeezed* is built from long-form reporting. Quart interviewed families in different parts of the country and combined their accounts with published economic data on wages, housing, tuition, medical costs and debt. The book makes no claim to a representative sample, and its authority rests on the detail and the consistency of the cases rather than on statistical inference. Chapters are organised around pressure points of middle-class life, including housing, work, child care, education, health care and debt.

## Main Arguments

First, Quart argues that the costs of the four things that define family security — a home, an education, health care and care for children — have risen faster than wages for many households. Second, she shows that debt bridges the gap between stagnant incomes and these costs, and that it then constrains later choices such as changing jobs or having another child. Third, she describes jobs that once led into the middle class, such as school teaching, library work and university instruction, increasingly requiring a second income stream, and she links this to the growth of contingent and gig work, where employers provide fewer benefits and less security. Fourth, she documents a pervasive sense of shame and isolation: because the dominant story treats economic outcomes as a matter of individual effort, families often blame themselves and hide their difficulties.

Quart concludes with policy proposals, including more affordable housing for essential workers, subsidised child care, and stronger protections for freelance and gig workers.

## Reception and Context

The book was published by Ecco (HarperCollins) in 2018. It belongs to a literature on middle-class insecurity that includes Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's *The Two-Income Trap* (2003), Richard Reeves's *Dream Hoarders* (2017), and Kathryn Edin and H. Luke Shaefer's study of extreme poverty, *$2.00 a Day* (2015). Reviewers generally praised the vivid cases and the refusal of a self-help framing of economic hardship, while others noted that its strength lies in documentation rather than policy design and that a reportage method cannot show how typical its families are. Quart returned to related themes in *Bootstrapped: Liberating Ourselves from the American Dream* (2023), which examines the cultural ideal of self-reliance.
