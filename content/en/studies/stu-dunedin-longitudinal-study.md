# The Dunedin Longitudinal Study of Development, Health, Behavior and Self-Control

A multidisciplinary population-based longitudinal study in New Zealand following 1,037 individuals born in a single hospital in 1972-1973, with a very high retention rate (about 94-95% of living participants at recent assessments) over five decades.

## Context and Experimental Design

The study followed an entire birth cohort, and each case underwent detailed multidisciplinary examinations every few years (at ages 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 18, 21, 26, 32, 38 and 45), including: psychological interviews, genetic tests, cardiac, dental and retinal health, and police and tax records.

## Results and Conclusions

The famous 2011 paper revealed that the measure of "self-control" (Self-Control), assessed in childhood between ages 3 and 11 (by observation and by parent, teacher, and self reports), predicted physical health (for example metabolic and cardiovascular risk markers and dental health), substance dependence, income and financial difficulties, and criminal convictions at age 32, even after taking into account intelligence (IQ) and family social class. The cohort has also been used to describe developmental trajectories of antisocial behavior (adolescence-limited versus life-course-persistent; Moffitt, 1993) and gene-environment interactions.

## Criticism and Methodological Debate

The Dunedin study is widely regarded as a model of longitudinal research because of its low attrition and breadth of measurement. Its limits include a single-city, mostly White cohort born in the early 1970s, and the correlational design: associations between childhood self-control and later outcomes do not by themselves show that training self-control would change those outcomes, although the authors argue that the findings support early intervention.

## The Famous Gene-Environment Interactions

The Dunedin study also produced some of the most famous research in psychology on the interaction between genes and environment; Avshalom Caspi and Terrie Moffitt (Caspi & Moffitt) reported that carriers of the short allele of the serotonin transporter gene (5-HTTLPR) were more likely to develop depression after multiple stressful life events (Caspi et al., 2003), and that a "low-activity" MAOA genotype was associated with later antisocial behavior mainly among children who had been severely maltreated (Caspi et al., 2002). These studies were influential in promoting research on gene-environment interaction, but they have had uneven replication: a large meta-analysis (Risch et al., 2009) did not support the 5-HTTLPR finding, whereas the MAOA finding has received more support from a meta-analysis (Kim-Cohen et al., 2006). Candidate-gene interaction findings of this kind are now treated with caution.

## Measuring the Pace of Biological Aging

Using biomarker data collected at ages 26, 32, and 38, Belsky and colleagues (2015) developed a measure of the "Pace of Aging" that combines 18 biomarkers (including cardiovascular, metabolic, kidney, lung, immune, and gum health measures) to estimate how fast a person's body is aging over time. They found that individuals of the same chronological age (38) differed considerably in their pace of aging, that faster aging was associated with poorer physical functioning and appearing older to independent raters, and that it was related to some childhood factors, which links developmental psychology with research on the biology of aging.
