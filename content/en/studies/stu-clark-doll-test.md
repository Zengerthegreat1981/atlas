# The Famous Kenneth and Mamie Clark Doll Test Study of Racial Identity Formation in Children

A historic psychological and social study that presented identical black and white dolls to African American children, revealing that most chose the white dolls as preferred and "nice" and the dark dolls as "bad."

## Context and Experimental Design

In the 1947 study, 253 Black children aged 3 to 7 were tested, in racially segregated schools in Arkansas and in integrated schools in Massachusetts. Each child was shown 4 dolls identical in everything except skin and hair color (two white dolls and two dark brown dolls), and was asked questions such as: 'Give me the doll you like to play with,' 'the nice/good doll,' 'the ugly/bad doll,' and finally the critical emotional question: 'Give me the doll that looks like you.'

## Results and Conclusions

About two-thirds of the children (67%) chose the white doll as the one they preferred to play with, and a majority described the white doll as "nice" and the dark doll as "bad." Most could correctly identify the doll that looked like them, but when asked to do so some children showed signs of distress. The Clarks interpreted the results as evidence that racial prejudice and segregation damage the self-image of Black children from an early age.

## Criticism and Methodological Controversy

The study is widely regarded as a landmark in the psychology of race and was used in litigation against school segregation. It has also been criticized: the sample was small and not random, results differed by region in ways that complicate the Clarks' interpretation (children in the integrated North did not show less preference for the white doll), and later scholars have questioned whether doll choices measure self-esteem or children's awareness of social status.

## The Study's Role in Brown v. Board of Education (1954)

Kenneth Clark testified as an expert witness in one of the lower-court cases consolidated in *Brown v. Board of Education* (the South Carolina case, *Briggs v. Elliott*), presenting doll-test findings as evidence that segregation harmed Black children's self-image. The Supreme Court's unanimous 1954 opinion cited social-science work, including Clark's, in a footnote (footnote 11) in support of its conclusion that segregated schooling generates feelings of inferiority. The role of the psychological evidence in the decision has been debated: the Court's ruling rested mainly on constitutional grounds, and the social-science citations drew criticism from some legal scholars.

## Recent Replications and the Controversy over Change over Time

Later versions of the test have given mixed results. A 2010 CNN report conducted with the psychologist Margaret Beale Spencer used a modified procedure (a set of cartoon figures) and found that many children, of several backgrounds, still associated lighter skin with positive attributes. Such media studies had small samples and little methodological control, so whether internalized racial bias in children has changed since the 1940s remains debated, and the replication status is therefore marked "contested."

## Systematic Replications in Multiracial and Cross-Cultural Societies

Subsequent decades saw extensive empirical replications across diverse multiethnic and multiracial settings, revealing that children's doll choices are shaped by evolving sociopolitical contexts and developmental processes rather than uniform internalized self-hatred. In a prominent replication conducted in Lincoln, Nebraska, Joseph Hraba and Geoffrey Grant (1970) found that Black children predominantly preferred Black dolls and assigned positive attributes to them. They attributed this reversal from the 1940s findings to the cultural impact of the Civil Rights and "Black Pride" movements, which provided positive collective racial affirmation.

Cross-cultural studies in multiracial societies (such as research in the United Kingdom by Milner in 1973 with South Asian, Afro-Caribbean, and White children, and work in New Zealand and South Africa) indicated that out-group preference tended to reflect awareness of institutional hierarchy and social power differentials rather than personal identity impairment. Crucially, developmental psychologist Margaret Beale Spencer (1982, 1984) argued for a critical theoretical distinction: children's forced-choice doll selections measure *racial awareness and societal knowledge* (accurate perception of societal prejudices and stereotypes) rather than *personal self-esteem* or personal self-concept. When evaluated with multidimensional self-concept instruments, minority children who made pro-white doll selections often did not show lower personal self-concept.
