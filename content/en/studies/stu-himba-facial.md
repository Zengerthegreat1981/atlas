# Himba Facial Expression Categorization Study

A cross-cultural field experiment conducted with the Himba people of northwestern Namibia, published in 2014 by Maria Gendron, Debi Roberson, Jacoba van der Vyver, and Lisa Feldman Barrett. The study asked whether people with almost no exposure to Western media sort photographs of facial expressions into the same emotion categories — happy, sad, angry, fearful, disgusted — that Western participants use. When no emotion words were supplied in advance, Himba participants did not reproduce the Western pattern. Instead they grouped faces by what the person in the photograph appeared to be doing, such as laughing or looking at something. The result became central evidence in the debate over whether facial expressions of emotion are universal.

## The Study: Setting and Method

The Himba are a semi-nomadic pastoral people living in small settlements in the Kunene region of northwestern Namibia, speaking Otji-Herero and living with relatively little contact with Western cultural products. That isolation was the point: if facial recognition were an inborn universal, it should show up even there. Gendron, then a postdoctoral researcher in Barrett's laboratory, travelled to Namibia in the fall of 2011 and again in the spring of 2012, spending weeks reaching remote villages by four-wheel drive.

The experiment used a free-sorting task rather than the forced-choice method that had dominated earlier research. Participants were shown photographs of posed facial expressions — smiling, scowling, wide-eyed, nose-scrunched, pouting, and neutral faces — and asked to sort them into piles of faces that seemed to show the same thing, using words of their own choosing. No emotion labels were offered. Fifty-four Himba participants from two remote villages completed the task, and their results were compared with sixty-eight American participants recruited at the Boston Museum of Science who performed the same task. A second group in each culture did an "anchored" version with emotion words supplied, to measure how much the words themselves mattered.

This design was a deliberate response to the classic studies of Paul Ekman, who in the 1960s and 1970s had shown photographs of faces to people in Papua New Guinea and asked them to match each face to one emotion word from a short list. Ekman's method produced high cross-cultural agreement, long taken as proof of a small set of universal basic emotions. Gendron and her colleagues argued that handing participants the words in advance could itself teach them the categories, so a test of universality had to remove the words and see what categories people produced on their own.

## Key Findings

Without emotion words as cues, the Himba sorting pattern diverged sharply from the presumed universal one. Himba participants did place smiling faces together in one pile and wide-eyed faces together in another, suggesting that some broad distinctions — a smiling face versus a startled-looking face — are shared. But they grouped scowling (anger), nose-scrunched (disgust), and pouting (sad) faces into a single pile, where American participants kept them apart. Himba participants labelled their piles with actions — "laughing," "looking at something" — rather than emotion words.

When emotion words were supplied as anchors, both groups sorted more closely to the presumed universal pattern, though substantial cultural variation remained. The contrast between the two conditions was the study's central finding: the words did real work. Perception of emotion from a face depended on having the relevant emotion concept available, suggesting the categories are learned, not read off the face by an inborn mechanism.

The same pattern appeared in a companion study of vocalizations published the same year. Himba listeners who heard Western nonverbal sounds — laughter, screams, sighs, growls — and were asked to label them freely did not use the expected emotion terms, except for laughter, which most listeners in both cultures identified as amusement. What crossed cultures reliably was valence: whether a sound was positive or negative, and to some extent how aroused it seemed. In a forced-choice task they beat chance only when the two sounds differed in valence. The voice, like the face, seemed to carry broad affective meaning across cultures, but not the specific discrete emotion categories of Western psychology.

## Reception and Influence

The two 2014 papers landed in the middle of a long-running dispute between basic-emotion theory, associated with Ekman, and the theory of constructed emotion developed by Barrett, which holds that emotions are made by the brain using concepts learned from culture. Barrett gave the Himba study a prominent place in her 2017 book *How Emotions Are Made*, presenting it as evidence that the classic universality findings were an artefact of method: give people the words and they will sort by them; take the words away and the universal pattern dissolves.

The response from the other side was swift. Disa Sauter and her colleagues, whose 2010 study had reported cross-cultural recognition of basic emotions from vocalizations among the Himba, published a reanalysis in 2015 arguing that Gendron's team had included positive emotions — triumph, sensory pleasure, relief — whose signals are culture-specific, and that once the analysis was restricted to basic emotions, Himba listeners did recognize specific emotions even when the competing sound matched in valence. Gendron's team replied that Sauter's manipulation checks had effectively taught Himba participants the Western categories. The exchange left the field split, but it changed the terms of the debate: what people perceive with words in hand differs from what they perceive without them.

For emotion science the study's lasting contribution is methodological as much as substantive. It showed that a forced-choice task can manufacture agreement, that free sorting and free labelling are stricter tests of universality, and that the boundary between perception and concept is thinner than basic-emotion theory assumed.
