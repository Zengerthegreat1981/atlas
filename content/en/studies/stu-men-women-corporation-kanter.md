# Men and Women of the Corporation

*Men and Women of the Corporation* (1977) is Rosabeth Moss Kanter's study of a large American manufacturing company, which she called "Indsco" (a pseudonym), in the early 1970s. It argues that the behaviour of men and women in organisations is shaped chiefly by the structure of their positions, in particular by the opportunity, power and numerical proportions they face, rather than by gender traits.

## Method and Content

Kanter, then a sociologist at Brandeis University, drew on observation, interviews, documents and consulting contacts inside Indsco. She gave particular attention to managers, to the (almost entirely female) secretarial ranks, and to the small group of women in the sales force, where women were a very small minority. Out of this material she developed an account of organisational life built on three structural conditions:

- Opportunity: the prospects for promotion and growth attached to a position. People in dead-end jobs tend to lower their aspirations, limit their commitment and seek satisfaction elsewhere, which can look like a personal trait but reflects the job.
- Power: the capacity to mobilise resources and support. Those with little power are more likely to rely on rigid supervision and to resist others.
- Proportions: the numerical balance of social types in a group. In "skewed" groups, where one type makes up perhaps 15 percent or less, the minority members are "tokens".

Kanter described three dynamics of tokenism: visibility (tokens attract attention and face heightened performance pressure), contrast (differences between the majority and the token are exaggerated and the majority reinforces its own boundaries), and assimilation (tokens are fitted to stereotyped roles). In the case of women she described role traps such as the "mother", the "seductress", the "pet" and the "iron maiden".

## Impact

The book shifted the explanation of gender differences at work from individual characteristics, such as a lack of ambition or a different leadership style, to organisational structure. Her concepts of tokenism and the opportunity structure became widely used in studies of organisations, gender and diversity.

## Critique

Later research qualified the tokenism argument. Lynn Zimmer (1988) argued that its predictions apply differently to men in female-dominated occupations than to women in male-dominated ones, and Christine Williams (1992) described a "glass escalator" for men in "women's professions" rather than the disadvantages Kanter predicted. Joan Acker (1990) and others argued that organisations are themselves gendered rather than neutral structures, so that structure and gender cannot be separated as cleanly as Kanter proposed.
