# Street Corner Society

*Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum* (1943) is William Foote Whyte's ethnography of a poor Italian-American district of Boston's North End, which he called "Cornerville". It is a classic study of participant observation and of the internal organisation of urban neighbourhoods.

## Background

Whyte came to the district in 1936 as a Junior Fellow of Harvard's Society of Fellows and carried out fieldwork there for about three and a half years. He rented a room with a local family, learned some Italian and joined in the activities of local groups. His access depended heavily on a young man he called "Doc", who introduced him to others, discussed his interpretations with him and became his principal informant. Whyte later took a doctorate at the University of Chicago.

## Method

Whyte relied on participant observation: passing time on street corners, in clubs and at social and political gatherings, keeping field notes, and tracing over time who initiated activities and who followed. The long period of contact allowed him to see relationships that interviews or official statistics would have missed.

## Main Findings

Against the common description of such districts as "disorganised", Whyte argued that Cornerville had an elaborate social organisation of its own. Among young men he distinguished "corner boys", whose lives centred on a street group and its mutual obligations, from "college boys", who pursued education and upward mobility. Corner groups such as the Nortons had informal hierarchies. A leader's position rested on meeting obligations and on the confidence of members, and performance in activities such as bowling tended to correspond to a man's standing in the group. Whyte also showed how these local groups were linked to larger structures, including the rackets and ward politics.

## Methodological Significance

An appendix added in the 1955 edition gives a frank account of how the research was done, including mistakes, ethical dilemmas and the researcher's relationship with his informants. It became a widely read guide to fieldwork. The study also contributed to the analysis of small groups and informal organisation, interests that Whyte pursued in later research on industry and work, much of it at Cornell University.

## Criticism and Contemporary Relevance

In 1992 Marianne Boelen published a critical revisit that questioned parts of Whyte's account and his research ethics; Whyte replied in the same journal, and the exchange fed wider debates about verification, representation and the protection of research participants. Readers have also noted that the account concentrates on men. The book remains a point of reference for urban ethnography and for the argument that neighbourhoods which outsiders regard as chaotic may have orderly social structures.
