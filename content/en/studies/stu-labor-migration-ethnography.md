# Labor Migration and Transnational Families: Ethnographies of Movement and Connection

Since the 1980s anthropologists have produced a substantial literature on labor migration: movement of workers from rural areas to cities and abroad, and the households, communities and states connected by it. Rather than treating migration as a one-way move that severs ties with home, this work shows migration as a sustained social process: migrants send remittances, maintain family ties across distance, and reshape gender, generation and class relations in both sending and receiving places. The entry surveys the main themes of this literature, not a single study.

## Development of the Field

Early anthropological work on migration, much of it in Africa, often saw urban movement as a break with "tradition." Michael Kearney's 1986 review in the *Annual Review of Anthropology* summarized a shift toward understanding migration within political economy: households combine wage work, farming and remittances, and are linked into wider labour markets. In the 1990s the concept of transnationalism, developed by Basch, Glick Schiller and Szanton Blanc (1994), described how migrants build social fields spanning borders. George Marcus's call for multi-sited ethnography (1995) gave a method for following people, things and ideas across locations.

## Themes and Examples

- **Households and remittances.** Studies in Latin America, South Asia and the Middle East show households as units that spread risk through migration. Katy Gardner's work on Sylheti migration to Britain from rural Bangladesh (1995) traced how overseas earnings changed local status, house-building and obligations.
- **Gender and care.** Rhacel Parreñas (2001, 2005) studied Filipina domestic workers abroad and the transnational families, including children, that they leave behind. This work is often discussed alongside the concept of the "global care chain" developed by Arlie Hochschild (2000). Nicole Constable (1997) studied Filipina domestic workers in Hong Kong, examining their working conditions and social worlds, including weekend gatherings.
- **Masculinity and family in the Andes.** Jeffrey Pribilsky (2007) studied Ecuadorian migrants from the southern Andes to New York and their families at home, examining how migration reworked marital and gender relations.
- **States and regulation.** Ethnographers have examined how visa regimes, recruitment agencies and bilateral agreements shape who can move and on what terms, and how migrant labour is made vulnerable.

## Method

Fieldwork typically combined household interviews and observation in sending communities with research among migrants at their destinations, sometimes by the same ethnographer (multi-sited research), and used life histories, surveys of remittances and analysis of policy. Emotional aspects of separation, such as longing, guilt and the strain of long-distance parenting, have received increasing attention.

## Critique

Scholars have cautioned that emphasizing household strategy and migrant agency can obscure structural inequality, since capital and receiving states often benefit from cheap, precarious labour; that remittance-led development has limits; and that the sending-country perspective may treat migrants and their families as homogeneous. Studies of transnational families have also been criticized for stressing separation and "woes" at the expense of family resilience, and for paying less attention to men's and children's own voices.
