# Labor Migration and Transnational Families: Ethnographies of Movement and Connection

A body of ethnographic work spanning from the 1980s onward, documenting labor migration as a widespread phenomenon reshaping households, communities, and nations globally. Ethnographers working in various locations—the Philippines, Indonesia, Mexico, India, South Asia—documented how rural families participated in circular migration, sending family members to work in cities or abroad, creating transnational households and economic strategies. This ethnographic literature revealed migration not as a one-time movement but as a complex social process involving sustained connections across distances, the transformation of family relationships, and the development of economic strategies that linked multiple locations.

## Publication Context

Labor migration ethnographies emerged as a substantial body of work from the 1980s onward, as anthropologists recognized that migration was reshaping social organization and economic strategies in numerous societies. Earlier anthropology had tended to treat migration as a breakdown of traditional communities or as a problem for governments; newer ethnographic work approached migration as a sophisticated economic and social strategy through which households diversified income sources and managed risk. The ethnographic literature on labor migration grew substantially from the 1990s onward as globalization intensified and as anthropologists became interested in how global processes shaped local lives. Ethnographers conducted fieldwork in sending communities (documenting how families experienced and managed labor migration), in receiving communities (studying how migrants lived and worked), and increasingly in transnational spaces (studying how migrants maintained connections across borders). This literature coincided with development of transnational anthropology as a subfield, as scholars recognized that understanding contemporary communities required attending to connections and flows across borders. The ethnographic work on labor migration influenced policy discussions about migration, remittances, and development.

## Research Question & Approach

Ethnographers studying labor migration asked: Why do people migrate? How do migrant households organize themselves? How do families maintain relationships across distances? How do remittances (money sent home by migrant workers) affect sending communities? Rather than studying migration as individual decision-making or as demographic flows, ethnographers examined migration as household and community strategy. They conducted fieldwork in sending communities, documenting which family members migrated and why, how households managed without those members, and how remittances were used. They also conducted fieldwork following migrants to receiving locations, studying the conditions migrants worked under, how migrants lived in migration destinations, and how they maintained transnational connections. Some ethnographers conducted multi-sited fieldwork, following migrants across locations and examining how transnational communities functioned. Their methodological approach was comprehensive: they interviewed household members, documented the economics of migration, observed how migrant labor was organized, and attended to the emotional and social dimensions of family separation.

## Ethnographic Setting

Labor migration ethnographies were conducted across diverse locations: agricultural families in Mexico sending members to US cities or fields; rural Indian families sending workers to urban centers or abroad; Philippine women working as domestic servants in Hong Kong, Saudi Arabia, and other countries; Indonesian workers in Malaysia; Ecuadorian farmers sending family members to cities or abroad. The ethnographic settings varied, but common themes emerged: rural families with limited access to land or inadequate agricultural income diversified household income through labor migration; women increasingly participated in wage labor migration; migration was often temporary or circular (migrants returned seasonally or periodically); and migrant income was essential to household survival and to funding consumption, education, and investment. The ethnographies were conducted during a period of increasing global economic integration and movement: as manufacturing relocated globally, as service economies expanded in developed countries, and as migration regulations both facilitated and constrained movement. The ethnographic work documented how global economic processes were reshaping local communities.

## Key Findings

Ethnographers discovered that labor migration was a sophisticated household strategy rather than a failure of development or a breakdown of traditional community. Families in agricultural regions with limited economic opportunities strategically sent family members to work in cities or abroad, using the income to support those remaining, to invest in education, to purchase land or improve houses, or to support periodic consumption. Remittances—income sent home by migrants—became essential to household and community economies. Ethnographers found that migration created extended households: some family members in villages, others in cities or abroad, all economically connected through remittance flows. This created what scholars called "transnational households"—families whose economic survival and strategies depended on maintaining connections across borders. Ethnographers also found that migration involved gendered patterns: women increasingly worked as migrant laborers, often in domestic service or manufacturing, and women's migration had specific consequences for household gender relations. Children of migrant workers often stayed in sending communities, cared for by relatives, while maintaining emotional connections to absent parents. Migration also generated significant social and psychological effects: family separation, changing gender roles, tension between migrants' desires to earn money and their desires to be with families, and the experience of living as outsiders in receiving communities. Ethnographers found that migrants faced discrimination, exploitative working conditions, and social marginalization in receiving communities, yet continued to migrate because sending community income opportunities were so limited. The ethnographic work also revealed how states tried to control and manage migration through laws, regulations, and bilateral agreements, and how these state interventions shaped migration patterns and experiences.

## Ethnographic Examples

Ethnographers provide detailed case studies of migrant households and trajectories. One ethnography describes a Philippine family: the father worked abroad as a migrant worker in the Middle East, the mother remained in the Philippines caring for children and managing a small business, and the father sent remittances monthly. The ethnographer documents the family's economic reliance on the father's income, the emotional strain of separation, the mother's frustrations with the father's absence, and the children's mixed feelings about their father. The ethnographer also documents how the remittances were used: some went to household consumption and school fees, some was saved for investment in a small business, and some was spent on consumption items marking social status. Another ethnography describes an Ecuadorian farmer who migrated to New York to work in restaurants, maintaining contact with his family in the village. The ethnographer documents the farmer's work conditions, his wage, the money he sent home, and how his family used remittances to invest in agricultural improvements and to send children to better schools. The ethnographer also documents the farmer's experience in New York: he lived in crowded housing with other migrants, worked long hours, experienced discrimination, and maintained connections to his home village through telephone calls and periodic visits. Another ethnography documents women domestic servants in Hong Kong working for households at low wages and under exploitative conditions, yet continuing to work because the income was essential to their families' survival. The ethnographer documents the intimate labor these women performed, the emotional relationships they developed with employers' families, and the ways they maintained connection to their own families while living far away.

## Theoretical Contribution

Labor migration ethnographies contributed theoretical understanding of how global economic processes shaped local communities and household organization. They demonstrated that migration was not a response to unemployment or to individual initiative but was a household and community strategy for managing risk and insufficient local income. This framing reoriented how scholars understood migration: instead of seeing migrants as individually ambitious or displaced, migration was understood as a rational economic strategy. The work contributed to developing transnational anthropology as a subfield by demonstrating that understanding contemporary communities required attending to transnational connections. The ethnographic work also contributed to feminist anthropology by documenting women's experiences of migration and by showing how migration transformed gender relations within households. The ethnographies also contributed to political economy approaches in anthropology by showing how global capitalism created conditions of inequality that prompted migration, and how migration served to benefit capitalist employers while imposing costs on migrant workers and sending communities.

## Methodological Legacy

Labor migration ethnographies pioneered multi-sited ethnography—the practice of conducting fieldwork in multiple locations to understand phenomena that spanned boundaries. Rather than studying communities as bounded places, ethnographers followed migrants and studied how transnational communities functioned. This methodological innovation influenced anthropology toward studying diaspora, transnationalism, and other phenomena that crossed borders. The ethnographic work also contributed methodologies for studying the emotional and affective dimensions of separation and connection, influencing anthropology toward attention to feeling and embodied experience. The work also contributed to developing mixed-methods approaches that combined ethnographic observation with demographic data on migration patterns and economic data on remittance flows.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has built on labor migration ethnographies while raising important critiques. Some scholars have questioned whether ethnographic attention to household strategies adequately addresses structural exploitation and the ways global capitalism benefits from migration. Others have noted that celebrating migrant agency in migration strategies might obscure the limited options available and the constraints on migrants' choices. Postcolonial scholars have also questioned whether ethnographic representation of migrants serves to highlight their creative strategies or reinforces stereotypes about the developing world as dependent on remittance income. Despite these critiques, the body of labor migration ethnographies remains valuable as documentation of how global economic processes reshape local communities and household organization. The work has had substantial policy influence, contributing to recognition of migration as a significant development strategy and to policy discussions about migrant worker rights and remittance flows. For contemporary anthropologists, the work exemplifies how ethnography can illuminate global processes and their local impacts, and how attention to household and family experiences reveals the human dimensions of economic change.
