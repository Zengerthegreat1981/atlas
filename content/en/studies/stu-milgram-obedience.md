# Milgram's Obedience to Authority Experiments

A famous series of social-psychological experiments conducted by Stanley Milgram at Yale University between 1961 and 1962 to investigate the degree to which ordinary individuals would obey an authoritative command to inflict harm on another human being.

## Context and Baseline Experimental Design

Recruiting 40 adult male participants from New Haven through newspaper advertisements and direct mail under the guise of an investigation into "memory and learning," Milgram paired each participant with a confederate (an accountant posing as another volunteer). A rigged drawing ensured the participant was always assigned the role of "teacher" and the confederate the "learner." 

The learner was strapped into an electric chair in an adjoining room. The teacher was seated before an imposing shock generator featuring 30 toggle switches graded in 15-volt increments from 15 to 450 volts, with verbal labels ranging from "Slight Shock" to "Danger: Severe Shock" and "XXX." The teacher was instructed to administer an escalating shock each time the learner made a mistake on paired-associate word tasks. Whenever the teacher hesitated or expressed reluctance, the experimenter deployed a standardized hierarchy of four verbal prods:
1. "Please continue" or "Please go on."
2. "The experiment requires that you continue."
3. "It is absolutely essential that you continue."
4. "You have no other choice, you must go on."

## Results and the Baseline Condition

In the baseline study reported in 1963, the learner began grunting at 75 volts, explicitly complained of a heart condition at 150 volts and demanded to be released, screamed at 270 volts, and became completely unresponsive after 330 volts. Despite exhibiting extreme physical and emotional distress—trembling, sweating, stuttering, and nervous laughter—65% of participants (26 out of 40) administered the maximum shock of 450 volts. Every single participant continued past 300 volts. 

Milgram interpreted this extreme compliance through his theory of the "agentic state": individuals shift from autonomous moral agency to viewing themselves merely as instruments carrying out the wishes of a legitimate authority figure.

## Systematic Experimental Variations

Across 18 experimental conditions involving roughly 1,000 subjects, Milgram systematically manipulated social and physical variables to isolate the determinants of obedience:
- **Victim Proximity**: In the "Remote" condition (learner unseen, banging on the wall at 300V), obedience reached 65%. In the "Voice-Feedback" baseline, obedience was 62.5%. When the learner was seated in the same room 1.5 feet away ("Proximity"), obedience dropped to 40% (16/40). In the "Touch-Proximity" condition, where the teacher was required to physically force the protesting victim's hand onto an insulated shock plate, obedience fell to 30% (12/40).
- **Surveillance and Experimenter Presence**: When the experimenter gave instructions by telephone from another room, full obedience fell to 20.5% (with several subjects covertly administering lower voltages than required).
- **Institutional Context**: To test the prestige of Yale University, Milgram relocated the laboratory to a run-down commercial office building in downtown Bridgeport, Connecticut, operating under the fictitious name "Research Associates of Bridgeport." Full obedience remained substantial at 47.5% (19/40), demonstrating that institutional prestige alone did not drive the effect.
- **Group Dynamics and Peer Rebellion**: When paired with two confederate teachers who refused to comply at 150 and 210 volts, participant obedience plummeted to 10% (4/40). Conversely, when the participant was assigned a secondary clerical role (reading word pairs) while another peer threw the shock switch, obedience escalated to 92.5% (37/40).
- **Gender**: In an all-female condition ($N = 40$), the rate of full obedience was 65%, identical to the male baseline, although women reported higher levels of subjective tension.

## Criticism, Archival Scrutiny, and Ethical Standards

Milgram's research generated intense ethical outrage regarding deception, inadequate debriefing, and severe psychological distress, directly shaping modern institutional review boards (IRBs) and ethical codes governing human experimentation. 

Archival research by Gina Perry (2013), examining Milgram's original audio recordings and notes at Yale, revealed that experimenters frequently went beyond the four scripted prods, improvising coercive pressure, and that some participants suspected the deception. Nonetheless, partial replications under contemporary ethical guidelines—such as Jerry Burger's (2009) study capping shocks at 150 volts (the point at which 79% of Milgram's continuing subjects persisted to the end)—found obedience rates not significantly lower than in Milgram's comparable condition (70% in Burger's sample), suggesting that the authority effect has persisted, though the 150-volt cutoff limits direct comparison.
