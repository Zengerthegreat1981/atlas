# Milgram's Obedience to Authority Experiments

A famous series of social-psychological experiments conducted by Stanley Milgram at Yale University to test how willing participants were to obey an authority ordering them to perform acts that conflicted with their moral conscience, by delivering fictitious electric shocks.

## Context and experimental design

Milgram began the experiments in 1961, around the time of the trial of Adolf Eichmann, in order to investigate psychological explanations for individuals' compliance with destructive orders. Forty male participants were recruited through newspaper advertisements and direct mail on the pretext of studying "memory and learning". The roles were apparently divided into "teacher" (the real participant) and "learner" (a confederate actor), and the teacher was asked to deliver electric shocks of graduated intensity from 15 to 450 volts to the learner whenever he erred in remembering word pairs, with the experimenter using standardized prods to oblige the participant to continue.

## Results and conclusions

The results showed that 65% of participants (26 of 40) continued delivering shocks up to the maximum (450 volts), despite showing signs of tension and severe distress and despite the fictitious learner's screaming and his protests, including a mention of a heart condition. Milgram concluded that individuals enter an "Agentic State" in which they surrender their own moral responsibility in favor of the directing authority.

## Criticism and methodological controversy

The experiment faced sharp ethical criticism because of the severe psychological stress and the deception practiced on participants, and the study is often cited in discussions that shaped later research-ethics standards. Archival work after Milgram's death, drawing on his papers and recordings at Yale (Gina Perry), suggested that the experimenters departed from the set protocol and urged participants repeatedly more than the four announced prods suggest, in addition to some participants' doubts about whether the shocks were real. Nevertheless, a partial replication by Jerry Burger (2009) under stricter ethical constraints (stopping at 150 volts, the point after which most of Milgram's obedient participants continued) found compliance rates comparable to Milgram's at that point, which supports the stability of the phenomenon in general despite the methodological controversy over the original experiment.

## Variations

Milgram ran a series of variations. Obedience fell when the learner was in the same room (about 40% reached the maximum shock), when the participant had to press the learner's hand onto the shock plate (about 30%), when the experimenter gave orders by telephone (about 20%), and when two peer confederates refused to continue (about 10%).

## Relation to the Eichmann trial and the concept of the banality of evil
Hannah Arendt, following her coverage of the trial of Adolf Eichmann in Jerusalem (1961), wrote in 1963 about the "Banality of Evil", that is, that the perpetrators of great atrocities are not necessarily deranged sadists but may be ordinary employees carrying out orders within a bureaucratic hierarchy. Milgram's experiments, which began at about the same time, are often linked to this thesis, since they showed that ordinary individuals can carry their obedience to authority to the point of harming others; the link is an interpretive one, and Arendt's and Milgram's accounts differ in important respects.
