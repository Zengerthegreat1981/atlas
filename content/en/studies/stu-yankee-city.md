# Yankee City

The Yankee City study, conducted by the anthropologist W. Lloyd Warner and his associates in Newburyport, Massachusetts, in the 1930s, was one of the largest and most ambitious community studies undertaken in the United States. Warner, who had done fieldwork among the Murngin people of Arnhem Land in Australia, set out to apply the methods of social anthropology to a modern American town, treating it as a complete social system to be described in its entirety. He and a team of researchers studied Newburyport — a small New England port city of about 17,000 people, renamed "Yankee City" to preserve its anonymity — during the early 1930s, conducting interviews, surveys, and observations of every institution in the town. The results were published in five volumes between 1941 and 1959, and the study became the model for a generation of community research.

Warner (1898–1970) was an American anthropologist and sociologist who had studied at the University of California, Berkeley, and at Harvard, and was strongly influenced by the British social anthropologist A. R. Radcliffe-Brown. He began the Yankee City project in 1930, while at Harvard, and continued it after moving to the University of Chicago in 1935, where he remained until 1959. His aim was to test whether anthropological concepts — social structure, status, ritual, and symbolism — could explain a complex modern society as well as they explained a small-scale one. Newburyport was chosen because it was small enough to be studied whole, old enough to have a settled social order, and representative enough, in Warner's view, of the New England town that had shaped American culture.

## Method

The method was ethnography on a very large scale. Warner's team collected census and tax data, interviewed residents in every occupational and ethnic group, observed church services, lodge meetings, factory routines, and funerals, and recorded the symbolic life of the town — its Memorial Day ceremonies, its gossip, and its status rituals. The team also studied the town's ethnic communities, its factory, and its class system, and the five volumes reflect that division of labour: *The Social Life of a Modern Community* (1941, with Paul S. Lunt), *The Status System of a Modern Community* (1942), *The Social Systems of American Ethnic Groups* (1945), *The Social System of a Modern Factory* (1947), and *The Living and the Dead: A Study in the Symbolic Life of Americans* (1959).

Warner's method was functionalist. Following Radcliffe-Brown, he treated the community as a system of interlocking institutions, each of which contributed to the maintenance of the whole, and he paid particular attention to the symbolic and ritual dimensions of social life — the ways in which status was displayed, deference was shown, and membership in the community was marked. The team combined interviews and observation with extensive statistical classification, and Warner insisted that a factory, a church, and a Memorial Day parade could only be understood as parts of a single system.

## Key Findings

The study's central finding was that American society was organised by a system of social class that was both more elaborate and more powerful than Americans liked to admit. Warner identified six classes in Yankee City — upper-upper, lower-upper, upper-middle, lower-middle, upper-lower, and lower-lower — and showed that class membership shaped where people lived, whom they married, which churches and clubs they joined, and how they were treated by their neighbours. Unlike the Lynds, who had found a simple division between a business class and a working class, Warner found a finely graded hierarchy, and he showed that the distinctions between its rungs were maintained by elaborate rituals of inclusion and exclusion.

A second finding came from the factory study, which examined the social system of a shoe factory and showed that the workplace had its own status hierarchy, its own rituals, and its own informal organisation, which could not be understood from the formal organisation chart alone. Finally, in *The Living and the Dead*, Warner argued that American civic ritual — above all the Memorial Day ceremony, in which the town honoured its war dead — performed a sacred function, binding the community together and expressing its deepest values.

## Reception and Influence

The Yankee City series was widely admired for its scope and ambition, and it established Warner as one of the leading figures in American social science. The study's class scheme, often called the Warner class system, was adopted by market researchers and sociologists and remained influential for decades, and its factory study anticipated the later sociology of organisations. Warner's insistence that modern American life had a symbolic and ritual dimension anticipated later work on civil religion, such as Robert Bellah's essay "Civil Religion in America" (1967), and his application of anthropology to modern institutions opened a field that later researchers extended to corporations, hospitals, and schools.

The study has also been criticised. Some scholars argued that Warner's functionalism made the community seem more harmonious and integrated than it was, and that his six-class scheme imposed a rigidity that the data did not always support. Arthur Vidich and Joseph Bensman's *Small Town in Mass Society* (1958) later showed how a small town was shaped by outside forces, a point that cuts against treating a community as self-contained. Related studies of class in American towns include August B. Hollingshead's *Elmtown's Youth* (1949).
