# Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum

A long-term ethnographic study carried out by William Foote Whyte in Boston's North End from 1936 to 1940 and published in 1943, which became a classic model of participant observation in American urban sociology.

## Method and Content

Whyte, then a Harvard Junior Fellow, lived for about three and a half years in the poor, largely Italian-American North End of Boston ("Cornerville" in the book), renting a room with a local family and taking part in the daily life of local groups, especially the "Nortons," a gang of young men. His key informant, to whom he gave the pseudonym "Doc" (later identified as Ernest Pecci), introduced him to the neighbourhood. Through long immersion he documented the internal structure of these groups: the informal hierarchy of leadership, the rules of loyalty and status, and the links between the groups, local politics and organised crime. A leader's place rested on meeting obligations and on the confidence of members, and performance in activities such as bowling tended to match a man's standing in the group. He contrasted "corner boys," who stayed within the neighbourhood's networks, with "college boys," who sought upward mobility through education. Against the prevailing view that poor immigrant neighbourhoods suffered from "social disorganisation," he argued that such neighbourhoods have their own tightly knit social organisation, though one that differs from middle-class norms.

## Context and Background

Whyte was born in 1914 and came to Cornerville as a young researcher funded by Harvard's Society of Fellows. He was a middle-class, Protestant outsider among working-class Italian Catholic families, and his acceptance depended heavily on Doc's sponsorship and on his own willingness to take part in local life. The book was first published in 1943 and a methodological appendix was added in the second edition (1955), with further material in later editions; in the appendix Whyte describes his errors, dilemmas and relationship with his informant. His later career at Cornell, where he worked on industrial relations and participatory action research, extended his interest in how organisations function from the inside. He later took a doctorate at the University of Chicago.

## Impact

The book became a standard teaching model for urban ethnography, in large part because of its candid methodological appendix. It also became the focus of debate about the ethics and reliability of ethnography. In 1992 Marianne Boelen published a revisit of Cornerville that questioned parts of Whyte's account, including aspects of his portrayal of the neighbourhood and his informants; Whyte replied in the same journal, and the exchange raised wider questions about verifying long-term ethnographic data and about the confidentiality of research participants whose identities can later become known. Readers have also noted that the account concentrates on men.
