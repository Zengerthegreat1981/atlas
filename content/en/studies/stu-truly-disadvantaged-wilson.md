# The Truly Disadvantaged: The Inner City, the Underclass, and Public Policy

*The Truly Disadvantaged* (University of Chicago Press, 1987), by the sociologist William Julius Wilson, argues that the concentrated poverty of large American inner cities in the 1970s and 1980s was the product of structural economic change interacting with the legacy of racial discrimination, not of a self-perpetuating culture or of present-day racism alone.

## Argument

Wilson started from the observation that, from the 1970s, poverty, joblessness, single-parent families and other problems had become concentrated in Black inner-city neighbourhoods in cities such as Chicago. He identified several linked processes:

- Economic transformation: the decline of manufacturing and the movement of jobs away from central cities, together with the growth of service employment requiring more education, left many inner-city residents, especially young Black men, without stable work. (The "spatial mismatch" between where jobs and where Black workers lived had been described earlier by John Kain, 1968.)
- Male joblessness and family structure: Wilson used a "male marriageable pool index" to argue that the shortage of employed men helped explain declining marriage rates and the rise in female-headed families among Black Americans, an explanation he offered in place of cultural accounts of family breakdown.
- Class-selective out-migration: after the civil rights era opened up housing and employment to middle-class and working-class Black families, many moved out of the inner city, leaving neighbourhoods poorer and with fewer of the stable families and institutions that had earlier buffered the effects of joblessness.
- Concentration effects and social isolation: as poverty became concentrated, residents were increasingly isolated from mainstream institutions, job networks and role models, which made it harder to find work and reinforced hardship.

Wilson used the term "underclass" for this concentrated disadvantage, and he argued that policies should be universal in appeal, such as full employment, education and child care, rather than narrowly race-specific.

## Intellectual Context

Wilson, then a professor at the University of Chicago and later at Harvard University, had argued in *The Declining Significance of Race* (1978) that class had become more important than race in determining the life chances of Blacks in the economic sphere. The book drew criticism, and *The Truly Disadvantaged* elaborated his position and responded to critics. He argued that historical discrimination had produced segregation and concentration, but that later economic shifts best explained the deepening of disadvantage. He also criticised liberals for avoiding discussion of the behaviour and family patterns associated with concentrated poverty, a reticence he linked to the controversy over the 1965 Moynihan Report, whose concern with family structure he partly revived but explained in structural terms. In *When Work Disappears* (1996), he extended the argument using survey and ethnographic evidence from Chicago.

## Impact and Critique

The book became one of the most influential works on urban poverty and shaped debates about the underclass, neighbourhood effects and anti-poverty policy. Critics argued that "underclass" is a stigmatising label, that the account underplays the continuing role of residential segregation, which Douglas Massey and Nancy Denton emphasised in *American Apartheid* (1993), and that the evidence for some of its mechanisms, such as the role of middle-class out-migration, is contested.
