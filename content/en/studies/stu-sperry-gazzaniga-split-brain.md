# Sperry & Gazzaniga Split-Brain Studies

Historic experimental neurological studies on patients who underwent section of the corpus callosum, showing that, once disconnected, each cerebral hemisphere can perceive, learn and respond largely independently of the other, and that the hemispheres differ in specialization for language and spatial processing.

## Context and experimental design

Patients with severe epilepsy who had undergone corpus callosum disconnection surgery were examined. Images and visual stimuli were presented for fractions of a second in the right visual field (processed by the linguistic left hemisphere) or the left visual field (processed by the non-linguistic right hemisphere). The patient was asked to name verbally what he or she had seen, or to identify it by touching an object with the left or right hand out of sight.

## Results and conclusions

When an object or word was shown in the right field, the patient could name it with ease. When it was shown in the left field, the patient typically said he had seen nothing, yet his left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere) could pick out the matching object from among several hidden objects. In later work (Gazzaniga and LeDoux, 1978), when each hemisphere was shown a different picture (for example a chicken claw to the left hemisphere and a snow scene to the right) and the patient chose matching pictures with each hand, the verbal left hemisphere readily explained the left hand's choice with an invented but plausible account. Gazzaniga proposed that the left hemisphere houses an "interpreter" that constructs explanations for behavior, contributing to a coherent self-narrative.

## Critique and methodological controversy

Later work indicates that functional specialization is not an absolute split as popular culture presents it (an emotional mind and a logical mind), but involves dynamic integration across multiple neural pathways. The core finding, the independence of each hemisphere's processing after callosal disconnection, has been confirmed over decades of examination of further split-brain patients, although the number of cases is small owing to the rarity of this kind of surgery, and the interpreter account remains a theoretical interpretation rather than an established mechanism.

## Nobel Prize and scientific impact
Roger Sperry received half of the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (the other half shared by David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel) for his discoveries on the functional specialization of the cerebral hemispheres. This research was a major experimental basis for understanding brain lateralization, that is, that certain functions such as language (usually in the left hemisphere) and spatial processing (often stronger in the right hemisphere) are distributed asymmetrically, with a warning against the excessive popular simplification that promoted the dichotomy of "creative right brain versus logical left brain" without sufficient scientific support in this stark form.
