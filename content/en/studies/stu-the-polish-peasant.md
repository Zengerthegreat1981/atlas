# The Polish Peasant

The Polish Peasant in Europe and America, published in five volumes between 1918 and 1920 by William I. Thomas and Florian Znaniecki, is widely regarded as a landmark of early empirical sociology. It is a study of Polish peasants — in their villages in partitioned Poland and as immigrants in the United States, above all in Chicago — based not on statistics or interviews but on the documents of ordinary life: letters, diaries, newspaper reports, parish records, court files and, most famously, an extended autobiography written by a single immigrant, Wladek Wiszniewski. From this mountain of personal material, Thomas and Znaniecki built a theory of social change that shaped the Chicago School and, through it, modern sociology.

## The Researchers and the Question

William Isaac Thomas (1863–1947), a sociologist at the University of Chicago, had been interested in immigrant communities and in the sociology of race and culture contact since the early 1900s. He believed that the great sociological question of his time was how people adapt when the social world that formed them collapses — and Polish peasants, leaving a traditional village society for industrial America, were the perfect case. Thomas began collecting Polish letters and documents, and he travelled in Europe gathering material. In 1913, in Poland, he met Florian Znaniecki (1882–1958), a young Polish philosopher working for the Polish Emigrants' Protective Association. Znaniecki came to Chicago in 1914, after the outbreak of the First World War, and became Thomas's collaborator. Thomas supplied the research programme and the theory of social disorganisation; Znaniecki, trained in philosophy, supplied the methodological argument and much of the analysis.

Their question was twofold. First, what happens to a traditional peasant society when its members emigrate — how do family, religion, neighbourhood and custom survive, break down and reorganise? Second, and more ambitiously, how should sociology study such a process at all? The famous "Methodological Note" that opens the work argues that personal life records, "as complete as possible, constitute the perfect type of sociological material," because only they reveal the meanings that actors themselves attach to their actions.

## Method: The Personal Document

The study's method was the personal document, or life record. Thomas and Znaniecki assembled hundreds of letters exchanged between Polish immigrants in America and their families in Poland — letters that discuss money, marriage, quarrels, illness, religious observance and homesickness in unguarded detail. They added parish registers, court records, newspaper reports, the records of immigrant aid societies, and the autobiography of Wladek Wiszniewski. The first two volumes, published by the University of Chicago Press in 1918, set out the method and the organisation of peasant life in Poland; the third, published by Richard G. Badger of Boston in 1919, presented Wiszniewski's autobiography, the fourth (1920) treated disorganisation and reorganisation in Poland, and the fifth (1920) treated organisation and disorganisation in America. A second edition appeared in 1927.

The work was nearly derailed by scandal. In 1918 Thomas was arrested by federal agents in Chicago in a scandal involving a soldier's wife; he was not convicted, but the University of Chicago dismissed him, and the later volumes were published by a different house. Thomas never returned to a post at Chicago.

## Key Findings

The study's central theoretical contribution is the pair of concepts social disorganisation and social reorganisation. In the Polish village, Thomas and Znaniecki argued, life was governed by primary-group controls: the family, the parish and the neighbourhood enforced custom. Emigration broke these controls. In America, the immigrant found himself in a world where the old rules no longer applied, where money replaced land as the measure of status, and where traditional authority — the father, the priest, the village elders — had lost its power. The result was disorganisation: rising rates of family conflict, crime, divorce, juvenile delinquency and what the authors called "demoralisation."

But disorganisation was not the end of the story. Immigrants also reorganised: they formed new institutions — Polish parishes, mutual aid societies, fraternal organisations, the Polish-language press — that gave their lives a new structure. The American Polish community that emerged was neither a copy of the village nor an assimilation into Anglo-America, but something new. This emphasis on reorganisation, on immigrants as active creators of new social forms rather than passive victims of change, was one of the study's most durable insights.

## Reception and Influence

The Polish Peasant was recognised almost immediately as a landmark, and in 1939 the Social Science Research Council selected it for a special appraisal, publishing Herbert Blumer's critical assessment. Blumer praised the work's richness. Later critics noted that the authors' picture of the Polish village was idealised and that they relied heavily on a single autobiography.

The study's influence was large. It helped establish the life-history method and the use of personal documents as legitimate sociological tools, and it shaped the Chicago School's programme of urban ethnography for a generation. It shaped the sociology of immigration and the study of social change as a process rather than a state. And its methodological argument — that sociology must take the actor's point of view seriously — runs through symbolic interactionism, interpretive sociology and qualitative research to this day.

## Why It Matters

The Polish Peasant matters because it asked sociology to listen before it counted. At a time when the discipline was tempted to model itself on the natural sciences, Thomas and Znaniecki insisted that the meaning of a letter home, the humiliation of a broken engagement, the pride of a first pay packet, are sociological facts — and that without them, no amount of statistics can explain why people do what they do.
