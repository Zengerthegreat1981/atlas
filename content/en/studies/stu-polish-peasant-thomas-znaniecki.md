# The Polish Peasant in Europe and America

A five-volume study by William Isaac Thomas and Florian Znaniecki, published between 1918 and 1920 and regarded as one of the first major American sociological studies to use personal documents as its principal evidence.

## Method and Content

The authors drew on a large collection of letters exchanged between Polish immigrants in America and their families in Poland (collected partly through advertisements in the Polish-American press), an extended autobiography written at their request by an immigrant, Wladek Wiszniewski, and institutional materials such as records of immigrant associations, parish and court records and newspapers. Through these materials they studied how Polish peasant immigrants fared in the transition from a cohesive rural society, organised around the family and the local community, to an industrial American city marked by social disorganisation, individualism and competition. The work's "Methodological Note" argued that personal life records, as complete as possible, are the perfect type of sociological material because they reveal the meanings that actors attach to their own situations. The concept of the "definition of the situation" appears in the book; the formulation now known as the "Thomas theorem" comes from a later work by W. I. Thomas and Dorothy Swaine Thomas, *The Child in America* (1928).

## Impact

The study helped establish the personal document (or life history) as a recognised sociological source and shaped the qualitative, case-based approach associated with the Chicago school; it also influenced later interactionist thinking about subjective meaning. Its methodological ambitions led the Social Science Research Council to commission a critical appraisal in 1939.

## Critique

Herbert Blumer's appraisal (1939), written for the Social Science Research Council, praised the work's richness but questioned how far its documents could verify the authors' general theoretical propositions. He pointed to the problem of representativeness, since the letters were a self-selected collection and the main autobiography was the account of a single individual, and he argued that the documents were used to illustrate the theory more than to test it. Thomas and Znaniecki had argued that rich personal materials reveal subjective meanings that statistics cannot, and the debate over the value of such qualitative data against broader quantitative generalisation continued for decades.
