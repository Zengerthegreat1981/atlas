# A Randomized Trial of Meaning-Centered Psychotherapy

A series of randomized controlled trials led by William Breitbart at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York tested whether a brief, structured psychotherapy built around the search for meaning could relieve the existential suffering of patients with advanced cancer. Meaning-Centered Psychotherapy (MCP), developed by Breitbart and his colleagues from Viktor Frankl's logotherapy, was examined first in a group format, then in an individual format, and finally in a large definitive trial. Across these studies, patients who received MCP showed greater gains in spiritual well-being, sense of meaning, and quality of life, and greater reductions in hopelessness and desire for hastened death, than patients who received supportive psychotherapy or therapeutic massage. The program is one of the rare cases in which a psychotherapy drawn directly from existential philosophy was subjected to the strict standards of evidence-based medicine — and passed.

## Background and Context

Frankl argued in *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946) that the will to meaning is a primary human motive, and that people can find meaning even in unavoidable suffering — through creative work, through love, and through the attitude they take toward it. Breitbart, a psychiatrist working in psycho-oncology, saw this framework as directly relevant to patients with advanced cancer, many of whom suffered less from pain than from a collapse of meaning: hopelessness, spiritual distress, and a wish to hasten death.

Before Breitbart's trials, the existential psychotherapy tradition rested almost entirely on philosophical argument and case studies. No manualized existential treatment had been tested in a randomized trial. Breitbart's group set out to change that, developing a treatment manual with set themes for each session and testing it against credible control treatments at a major cancer center.

## Design and Method

The program unfolded in three trials. A pilot randomized trial of the group format, published in 2010, assigned 90 patients with advanced cancer to eight weekly 90-minute sessions of Meaning-Centered Group Psychotherapy (MCGP) or to supportive group psychotherapy. Each session combined brief teaching, discussion, and experiential exercises organized around a theme of meaning: the sources of meaning in one's life history, meaning through creativity and responsibility, meaning through love and encounter, and meaning through the attitude taken toward suffering.

A pilot trial of the individual format, published in 2012, assigned 120 patients with stage III or IV cancer to seven sessions of Individual Meaning-Centered Psychotherapy (IMCP) or to therapeutic massage, with assessments before and after treatment and again two months later.

The definitive trial, published in 2015, was a large randomized controlled trial (registered as NCT00494910) in which 253 patients with advanced cancer were assigned to eight sessions of MCGP or supportive group psychotherapy. Outcomes across the trials included spiritual well-being and sense of meaning, quality of life, anxiety, hopelessness, and desire for hastened death, measured with validated scales.

## Key Findings

The group pilot (2010) found that patients in MCGP showed significantly greater increases in spiritual well-being and sense of meaning than patients in supportive group psychotherapy, along with reductions in hopelessness. The individual pilot (2012) found that IMCP produced significantly greater improvement than therapeutic massage on the primary outcomes of spiritual well-being (b = 0.39, p < .001) — including both sense of meaning (b = 0.34, p = .003) and faith (b = 0.42, p = .03) — and quality of life (b = 0.76, p = .013). Of the 120 participants randomized, 78 (65%) completed the post-treatment assessment and 67 (56%) completed the two-month follow-up.

The large 2015 trial confirmed and extended these results: MCGP reduced psychological distress and end-of-life despair and improved spiritual well-being more than supportive group psychotherapy, providing the strongest evidence yet for an existential psychotherapy tested under controlled conditions.

## Significance and Reception

The Breitbart trials mattered for two reasons. Clinically, they gave palliative care teams a brief, manualized intervention that could be delivered in seven or eight sessions to patients whose time was short — a practical answer to spiritual suffering that drugs alone could not address. MCP has since been adapted for other populations, including cancer survivors, caregivers, and patients in other cultural settings, and has been incorporated into palliative psychiatry training.

Methodologically, the trials set a precedent. Existential and humanistic psychotherapies had long been criticized as untestable; Breitbart's group showed that a meaning-based intervention could be manualized, delivered consistently, and evaluated with the same rigor applied to cognitive-behavioral protocols. The work helped existential psychotherapy gain a hearing in psycho-oncology and gave Frankl's ideas an empirical footing they had previously lacked.

## Limitations and Critique

The trials have real limits. Attrition was high, as the 2012 pilot shows: more than a third of randomized patients did not complete post-treatment assessment, and nearly half were lost by two-month follow-up — unsurprising in advanced cancer, but a threat to the robustness of the findings. The short-term gains in the individual pilot were no longer significantly greater than the control condition at two-month follow-up, raising questions about how durable the benefits are without booster sessions or ongoing support.

The comparison conditions also invite scrutiny: therapeutic massage controls for attention and human contact but not for a structured psychological treatment, and supportive group psychotherapy is a relatively weak comparator. The trials were conducted largely at a single elite cancer center in New York, with patients willing and able to participate in research, so generalizability to other settings — community hospitals, other cultures, patients with fewer resources — remains an open question. Finally, the mechanism is unresolved: whether the gains come specifically from the meaning-focused content, or from common factors such as a skilled therapist's empathic attention, has not been separated experimentally.
