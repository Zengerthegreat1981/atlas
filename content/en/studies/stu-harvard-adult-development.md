# Harvard Study of Adult Development (Grant & Glueck Longitudinal Studies)

A historic longitudinal study at Harvard University that began in 1938 and followed 724 men throughout their lives (268 Harvard students, among them, reportedly, John F. Kennedy, and 456 boys from the poorest neighborhoods of Boston), tracking health, adjustment, and well-being across more than eight decades.

## Context and experimental design

Two contrasting samples were combined: the sample of the "Grant" study (outstanding Harvard students) and the "Glueck" sample (boys from the poor and deprived neighborhoods of Boston). The participants were followed with periodic questionnaires (every two years), medical examinations, interviews, and in later waves blood tests and brain scans, and some wives and children were also interviewed, over more than 85 years.

## Results and conclusions

Robert Waldinger, the study's current director, summarizes its central message as follows: close, satisfying relationships are associated with better health and greater happiness. In reports from the study, satisfaction with relationships at age fifty predicted physical health at age eighty better than midlife cholesterol level did, and loneliness was associated with poorer health and earlier mortality. George Vaillant also reported that adaptive, "mature" psychological defenses (such as altruism, humor, and sublimation, in his hierarchy of defenses) were associated with better adjustment across adult life. These are correlational findings and do not by themselves show that relationships cause better health.

## Criticism and methodological controversy

The original sample consisted only of men, almost all white, from a specific American setting, so generalization is limited. The study has since added the wives and the more than 1,300 children of the original participants, including women, and in later years recruited additional participants, which broadens its scope. The link between social relationships and health has been supported by independent research (for example the meta-analysis by Holt-Lunstad and colleagues, 2010), though the Harvard data are observational and cannot establish causation.

## Continuity of the study's leadership

The leadership of the study passed through generations of researchers: Arlie Bock began it, then George Vaillant directed it for many decades and documented its results in his book *Adaptation to Life*, and then Robert Waldinger took over its direction more recently and widened its scope to include the wives and children of the original participants (the second-generation study), in one of the longest-running longitudinal studies in the history of psychology.
