# Gain-of-Function Ethics Ethnography

## Subject

Gain-of-function (GOF) research refers to experiments aimed at increasing the transmissibility or virulence of pathogens (such as viruses), often in order to understand the mechanisms by which they spread to humans or to assess their potential epidemic risks before they occur naturally. Since the mid-2010s it has been a field of intense ethical and political sensitivity. It balances a possible scientific benefit for epidemic preparedness against the risk that modified pathogens could escape from the laboratory, either accidentally or through deliberate misuse.

## Ethical and Regulatory Context

The publication of research that modified the avian influenza virus H5N1 to make it more transmissible between mammals (2011–2012) sparked a wide international controversy. In 2014 the Obama administration paused new federal funding for certain gain-of-function research on influenza, SARS, and MERS viruses, encouraged a voluntary moratorium on related ongoing work, and tasked the U.S. National Science Advisory Board for Biosecurity (NSABB) with studying the risks and benefits before funding resumed in 2017. The debate returned strongly after the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), in the context of questions about the possible laboratory origin of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, which reopened the discussion about the oversight of this kind of research worldwide.

## Anthropological Contribution

Little or no published anthropological ethnography targets specific gain-of-function laboratories. However, the broader field of "anthropology of pandemic preparedness" has offered analytical tools of direct relevance. The medical anthropologist **Carlo Caduff** studied, in his book *The Pandemic Perhaps: Dramatic Events in a Public Culture of Danger* (2015), how scientists and public health officials in New York escalated preparations for an anticipated catastrophic influenza pandemic that did not actually occur. He analyzed the logic of "pandemic prophecy," which justifies costly proactive interventions based on catastrophic scenarios that are possible rather than actual events. The sociologist **Andrew Lakoff**, in his work on "preparedness," including *Unprepared: Global Health in a Time of Emergency* (2017), traced the concept of preparedness to Cold War civil-defence planning and argued that it is not a neutral alternative to "prevention" but a hybrid political-epistemic field that blends epidemic theory with national security practices.

## Open Ethical Questions

The debate over gain-of-function research remains open on several questions: who holds the authority to approve research with "dual use" potential that could be exploited for harmful purposes; how the probable scientific benefit is weighed against the risk of a real, if rare, leak; and what level of transparency and international oversight is needed in laboratories operating under multiple national sovereignties. In this context, anthropology offers a tool for understanding how "accepted risk" is produced socially and politically, rather than being merely a purely technical judgment.

## Ethnography around the Laboratories

A published ethnography of a named gain-of-function laboratory, written from inside its daily work, is still scarce. Closest is Natalie Porter's study of the controversy that followed the 2011 avian influenza experiments already noted above. In *BioSocieties* (2016) she combines published scientific papers, interviews with microbiologists and with people who regulate biosecurity, and ethnographic observation in a biosecure laboratory. Her argument is that new rules did not stay on paper. They changed how experimental systems were organized, including how experimental organisms were used and interpreted, and they encouraged a culture of security among scientists who work across national borders. She treats those changes as the joint product of local pressures to get research done and of wider worries about accident and misuse. The article is therefore an ethnography of the controversy and of laboratory practice under biosecurity rules, rather than a portrait of one gain-of-function project from start to finish.
