# Suburban Dreams

This entry, listed under the title "Suburban Dreams", presents Becky M. Nicolaides's history of working-class suburbia in Los Angeles, published as *My Blue Heaven: Life and Politics in the Working-Class Suburbs of Los Angeles, 1920–1965* (University of Chicago Press, 2002). Nicolaides, a historian, built the book around South Gate, an industrial suburb southeast of downtown Los Angeles whose residents worked in nearby plants such as those of Firestone and General Motors.

## Argument

Popular images of postwar suburbia, and much early scholarship, equated the suburb with the white middle class. Nicolaides argues that a large part of American suburban growth was in fact blue-collar. In South Gate, working families, many of them migrants from other parts of the country, bought inexpensive lots and, in many cases, built or improved their own houses over time, keeping gardens and sometimes small livestock. For them, home ownership offered security in an industrial economy that was prone to layoffs, and it was tied to an identity as respectable, independent and different from the inner city.

The study traces how this outlook shaped politics. During the Depression and the New Deal years, South Gate's residents supported the Democratic Party and the labour movement, seeing government and unions as protections for working families. After the Second World War, Nicolaides argues, a stronger homeowner identity, concern about property values and taxes, and the defence of a racially exclusive community pushed many residents toward a more conservative suburban politics. Race is central to her account: South Gate's whiteness was actively maintained, and its history is part of the story of how working-class white suburbs resisted integration.

## Method

The book combines archival sources such as local government records, newspapers and housing records with oral-history interviews with residents. As a study of one community it cannot show how typical South Gate was, but it is intended as a counterweight to the assumption that suburbs were uniformly middle class.

## Relation to Other Suburban Studies

Nicolaides's work complements Herbert Gans's ethnography *The Levittowners* (1967), which studied a lower-middle-class and working-class planned suburb in New Jersey, and the broader historical account in Kenneth Jackson's *Crabgrass Frontier* (1985). She also co-edited with Andrew Wiese *The Suburb Reader* (2006), a collection of key texts on suburban history.
