# Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" Study and Information Asymmetry (1970)

In 1970 George Akerlof published "The Market for 'Lemons': Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism" in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*. He later recounted that leading journals had rejected the paper before the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* accepted it. The article became a standard reference for how asymmetric information can upset a market. It did not by itself create the economics of information. Michael Spence's work on signalling and Joseph Stiglitz's work on screening were developed alongside it, and the three shared the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel in 2001.

## The Model: The Used-Car Market as an Example

Akerlof built his analysis on the example of the used-car market. The seller knows the precise mechanical condition of his car (sound, or a "lemon" with a hidden defect, in American slang), whereas a prospective buyer has only average, estimated information about the quality of cars generally offered on the market. The buyer therefore forms an expectation of a reasonable price that reflects the average expected quality, not the actual quality of any particular car.

## The Mechanism: Adverse Selection and Market Collapse

The consequence of this situation is that sellers of genuinely good cars, who know that the market price on offer is below the true value of their good car, gradually withdraw from offering their cars for sale in this market. This leaves the field largely to sellers of bad cars, for whom the average market price is a relatively profitable price. This mechanism is known as **adverse selection**. With repetition of this cycle, the average quality on offer may fall successively, until the market theoretically collapses entirely in the extreme case, leaving only the worst goods while buyers' confidence vanishes completely.

## What the paper changed in microeconomics

Akerlof's example showed that a market can unravel even when buyers and sellers are willing to trade at some price that would be mutually beneficial if quality were known. The paper also discussed insurance, credit markets in poorer countries, and the employment of minorities, where the uninformed side cannot tell high-quality from low-quality counterparties. In the insurance discussion, people who know they are higher risk are more eager to buy cover at a premium set for the average risk. That is adverse selection. Akerlof treated guarantees and brand names as private devices that can keep higher-quality sellers in the market. He likened the withdrawal of good cars to Gresham's law, with a bad product driving out a good one, but the mechanism is lack of information rather than a fixed legal price of money.

Michael Rothschild and Joseph Stiglitz (1976) later set out a formal competitive-insurance model in which equilibrium may fail to exist, or may involve contracts that screen risk types. That paper is a sequel in the same research programme, not a result already proved in Akerlof's 1970 article. Labour-market and credit-market applications were developed by other authors, including Stiglitz and Andrew Weiss (1981) on credit rationing. Using the lemons logic to discuss microfinance is a later application. Akerlof's article does not report a microfinance study.

## A note on replication

The paper is a theoretical model, so there is no data set to replicate. Later empirical work has looked for adverse selection in insurance, credit and other markets, with results that depend on the market and on whether hidden information can be separated from hidden behaviour (moral hazard).
