# Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" Study and Information Asymmetry (1970)

In 1970 George Akerlof published his landmark paper "The Market for 'Lemons': Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism" in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, after several prestigious economics journals had rejected it before it was accepted. It later became one of the most influential papers in the history of modern microeconomics, and effectively founded the field of the economics of asymmetric information.

## The Model: The Used-Car Market as an Example

Akerlof built his analysis on the example of the used-car market. The seller knows the precise mechanical condition of his car (sound, or a "lemon" with a hidden defect, in American slang), whereas a prospective buyer has only average, estimated information about the quality of cars generally offered on the market. The buyer therefore forms an expectation of a reasonable price that reflects the average expected quality, not the actual quality of any particular car.

## The Mechanism: Adverse Selection and Market Collapse

The consequence of this situation is that sellers of genuinely good cars, who know that the market price on offer is below the true value of their good car, gradually withdraw from offering their cars for sale in this market. This leaves the field largely to sellers of bad cars, for whom the average market price is a relatively profitable price. This mechanism is known as **adverse selection**. With repetition of this cycle, the average quality on offer may fall successively, until the market theoretically collapses entirely in the extreme case, leaving only the worst goods while buyers' confidence vanishes completely.

## The Impact: Founding the Economics of Information

This paper, together with Michael Spence's work on signalling and Joseph Stiglitz's work on screening, opened an entirely new field of microeconomics that studies the effect of information asymmetry between parties to economic transactions on market efficiency. The three shared the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2001 in recognition of this contribution, which undermined the assumption of perfect information on which many traditional neoclassical economic models were built.

## Wide Applications

The model's influence went beyond the used-car market to explain a range of economic phenomena. These include the failure of health insurance markets when healthy individuals gradually withdraw, leaving those with higher health risk (which later helped justify compulsory health insurance in some public policies), and the role of quality certificates and commercial guarantees as signalling mechanisms through which better-informed parties try to distinguish themselves from worse ones in the same market.

The same adverse-selection mechanism is also invoked in analysing micro-finance and credit markets in many developing economies, where lenders face a similar difficulty in distinguishing borrowers who will repay from others without sufficient reliable credit information.

## A Note on Replication

Because the paper is a theoretical model, direct replication does not apply. What has been repeated is empirical testing of adverse-selection mechanisms in many real markets, such as insurance and credit.
