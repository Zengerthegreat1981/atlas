# Pinned Down

This entry, listed under the title "Pinned Down", presents the sociologist Arne L. Kalleberg's work on precarious employment in the United States, developed in *Good Jobs, Bad Jobs: The Rise of Polarized and Precarious Employment Systems in the United States, 1970s–2000s* (2011) and extended comparatively in *Precarious Lives: Job Insecurity and Well-Being in Rich Democracies* (2018). The title evokes the condition Kalleberg describes: workers held in place by jobs that are insecure, poorly paid and short of benefits, with little prospect of moving to something better. Kalleberg, Kenan Distinguished Professor of Sociology at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, argues that the American labour market has polarised since the 1970s into good jobs and bad jobs, and that the growth of precarious employment is one of the central facts of contemporary American life, with consequences for family formation, health and well-being.

The study begins from the postwar bargain. In the decades after the Second World War, Kalleberg shows, relatively secure, well-paid industrial jobs enabled many American workers to join the middle class, supported by unions, labour law and employers who offered pensions and health insurance. From the 1970s onwards that bargain unravelled. Deregulation, global competition, the decline of manufacturing and the growth of the service sector shifted employment toward jobs that paid less and offered less security, while institutional protections such as unions weakened. Union membership among American wage and salary workers fell from 20.1 percent in 1983 to 10.7 percent in 2017 (US Bureau of Labor Statistics figures), one measure of the decline Kalleberg documents. The result, he argues, is a labour market in which the risks of work have shifted from employers and governments onto workers.

## Method

Kalleberg's method is quantitative and comparative. *Good Jobs, Bad Jobs* uses survey data on the American labour market, including the Current Population Survey and the General Social Survey, to trace trends in job quality, such as wages, benefits, security and control over working time, from the 1970s to the 2000s, and to examine how those trends differed by race, gender, education and immigration status. *Precarious Lives* extends the analysis comparatively, examining the United States alongside other rich democracies (including Denmark, Germany, Japan, Spain and the United Kingdom) and asking how national institutions — labour law, welfare systems and systems of worker representation — shape the experience of insecurity.

Kalleberg defines precarious work as employment that is uncertain, unpredictable and risky from the point of view of the worker. In his 2009 article "Precarious Work, Insecure Workers" he treats precariousness as a property of the employment relationship, which is distinct from poverty, a property of income, and he links its spread to weakened institutional protections. The closely related idea of the "precariat" was developed separately by Guy Standing.

## Key Findings

The first finding is polarisation. Kalleberg shows that the gap between good and bad jobs widened from the 1970s to the 2000s: the best jobs paid more and gave more autonomy, while the worst offered lower pay, fewer benefits and less security. The second finding is that precarious work is not confined to the margins of the economy; it has spread from temporary and part-time work into broader sections of the labour market, including service work and, increasingly, professional and educational employment. The third finding concerns who bears the burden. Black workers, Latino workers, immigrants and women are over-represented in lower-quality jobs, so that polarisation reinforces existing inequalities of race and gender.

## Reception and Influence

*Good Jobs, Bad Jobs* was published by the Russell Sage Foundation in 2011 as a volume in the American Sociological Association's Rose Series in Sociology, and it has become a standard reference in the sociology of work. *Precarious Lives* was published by Polity Press in 2018. Kalleberg's account of precarious work is widely cited alongside David Weil's analysis of the "fissured workplace" (2014), which stresses how firms push employment risk onto subcontractors, franchisees and temporary workers. Critics note that survey measures of job quality capture only part of precarity, and that debates continue over how far the postwar secure job was ever available to women and minority workers.
