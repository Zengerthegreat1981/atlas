# Miller's Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two

A historical review and research paper that demonstrated that human short-term memory has a very limited processing capacity of 7 ± 2 perceptual units or grouped chunks (Chunks).

## Context and experimental design

Miller reviewed the results of varied experiments in the field of one-dimensional absolute judgment (discriminating pitches, loudness and tastes) and experiments on the immediate span of immediate memory for digits, letters and words, analyzing them using the concepts of Shannon's information theory (communication channel and transmission capacity).

## Results and conclusions

Miller found a striking consistency in the limits of human perceptual capacity; immediate memory accommodates about 7 units (between 5 and 9). He explained that the mind overcomes this constraint through the process of "chunking," that is, merging separate elements into larger semantic units, which allows the retention of larger quantities of information, since the limit applies to the number of chunks rather than to the amount of information in each.

## Criticism and methodological controversy

Modern cognitive psychology research (Nelson Cowan, about half a century after Miller's paper) argued that the capacity of working memory, when rehearsal and chunking strategies are prevented, is closer to about 4 chunks; Miller's paper nonetheless remains a landmark of the information-processing approach in psychology.

## Relation to Baddeley and Hitch's working memory model

Miller's concept of a single short-term span was later developed, in 1974, into Baddeley and Hitch's multicomponent working memory model. Instead of a single fixed span, this model proposed separate limited-capacity stores (the phonological loop and the visuospatial sketchpad) coordinated by a central executive. Within such accounts, span measures such as Miller's depend partly on rehearsal and chunking strategies, which is one reason Cowan's estimate of about 4 chunks, obtained when such strategies are blocked, is lower.

## Impact on the cognitive revolution
Miller's paper is often cited among the early landmarks of what became known as the "cognitive revolution" in psychology, since it borrowed from Claude Shannon's information theory concepts such as the "bit" and the "channel" to describe the human mind as an information-processing system of limited capacity, instead of viewing it as a behavioral black box as the then-dominant behaviorist approach tended to do. This conceptual shift paved the way directly for the emergence of cognitive psychology as an independent field in the late 1950s and the 1960s.
