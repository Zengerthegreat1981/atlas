# The Second Shift in China

This entry, listed under the title "The Second Shift in China", discusses how the "second shift" described by Arlie Russell Hochschild in the United States (*The Second Shift*, 1989) has been applied to women's paid and unpaid work in China. Hochschild's own study concerned American dual-earner couples; the Chinese case comes from other researchers.

## Context

After 1949 the Chinese state promoted women's participation in paid work as part of socialist transformation, and urban women's labour-force participation rose to very high levels, summed up in the slogan that women "hold up half the sky". Policies of this period changed women's employment far more than they changed the division of domestic labour. Historians and sociologists, such as Judith Stacey in *Patriarchy and Socialist Revolution in China* (1983), have argued that the revolution weakened the old patriarchal family but left many of its gendered assumptions in place, and that women commonly carried the main responsibility for housework and child care alongside paid employment, a pattern close to Hochschild's "second shift".

## Research

Studies of urban couples in China, such as work by Jiping Zuo and Yanjie Bian (2001), examine how housework is divided and how spouses' relative resources and ideas about fairness shape that division. They find that although urban Chinese women have long been employed, they have generally done more of the housework than men. Such research uses surveys of households and perceived fairness rather than the in-depth couple interviews used by Hochschild, and the data come from specific cities and periods, so they should not be read as describing all of China.

## Why It Matters

The Chinese case shows that high female employment under a state committed to gender equality in the workplace does not by itself equalise domestic labour, and it highlights how the second shift can arise under very different political and economic systems.

## Time-Use Findings

China's National Bureau of Statistics has measured how residents divide their days. Its 2018 survey covered 48,580 people in eleven provincial-level regions. As reported by *Beijing Review*, women spent on average 3 hours 48 minutes a day on unpaid work and men 1 hour 32 minutes. Housework took women 2 hours 6 minutes and men 45 minutes; child care averaged 53 minutes for women and 17 minutes for men. About 84 percent of women took part in unpaid work, compared with about 55 percent of men. The bureau's communiqué on its third survey, conducted in 2024, reports that among those who took part in unpaid labour, men spent 1 hour 52 minutes a day on it and women 3 hours 29 minutes. The two sets of figures are not directly comparable: the 2024 values quoted here are averages for participants, while the 2018 durations are averages across respondents. National averages also include people who are not in paid work or have no young children, so they indicate a gendered gap in unpaid labour without measuring the second shift of employed parents specifically.
