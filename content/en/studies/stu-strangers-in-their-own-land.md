# Strangers in Their Own Land

Why would people who live beside some of the worst industrial pollution in America vote for politicians who promise to deregulate the industries that poison them? That question — which Arlie Russell Hochschild called the "Great Paradox" — sent her from Berkeley, California, to the bayous of Louisiana, where over five years of repeated visits she spent time with Tea Party supporters, attended their church suppers and fish fries, listened to their stories, and tried to understand how the world feels from their side. Strangers in Their Own Land: Anger and Mourning on the American Right, published in 2016, is an ethnography of emotion in American politics, and an attempt to climb what Hochschild calls the "empathy wall" between liberals and conservatives.

## The Researcher and the Question

Arlie Russell Hochschild, professor emerita of sociology at the University of California, Berkeley, was already a leading sociologist of emotion. Her book The Managed Heart (1983) had introduced the concepts of emotional labour and feeling rules; The Second Shift (1989), written with Anne Machung, had shown how working mothers carried a double burden of paid and unpaid work. In Strangers in Their Own Land she turned the sociology of emotion to a political puzzle: the rise of the Tea Party and, by the time the book appeared, of Donald Trump.

Hochschild chose Louisiana deliberately. It was one of the most politically conservative states in America, and it was also home to some of the worst environmental pollution in the country: the stretch of the lower Mississippi between Baton Rouge and New Orleans, lined with petrochemical plants, is known as Cancer Alley. Residents were getting sick — cancers, respiratory illnesses, children with rare diseases — and yet many of them opposed environmental regulation and the federal government that might enforce it. Hochschild wanted to understand not whether their views were correct, but how they felt true: what story about America made these positions feel reasonable, even honourable, to the people who held them?

## Method: Climbing the Empathy Wall

The method was sustained, immersive ethnography. Over five years Hochschild made repeated extended visits to Louisiana, building relationships with a core group of Tea Party supporters and their families. She interviewed dozens of people, attended rallies, church services and community gatherings, shared meals, looked at family photo albums, and listened — often for hours — to life stories. She also read the books her subjects read, watched the television they watched, and studied the history of the region's oil and petrochemical industries.

Hochschild defined the "empathy wall" as an obstacle to deep understanding of another person, one that can make people feel indifferent or even hostile toward those who hold different views or come from different backgrounds. Her method was designed to scale that wall: not to agree with her subjects, but to see reality through their eyes, to understand the links between life, feeling and politics. She was explicit about her own position — a liberal sociologist from Berkeley — and about the moments when her own feelings of judgement threatened the work.

## Key Findings

The book's central finding is the "deep story" that Hochschild distilled from her subjects' accounts. Imagine, she writes, waiting patiently in a long line leading up a hill toward the American Dream. You have worked hard, played by the rules, endured hardship. Then you see people cutting in line ahead of you helped by a president who seems to be on their side. And when you complain, someone ahead of you turns around and calls you an ignorant redneck. Economically, culturally, demographically and politically, you have become a stranger in your own land. The deep story, Hochschild argues, is not a set of policy positions but a structure of feeling: pride in endurance, resentment at being overtaken, grief for a country that seems to have moved on without you.

A second finding concerns the moral economy of the region. Hochschild's subjects were not ignorant of pollution: many had lost family members to cancer and knew the plants were dangerous. But they understood the plants as sources of jobs, identity and community, and they understood regulation as an insult — an accusation from distant liberals that they were too stupid or too selfish to protect their own families. Their opposition to government was therefore not primarily about economics but about honour.

## Reception and Influence

Strangers in Their Own Land was a National Book Award finalist for nonfiction in 2016 and became one of the most discussed books of the Trump era. Reviewers noted that Hochschild had done something rare: she had taken conservative voters seriously as moral actors rather than treating them as puzzles to be solved or dupes to be pitied. The concepts of the empathy wall and the deep story entered the vocabulary of political sociology, journalism and public debate, and the book was read alongside other studies of rural and conservative America, such as Katherine Cramer's The Politics of Resentment (2016) and Robert Wuthnow's The Left Behind (2018). Critics noted that the book rests on a small number of mainly white, conservative informants in one region, that the deep story is an interpretive device and not a tested finding, and that it gives limited weight to the role of race in shaping her subjects' views and in shaping who is seen as cutting in line.

## Why It Matters

Strangers in Their Own Land matters because it offered an influential way of studying political division. It showed that political disagreement is not only a disagreement about facts or interests, but a disagreement about feeling: about who deserves respect, who has suffered, and who is entitled to the future. That insight has shaped research on populism around the world, and it has shaped journalism, where the practice of "going to listen" to voters in places reporters rarely visit owes much to Hochschild's example.
