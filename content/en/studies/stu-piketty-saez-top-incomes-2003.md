# Piketty & Saez's Top Incomes in the United States (2003)

This is a landmark study by **Thomas Piketty** and **Emmanuel Saez**, published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* in 2003. It reconstructed the history of income distribution specifically among wealthy Americans over almost a full century. It used an innovative methodology that relied on actual income tax records rather than conventional survey data. The study became the methodological foundation on which the broader "World Inequality Database" project was later built.

## The methodological problem: the inadequacy of surveys for measuring the top

Earlier inequality studies relied mainly on household sample surveys (such as the US Current Population Survey). These surveys have a well-known methodological weakness in recording very high incomes. First, the very wealthy tend to refuse to take part in surveys or to understate their actual income. Second, many of these surveys apply "top-coding", capping the recorded income at a certain ceiling for reasons of statistical confidentiality. This effectively hides the real variations within the wealthiest segment, which is precisely the segment most important for understanding modern inequality dynamics.

## The methodological solution: tax records as an alternative source

Piketty and Saez overcame this problem by using US federal income tax records that have been published statistically (not private individual files, but the aggregated statistical tables that the tax authority publishes annually). These records have been available historically since the federal income tax was introduced in 1913. This made it possible to reconstruct a long and relatively reliable time series of the shares of national income going to the top 10%, the top 1%, and even the top 0.1% of the population, with a precision that conventional household surveys do not allow.

## The central finding: a U-shaped curve across the twentieth century

The reconstructed data revealed a striking historical pattern resembling the letter U. The share of national income going to the top 1% was very high in the 1920s. It then fell sharply between the Great Depression and the Second World War, mostly in the 1940s, a drop commonly called the "Great Compression" (a term introduced by Goldin and Margo in 1992). It remained at relatively low levels throughout the post-war decades of economic prosperity, with steadily rising average wages. The trend then reversed sharply and rapidly from the early 1980s onward. The share of the top 1% then rose steeply until, by the late 1990s, it approached its earlier high levels recorded before the Great Depression. This is a temporal pattern of sharp clarity that conventional survey data had not revealed with the same precision before.

## The wider methodological and intellectual impact

This study laid the methodological foundation on which Piketty's book *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (French original 2013; English 2014) was later built. The project itself later expanded, with the participation of Anthony Atkinson, Facundo Alvaredo, Gabriel Zucman and others, to cover dozens of countries around the world through the "World Inequality Database". This uses the same methodology (tax records rather than surveys alone) to build comparative historical series of the distribution of income and wealth globally.
