# Piketty & Saez's Top Incomes in the United States (2003)

This is a landmark study by **Thomas Piketty** and **Emmanuel Saez**, published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* in 2003. It reconstructed the history of income distribution specifically among wealthy Americans over almost a full century. It used an innovative methodology that relied on actual income tax records rather than conventional survey data. The study became the methodological foundation on which the broader "World Inequality Database" project was later built.

## The methodological problem: the inadequacy of surveys for measuring the top

Earlier inequality studies relied mainly on household sample surveys (such as the US Current Population Survey). These surveys have a well-known methodological weakness in recording very high incomes. First, the very wealthy tend to refuse to take part in surveys or to understate their actual income. Second, many of these surveys apply "top-coding", capping the recorded income at a certain ceiling for reasons of statistical confidentiality. This hides variation inside the top of the distribution, which is the part of the distribution the tax-table method is meant to recover.

## The methodological solution: tax records as an alternative source

Piketty and Saez overcame this problem by using US federal income tax records that have been published statistically (not private individual files, but the aggregated statistical tables that the tax authority publishes annually). These records have been available historically since the federal income tax was introduced in 1913. This made it possible to reconstruct a long and relatively reliable time series of the shares of national income going to the top 10%, the top 1%, and even the top 0.1% of the population, with a precision that conventional household surveys do not allow.

## The central finding: a U-shaped curve across the twentieth century

The series has a U-shape. The share of fiscal income going to the top 1 percent was high in the late 1920s, fell from the Depression through the 1940s, stayed comparatively low in the postwar decades, and rose again from the early 1980s. By the late 1990s it was approaching the levels of the interwar peak, though the original article stops in 1998 and later updates change the endpoint. The fall in wage inequality in the 1940s is what Claudia Goldin and Robert Margo (1992) called the Great Compression. Piketty and Saez use that label for a broader collapse of top income shares, much of it in capital income as well as wages.

A second result is easy to miss if one looks only at the U-shape. In the early twentieth century the top of the distribution was dominated by capital income: dividends, interest and rent. The rise after about 1980 was, in their account, driven much more by wages, salaries and business income, including the pay of executives and the profits of closely held firms. The composition of "the rich" changed. The paper does not treat the tax series as a complete measure of Haig–Simons income. Capital gains are excluded from the main series, rankings use tax units rather than households, and avoidance and evasion can move reported income without moving true income. Those limits are why later work, including work with Gabriel Zucman on wealth, uses estate taxes and capital accounts alongside income tax tabulations.

## The wider methodological and intellectual impact

The article was a direct extension of the top-income method Simon Kuznets had used for the United States in *Shares of Upper Income Groups in Income and Savings* (1953), applied with a finer breakdown of the top and carried through the late twentieth century. Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (French edition 2013; English translation, Harvard University Press, 2014) uses these series, together with similar series for other countries. Anthony Atkinson, Facundo Alvaredo, Piketty, Saez and later collaborators assembled the World Top Incomes Database, subsequently broadened into the World Inequality Database, which adds wealth as well as income and should not be read as a simple reprint of the 2003 US paper.
