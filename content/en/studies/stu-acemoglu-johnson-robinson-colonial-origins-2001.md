# Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson's Colonial Origins of Comparative Development (2001)

A highly influential study in development economics and institutions, published by Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James A. Robinson in the *American Economic Review* in 2001. It sought to answer a difficult causal question: do the quality of economic and political institutions actually explain the vast differences in income between countries today? Or is the statistically observed relationship between "good institutions" and "high income" merely a correlation arising from other shared factors, such as geography or earlier development level, rather than institutions being a genuine, independent cause?

## The Methodological Challenge: The Problem of Reverse Causality

Any researcher who wants to prove that "good institutions cause economic growth" faces a fundamental methodological problem. The reverse may be true: rich countries can build better institutions because of their wealth, rather than the other way around. Alternatively, a third common factor (such as geographic location or climate) may explain both institutional quality and income level, without any direct causal relationship between them. This dilemma cannot be resolved merely by observing the statistical correlation.

## The Innovative Solution: Settler Mortality as an Instrumental Variable

The researchers devised a creative methodological solution. They used the mortality rates of early European settlers in different colonies, drawn mainly from historical records of European soldiers, bishops and sailors stationed in the colonies (notably compiled by Philip Curtin), as an "instrumental variable." This variable affects the quality of the institutions historically established, without affecting current income directly, except through this historical institutional influence.

The basic idea rests on a historical observation. In areas where European settlers faced deadly disease environments (such as malaria and yellow fever in parts of tropical Africa and Asia), Europeans could not settle permanently in large numbers. They instead established "extractive institutions" whose primary aim was to plunder and export resources, with minimal investment in an administrative or legal structure protecting long-term local property rights. In areas with a climate more favourable to European health (such as Australia, Canada and the United States), Europeans settled in large, permanent numbers. They established political and economic institutions closely resembling those of their home countries, such as reliable property rights and constraints on the executive, to protect their long-term private investments in the very place where they settled.

## The Result: Continuity of Institutions Across Centuries

The researchers found a strong statistical relationship. Colonies that historically experienced high settler mortality, and therefore established extractive institutions, still show today (after centuries of independence and many political changes) weaker economic institutions, with lower protection of property rights and higher risk of expropriation. They also have much lower per-capita income levels compared with colonies that experienced dense European settlement, even after controlling for other possible geographic and climatic factors. The researchers explained this by suggesting that early institutions, once established, tend to persist through long-term political and path-dependent mechanisms (path dependence), even after the original conditions that created them have disappeared.

## Criticisms and Continuing Methodological Debate

The study was subject to an important methodological critique, most notably that offered by David Albouy in 2012. He questioned the accuracy of some of the original historical settler mortality data used, pointing to contradictions among the historical sources, which give different mortality figures for the same colonies. This critique prompted wide debate over how well the central result survives correction of these data. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson published a reply in the same issue defending their results. The debate has not been fully resolved in favour of either side, although the original study remains one of the most cited works in development economics and institutional economics.
