# Spreading Non-Natural Concepts (Barrett & Nyhof, 2001)

An experimental study conducted by Justin Barrett with Melanie Nyhof, published in the *Journal of Cognition and Culture* in 2001. It directly tested the central hypothesis of the concept of "minimally counterintuitive concepts," formulated by Pascal Boyer: that concepts violating only one ontological expectation are retained and transmitted orally with greater accuracy than others.

## Definition

This study belongs to the field of the cognitive science of religion, which assumes that the spread of some religious beliefs across cultures and their historical persistence are not random accidents. They are the result of specific cognitive features that make some concepts easier to memorize and transmit orally than others. Examples of such concepts are supernatural agents that violate only one expectation of their ontological category, such as an invisible god who hears, sees and remembers like humans. Other concepts are more complex or more consistent with ordinary expectations.

## Method

The two researchers used the method of "serial reproduction." Short stories were constructed containing elements classified in advance into three categories: entirely ordinary (consistent with the expectations of their ontological category); minimally counterintuitive (violating one expectation); and bizarre (odd, but without violating ontological expectations). These stories were narrated to a chain of participants, each of whom passed on what they remembered to the next participant. The accuracy and proportion of elements retained were measured across each round of transmission.

## Results

Barrett and Nyhof reported that minimally counterintuitive elements were, in their materials, better retained across the serial retelling chain than ordinary or merely bizarre elements. The authors read this as support for the view, associated with Boyer, that limited violations of intuitive expectations aid memory and transmission, whereas mere oddness does not. The results were not uniform across all conditions, and readers should consult the article for the details.

## Impact

This study is regarded as one of the first direct experimental tests of the "minimally counterintuitive concepts" hypothesis. It was followed by further studies, including Boyer and Ramble (2001), which tested recall in France, Gabon and Nepal (`stu-cognitive-templates-boyer-ramble-2001`). It is frequently cited in the cognitive science of religion literature in support of the "byproduct" explanation of the spread of religious concepts, though later work has also qualified the minimal-counterintuitiveness effect.

## Limits

The original participants were university students in Western settings, and the materials came from a single cultural context, a limitation that the authors acknowledged. Cross-cultural testing came with later studies such as Boyer and Ramble (2001). Critics have also asked whether laboratory memory advantages explain the actual historical spread of religious ideas, which depends on social and institutional factors as well.
