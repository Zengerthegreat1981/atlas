# Hillbilly Elegy

*Hillbilly Elegy: A Memoir of a Family and Culture in Crisis* (2016), by J. D. Vance, is a memoir and social study of white working-class life in Appalachia and the Rust Belt. Vance tells the story of his own family — Scots-Irish migrants from the hills of eastern Kentucky who settled in Middletown, Ohio, a steel town — and uses it to examine the decline of the white working class in the United States: the loss of industrial jobs, the spread of addiction and family breakdown, and a culture that, in his account, both sustains its members and holds them back. The book became a bestseller in the year of Donald Trump's election to the presidency, and it has been read, praised, and attacked as an explanation of the anger and disaffection of white working-class voters.

Vance was born in Middletown, Ohio, in 1984, and raised largely by his grandmother, Mamaw, after his mother's struggles with addiction and a series of unstable relationships made his home life chaotic. He served in the United States Marine Corps and then attended Ohio State University and Yale Law School.

## Method

The book's method is memoir combined with social analysis. Vance draws on his own memory, on family stories, and on the sociology and economics of the white working class, citing research on social mobility, addiction, and family structure to support his account. He is explicit that he is not writing a work of academic sociology: his evidence is his family and his town, and his argument is an interpretation of that evidence rather than a systematic study. The book moves between personal narrative — scenes from his childhood, his grandmother's fierce protection and her violence, his mother's addiction, his own struggles at school and in the Marines — and short essays on the wider condition of the white working class, on subjects including the decline of manufacturing in Middletown, the opioid epidemic, and the culture of learned helplessness he believes characterises his community.

At the same time, critics have argued that a memoir of one family cannot support general claims about a whole class or region. The historian Elizabeth Catte, in *What You Are Getting Wrong About Appalachia* (2018), argues that Vance's portrait misrepresents the region and overlooks structural forces, while sociological work on the working class, such as Jennifer Silva's *Coming Up Short: Working-Class Adulthood in an Age of Uncertainty* (2013), emphasises deindustrialisation and the erosion of institutions and security rather than the cultural explanations Vance favours.

## Key Findings

The book's first finding, as Vance presents it, is that the white working class has experienced a collapse of the institutions that once organised its life. Middletown's steel mill, the Armco plant, once provided stable, well-paid work for men without college degrees; by Vance's childhood, that work was disappearing, and with it went the unions, the churches, and the expectations of steady employment that had sustained the town. The second finding concerns the family. Vance describes a world of unstable households, addiction, and domestic violence, and he argues that the breakdown of the family is both a consequence of economic decline and a cause of further decline, because children raised in chaos struggle to acquire the stability and the habits that schooling and work require.

A third finding is cultural. Vance argues that the hillbilly culture he grew up in — proud, loyal, suspicious of outsiders, and quick to take offence — also carries attitudes that make upward mobility difficult: a distrust of education, a resistance to moving for work, and a tendency to blame outside forces rather than to adapt. He recounts his own difficulty at Yale, where he did not know the unspoken rules of elite professional life, and he credits his grandmother's insistence on hard work and personal responsibility, and the discipline he learned in the Marines, with his eventual success.

## Reception and Influence

*Hillbilly Elegy* was published by HarperCollins in June 2016 and became a *New York Times* bestseller and sold widely. It was praised by reviewers for its honesty and its vivid portrait of a world rarely described from the inside, and it was widely discussed as the book that explained the 2016 election. It was also sharply criticised, above all for its cultural argument: critics argued that Vance blamed the poor for their poverty, that he ignored the structural causes of deindustrialisation, and that he presented a narrow account of Appalachia that overlooked the region's diversity and its long history of labour organising.

The book's influence on sociology has been mixed. It brought the study of the white working class to a mass audience and renewed interest in the sociology of Appalachia and the Rust Belt, alongside works such as Arlie Russell Hochschild's *Strangers in Their Own Land* (2016) and Katherine Cramer's *The Politics of Resentment* (2016). At the same time, sociologists have treated it as a memoir rather than a study, and have used it as a case for teaching the difference between personal testimony and systematic evidence. Its enduring significance lies in the debate it provoked: about whether the decline of the white working class is best explained by culture or by structure, about who gets to speak for a community, and about how the personal story of one man's escape from poverty can illuminate — and can also distort — the condition of the people he left behind.
