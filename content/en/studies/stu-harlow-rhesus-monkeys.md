# Harlow's Rhesus Monkey Attachment and Contact Comfort Studies

Famous experimental studies on infant rhesus monkeys separated from their mothers, in which they were placed before a wire "surrogate mother" that supplied milk and another covered with soft, warm cloth without milk, revealing that the infants spent most of their time with the cloth mother.

## Context and Experimental Design

Harlow isolated newborn rhesus monkeys and placed them in cages with two models of artificial surrogate mothers: a mother made of coarse iron wire with a milk bottle attached for feeding, and another mother made of wood and covered with soft, warm cotton cloth but without milk. In the 1958 experiment the feeding was counterbalanced: four infants received milk from the wire mother and four from the cloth mother. The hours the monkey spent with each mother were measured, along with its behavior when a frightening robot toy was introduced into the cage.

## Results and Conclusions

The infant monkeys spent most of each day clinging to the cloth mother, even when the wire mother was the one that fed them, and went to the wire mother only briefly to nurse. When frightened with a terrifying stimulus, the monkeys ran to and clung to the cloth mother for safety and regained their courage to explore the room, which Harlow took to show that attachment in infant primates depends on contact comfort and not merely on feeding. Extending the result to human infants is an inference by analogy.

## Criticism and Methodological Debate

Later experiments by Harlow and colleagues, in which monkeys were raised in partial or total social isolation, produced severe behavioral, social, and sexual disturbances, and isolation-reared females were often inadequate mothers. These studies, including the "vertical chamber" isolation experiments, provoked sharp ethical criticism and contributed to growing debate about the treatment of animals in research. The principle that contact comfort matters more than feeding is consistent with later primate work and with Bowlby's and Ainsworth's attachment theory, but the original design had a very small sample and used isolated infants, and such procedures would not be approved today for ethical reasons.

## Historical Context and Motive for the Experiment

Harry Harlow conducted his experiments at the University of Wisconsin-Madison at a time when the two prevailing theories in psychology, Freudian psychoanalysis (which links the child's attachment to the breast as a source of instinctual gratification) and behaviorism (which explains attachment as a mere conditioned association with the source of feeding), agreed that love and emotional warmth are secondary compared with the satisfaction of basic biological needs. Harlow designed his experiment specifically to separate the variable of "feeding" from the variable of "contact comfort" in order to test which is the true motive for attachment.

## Impact on Attachment Theory

Harlow's results coincided with John Bowlby's independent research in London on human attachment, and gave him and later Mary Ainsworth strong animal experimental support for the idea that attachment is a primary need in itself and not a secondary result of feeding. Along with studies of institutional care and hospitalization, they added to pressure for child-care practices in hospitals and orphanages that gave more attention to warm physical contact and emotional needs.

## Neurodevelopmental Consequences of Early Deprivation

Subsequent research by Harlow, Stephen Suomi, Gary Kraemer, and later developmental neurobiologists examined the enduring biological sequelae of early social and maternal deprivation in rhesus macaques. Monkeys reared in total social isolation or with surrogate mothers exhibited alterations in neuroendocrine functioning. Early social deprivation has been associated with dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, including altered cortisol responses to stressors. Later primate work also linked early deprivation to altered monoaminergic function and to stereotyped movements, heightened aggression, self-injurious behavior, and difficulty in peer social interaction. These findings are generally taken to indicate that early social contact matters for the development of systems regulating affect and stress, though the specific neurobiological mechanisms remain under study.
