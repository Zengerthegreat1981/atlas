# Schism and Continuity in an African Society

*Schism and Continuity in an African Society* (1957) is Victor Turner's first major monograph and the foundation of his processual anthropology. Based on fieldwork among the Ndembu of northwestern Zambia (then Northern Rhodesia) in the early 1950s, carried out under the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute, it studies how villages split apart — and how, through that splitting, Ndembu society holds together. Where earlier British structural-functionalism treated African societies as stable systems, Turner showed a society in perpetual motion, held in equilibrium by conflict itself.

Turner arrived among the Ndembu as a student of Max Gluckman at the University of Manchester, part of the circle that would become known as the Manchester School. The Manchester anthropologists were dissatisfied with the static models of A. R. Radcliffe-Brown: they wanted to explain change, conflict, and the choices of real people. *Schism and Continuity* is the fullest early statement of that ambition, built from the close observation of one village's life over time.

## Thesis and Structure

The book's central puzzle is a structural contradiction in Ndembu social organisation. Ndembu descent is matrilineal, so a man's heirs are his sister's children, but residence is virilocal: a wife moves to her husband's village. A village headman therefore lives surrounded by his own children, who have no rights in his lineage, while his true heirs — his nephews — live elsewhere and owe loyalty to their own maternal villages. Every village contains the seeds of its own division, and every succession is a crisis waiting to happen. Hunting values, men's ties to matrilineal kin, and competition for the headmanship add further strain. Marriages were often fragile, and people could change residence in search of advantage or safety.

Turner traces this contradiction through the history of a single village, Mukanza, and the career of Sandombu, an ambitious man who sought the headmanship. He follows quarrels over land, witchcraft accusations, adultery, and the struggle for succession as they accumulate, flare, and repeatedly threaten to split the village. Such division, when it comes, is not simply a failure of the system but part of its normal operation: fission relieves pressure, redistributes people across the landscape, and allows new villages to form that will themselves, in time, divide. Schism is the mechanism of continuity.

The book's method is the extended case study, a Manchester School hallmark. Rather than presenting customs in the abstract, Turner narrates events as they unfolded, naming individuals, quoting their arguments, and following consequences across months and years. The reader watches Sandombu manoeuvre, fail, and manoeuvre again. The analysis earns its generality from this density: the principles of Ndembu social structure emerge from the story, not in place of it. Rules do not by themselves decide outcomes. People appeal selectively to kinship or to residence, and the same conflict returns in later episodes. Genealogies and village censuses sit beside the narratives, so that named actions can be read against broader counts. Redress, in the social-drama sequence, may take the form of arbitration, a legal procedure, or ritual. Accusations of sorcery or witchcraft voiced rivalry and could be used to justify a separation.

## Social Drama and Ritual

From these village histories Turner derived his most influential concept, the social drama. A social drama unfolds in four phases: breach of a norm, mounting crisis, redressive action, and either reintegration or schism. The model began here, in the quarrels of Mukanza village, and Turner would develop it across his later work into a general theory of social process, applied by Turner to historical conflicts such as the Thomas Becket affair, and later to theatre and performance.

Ritual plays a central role in the book's account of redress. Turner's later analyses of Ndembu rituals of affliction, such as *Isoma*, performed for women suffering reproductive troubles (analysed in *The Ritual Process*), and *Ihamba* (discussed in *The Drums of Affliction*, 1968), show ritual as a way of diagnosing misfortune and, as in *Ihamba*, of treating social conflict: kin are gathered, hidden grudges are brought out, and public reconciliation is sought. Cults of affliction, performed for people troubled by ancestral spirits, drew participants from many villages and restated ties that crossed a local split. A village could divide while a wider moral community continued. Ritual is not a static expression of structure but a process in which structure is worked out, contested, and sometimes remade. This insight would lead directly to *The Ritual Process* (1969) and to Turner's concepts of liminality and communitas.

## Reception and Influence

*Schism and Continuity* came to be recognised as a major contribution, though its dense ethnography makes it demanding reading. It established Turner as the leading figure of processual anthropology and provided the empirical foundation for a generation of work on conflict, ritual, and social change. Its influence extended beyond anthropology: the social drama model was taken up in sociology, history, theatre studies, and performance studies, and Turner's later collaboration with the theatre director Richard Schechner drew on the ideas first worked out among the Ndembu.

Critics have noted the book's limits. Its focus on male headmen and village politics gives women's perspectives less space, and its village-centred frame says relatively little about the colonial state, the copper mines, and the labour migration that shaped Ndembu life in the 1950s. The account still tends to find that conflict renews a social order, and critics have asked how far that expectation steers the reading. The dramas are also the ethnographer's reconstructions: his sources and relationships shape which version of a quarrel is told. Colonial taxation and cash earning appear, but they stay at the edge of an analysis centred on village structure. Later scholarship has also questioned whether the fission model applies as neatly to other matrilineal societies. But the book's core achievement stands: it showed that social structure is not a thing societies have but a process they do, and that the anthropologist's task is to follow that process as it happens, quarrel by quarrel and ritual by ritual.
