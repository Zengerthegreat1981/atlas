# On the Edge

This entry, listed under the title "On the Edge", treats Herbert J. Gans's study of Boston's West End, published as *The Urban Villagers: Group and Class in the Life of Italian-Americans* (1962); see also the separate entry on that book. The title fits the situation of the people Gans studied: a working-class neighbourhood with a large Italian-American population, living on the edge of demolition, designated a slum by city planners and cleared in the late 1950s under the federal urban renewal programme to make way for new, higher-income housing. Gans lived in the West End from late 1957 to mid-1958, and his book is both an ethnography of the community as it was and a critique of the renewal programme that destroyed it. Its central finding was that the West End was not the disorganised slum of official description but a functioning, close-knit community whose social life was invisible to the planners who condemned it.

Gans was born in Cologne, Germany, in 1927, left Germany with his family in 1938, and reached the United States in 1940. He trained as a city planner and took his doctorate at the University of Pennsylvania in 1957. He came to the West End as a researcher with a particular interest in how people experience planning decisions, and he chose his moment deliberately, arriving while the neighbourhood was still intact but already marked for clearance. The district had been declared a redevelopment area in the 1950s, clearance began in 1958, and the neighbourhood was largely razed by about 1960, its residents scattered across Greater Boston.

## Method

Gans's method was participant observation of an immersive kind. He and his wife lived in an apartment in the West End as ordinary neighbours, attending weddings, funerals and informal gatherings, joining local organisations, and conducting interviews. He paid particular attention to the peer groups — the groups of young men, the women's networks, the family gatherings — through which West End life was organised, and he distinguished between the behaviour of the neighbourhood's residents and the physical condition of its buildings, a distinction the planners had failed to make. He later described the limits of his role as an outsider studying a community that knew it was about to be destroyed.

## Key Findings

The first finding was that the West End possessed a dense and functioning social system. Families were large and close, neighbours knew one another well, and the street, the church and the corner store provided a web of support that residents valued highly. Gans argued that the problems of the area — low incomes, poor housing and some crime — did not stem from the neighbourhood's social life, as the planners claimed, but from poverty and from residents' position in the wider society. The second finding was that the label "slum" was an administrative and political judgement rather than a description of social life: planners equated physical deterioration with social pathology and did not seek residents' views of their neighbourhood.

A third finding concerned the human cost of renewal. The dispersal of the West Enders broke up social networks on which they depended, and the psychologist Marc Fried, studying relocated residents, found that many experienced grief comparable to bereavement for a lost home. Gans's book thus became an influential statement of a principle that shaped urban sociology: that a neighbourhood is a social system, not a collection of buildings. A fourth finding was about class. Gans showed that the West Enders were working-class people whose culture was coherent on its own terms, and he rejected the middle-class assumption, common among reformers, that their way of life was a problem to be solved by moving them into better housing.

## Reception and Influence

*The Urban Villagers* became a landmark of urban sociology and a classic statement against indiscriminate urban renewal. It belongs with Jane Jacobs's *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961) in the critique of large-scale clearance programmes in the 1960s, and it helped shift planning practice toward rehabilitation and community participation, a shift Gans himself advocated in his paper "Planning for People, Not Buildings" (1969).

Gans went on to teach sociology and planning at Columbia University. His later books included *The Levittowners: Ways of Life and Politics in a New Suburban Community* (1967), based on his residence in the new suburb of Levittown, New Jersey, and *People, Plans, and Policies* (1991), a collection of essays on planning, poverty and urban life. *The Urban Villagers* is still assigned in courses on urban sociology and community studies, and its central insight — that outsiders cannot judge a community by its buildings — continues to inform debates about gentrification, displacement and the redevelopment of poor neighbourhoods.
