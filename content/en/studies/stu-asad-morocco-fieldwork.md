# Asad's Moroccan Fieldwork and Reflexive Ethnography: Power and Representation in Anthropology

Talal Asad's critical reflections on his own fieldwork among the Kababish Bedouin of Sudan and his subsequent theoretical work arguing that anthropology must grapple with its implication in colonial power relations and with how power shapes anthropological representation. Although Asad's fieldwork was conducted in Sudan rather than Morocco, his methodological and epistemological reflections on ethnography (particularly in works published from the 1970s onward) advocated for what became called reflexive anthropology: an approach that explicitly acknowledges how power relations between anthropologists and research subjects shape ethnographic knowledge production. His work contributed to establishing anthropological critique of anthropology itself.

## Publication Context

Asad's major theoretical works were published from the 1970s onward, particularly *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973), in which he collected essays reflecting on anthropology's relationship to colonialism. Asad, a Palestinian-American anthropologist educated at Oxford, had conducted fieldwork among the Kababish Bedouin (not in Morocco, though the scholarly reference points toward Middle Eastern ethnography more broadly), and used his fieldwork experience to develop broader critiques of anthropological practice. His work appeared in a context of growing postcolonial critique of anthropology, when younger anthropologists were challenging the discipline's establishment figures and questioning anthropology's role in colonialism. Asad's intellectual intervention was to argue that anthropology was fundamentally structured by colonial power relations—that anthropologists had gained access to research communities through colonial channels, that anthropological knowledge was shaped by colonial interests, and that even well-intentioned anthropologists could not escape implication in colonial structures. His work contributed to establishing a critical tradition within anthropology that examines the discipline's own assumptions, methods, and politics. Subsequent theorists developed and extended Asad's critiques.

## Research Question & Approach

Asad's central research questions were philosophical and epistemological: How does anthropology produce knowledge about other peoples? What are the power relationships between anthropologists and research subjects? How does colonialism shape anthropological knowledge production? How should anthropologists acknowledge and respond to these power dynamics? Rather than conducting new fieldwork to answer these questions, Asad reflected critically on existing anthropological practices and on his own fieldwork experience. His methodological approach involved careful reading of anthropological texts (examining the assumptions embedded in them), historical analysis of how anthropology developed in relation to colonialism, and reflection on his own positionality as an anthropologist. He argued that anthropologists needed to become more conscious of how their own position, assumptions, and the colonial context shaped what they could know and how they represented knowledge. He called for anthropologists to examine critically how they had positioned themselves relative to research subjects, how they had defined their research questions, and how power relations had structured their understanding.

## Ethnographic Setting

While Asad's fieldwork was conducted among the Kababish Bedouin of Sudan (not Morocco, though his theoretical work engaged with Middle Eastern anthropology broadly), his intellectual project was to examine how fieldwork was conducted across diverse sites. The Kababish inhabited semi-arid pastoral regions and practiced camel herding, organized through tribal structures. Asad's fieldwork was conducted in the 1960s-1970s, during Sudan's post-colonial period, when Sudan was dealing with political instability and when anthropologists' access to research sites was mediated through Sudanese government institutions. Asad's reflection on his own fieldwork highlighted how colonial and post-colonial structures had shaped his access: he had arrived with letters from British academic institutions, he had worked within frameworks established by colonial anthropology, and he had necessarily maintained certain distance from Kababish political organizing. His reflections on these structural constraints became the basis for his broader critiques of anthropology.

## Key Findings

Asad's critical analysis revealed several important points. First, he argued that anthropology had historically been implicated in colonialism: anthropological knowledge about colonized peoples had been produced through colonial channels and had sometimes served colonial administrative purposes. Even anthropologists who were critical of colonialism could not escape complete implication in these structures. Second, he argued that anthropological knowledge production was structured by power relations: who anthropologists had access to, what questions they could ask, what information would be shared—all were shaped by the power relationships between anthropologists (positioned as educated Westerners with institutional backing) and research subjects (positioned as colonized or post-colonized people). Third, Asad argued that anthropological representation involved power: how anthropologists represented other peoples, in what terms, to what audiences, all involved representation that positioned anthropologists as experts and interpreters and positioned research subjects as the objects of interpretation. Asad's work did not condemn anthropologists as individuals but argued that the discipline itself, as constituted, had structural problems related to power and representation. His solution was not to abandon ethnography but to encourage anthropologists to become more conscious of these power relations, to examine their own assumptions and positions, and to acknowledge the partial and situated nature of anthropological knowledge.

## Ethnographic Examples

Asad provides limited specific ethnographic examples but instead works through conceptual analysis. However, his reflections on his own fieldwork illustrate his arguments: he discusses how his status as a Western-educated intellectual affected his access to Kababish communities, how power differences shaped conversations and observations, and how his own interests and assumptions had constrained what he could understand. He uses his own experience to illustrate broader points about anthropological practice. Asad also analyzes existing ethnographic texts, showing how power assumptions are embedded in ethnographic writing—for instance, how ethnographic descriptions of "traditional" practices can reinforce assumptions about the unchanging nature of colonized peoples, or how ethnographic authority positions anthropologists as interpreters speaking for their research subjects.

## Theoretical Contribution

Asad's theoretical contribution was to establish reflexivity and critical awareness of power as central to anthropological practice. He argued that anthropologists must grapple with how power shapes their work, must acknowledge their own positions and assumptions, and must recognize the partiality of anthropological knowledge. His work contributed to developing postcolonial critique of anthropology and influenced subsequent scholarship examining anthropology's relationship to colonialism and to ongoing power inequalities. His argument that anthropological representation is an exercise of power influenced theories of representation and contributed to the development of what became called "writing culture" studies—critical examination of how ethnography is written and what authority claims are embedded in ethnographic texts. His work also contributed to establishing that anthropological reflexivity should extend beyond individual anthropologists' self-awareness to systemic examination of how the discipline is structured by power relations.

## Methodological Legacy

Asad pioneered methods and practices of reflexive anthropology, arguing that anthropologists should explicitly examine and discuss their fieldwork methods, their access and relationships with research subjects, and the constraints and possibilities their positions created. His practice of critically reading anthropological texts as documents revealing disciplinary assumptions influenced subsequent textual and discourse analysis approaches in anthropology. His integration of historical analysis of the discipline with ethnographic and theoretical work showed how understanding anthropology required understanding its history and politics. His encouragement of anthropologists to articulate their own positions explicitly and to discuss power dynamics in fieldwork influenced the practice of increasingly reflexive ethnographic writing.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has built on Asad's reflexive approach while raising important questions. Some scholars have argued that excessive reflexivity can become self-indulgent, focusing on the anthropologist rather than on research subjects and their concerns. Others have noted that reflexivity alone, without commitment to addressing the political implications of anthropological work, may be insufficient. Postcolonial anthropologists have extended Asad's critique in various directions, developing more explicit political commitments and arguing for anthropology in service to indigenous communities and social justice. Despite these extensions and critiques, Asad's foundational insight—that anthropology is structured by power relations and that anthropologists must grapple with these dynamics—remains central to contemporary anthropological practice. For contemporary students, Asad's work exemplifies how anthropologists can critique their own discipline and how reflexivity about power is essential to ethical ethnographic practice. His work has contributed to establishing that anthropology should be practiced with awareness of its political implications and with commitment to representing research subjects respectfully and fairly.
