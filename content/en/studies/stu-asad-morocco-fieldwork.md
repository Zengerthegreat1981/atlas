# Asad's Kababish Fieldwork and Reflexive Anthropology: Power and Representation

Talal Asad's ethnographic fieldwork was conducted among the Kababish, camel-herding Arab nomads of northern Kordofan in Sudan, and was written up as *The Kababish Arabs: Power, Authority and Consent in a Nomadic Tribe* (1970). Asad later became one of the most influential critics of the discipline's relation to colonial power, most famously as editor of *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973). This entry treats the fieldwork and the critical, reflexive thinking that grew out of it.

## Background

Asad was born in 1932 in Medina (in present-day Saudi Arabia) and trained in British social anthropology; he later taught in Britain and the United States. He did his fieldwork among the Kababish in the early 1960s, in the first years of Sudanese independence, when access to rural communities was still mediated by state and administrative structures that were a legacy of colonial rule.

## The Kababish Study

The Kababish are pastoral nomads whose political organization centred on a paramount shaykh. The monograph's central problem is the nature of political authority in a tribal society: how a chief's power is related to the consent of his followers, and how far it rested on his position within the wider (colonial and then national) administrative system. Asad's analysis suggested that the paramount shaykh's authority depended not only on consent within the tribe but also on external support and on the shaykh's ability to control resources, so that the neat contrast between "consensual" tribal leadership and "coercive" state power did not hold. The book is a careful work in the British structural-functionalist tradition that already shows the author's interest in questions of power.

## From Fieldwork to Critique

In the 1973 volume, Asad and his contributors argued that modern social anthropology developed within, and was shaped by, the unequal power relations of colonial rule: anthropologists generally worked in colonized societies, relied on colonial order for access, and produced knowledge that, whatever their own intentions, could be used in administration. Asad did not claim that anthropologists were simple agents of empire; the argument was that the discipline's objects, concepts and access were structured by a political context it rarely examined. His essay on cultural translation (1986) further argued that ethnographic "translation" is conducted within asymmetrical relations between languages and societies, so that the ethnographer's representation is never politically neutral.

## Later Work

Asad's later writings turned to the anthropology of religion and Islam. In *The Idea of an Anthropology of Islam* (1986) he proposed treating Islam as a discursive tradition, and in *Genealogies of Religion* (1993) he criticized the assumption that "religion" is a universal, essentially private category, tracing how it was shaped in the Christian West. These works carry forward the earlier insistence that analytical categories have histories tied to power.

## Significance and Critique

Asad's critique became a founding reference for postcolonial and reflexive anthropology and is often read alongside Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) and the "writing culture" debates. Critics have noted that reflexivity can slide into self-absorption, and that some accounts of the "colonial encounter" overstated the uniformity of anthropologists' relations to colonial administrations; nonetheless, the demand that anthropologists examine the political conditions of their own knowledge remains standard in the field.
