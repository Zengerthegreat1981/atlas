# Dam Construction & Displacement

## Subject

Development anthropology studies the effect of building **large dams** on the local communities who are forced to vacate their lands submerged by reservoirs. This is a prominent example of what is known as **"development-induced displacement and resettlement"** (DIDR): millions of people around the world have been forcibly uprooted from their land in the name of major "development" projects, often without adequate compensation or genuine participation in the decision.

## Scale and Historical Context

The **World Commission on Dams** estimated that between 40 and 80 million people around the world were displaced by dam construction alone during the twentieth century. This makes the phenomenon one of the largest forms of forced displacement not caused by war or natural disaster in modern history. Among the most prominent cases studied anthropologically are the Three Gorges Dam in China (which displaced more than a million people), the Aswan High Dam in Egypt, and numerous dams in India, sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America.

## The Cernea Model of Impoverishment Risks

Michael M. Cernea, a sociologist and anthropologist who served as senior adviser for social policy at the World Bank, developed the **Impoverishment Risks and Reconstruction (IRR) model**, which identifies eight interrelated risks faced by forcibly displaced people: landlessness, joblessness, homelessness, marginalization, increased morbidity and mortality, food insecurity, loss of access to common resources (such as pastures and forests), and the disintegration of community social fabric. Cernea argues that forced displacement is not merely a temporary disruption but a process that, if not managed carefully, leads to long-term impoverishment and lasting economic and social weakness among the displaced.

## Anthropological Critique

Many anthropologists, including some who worked directly within institutions such as the World Bank, criticized official resettlement policies for often reducing the complexity of the social and cultural loss suffered by the displaced community (kinship ties, local knowledge of the land, sacred or heritage sites) to a purely financial or property compensation that does not reflect the actual value of what was lost. Research on resettlement is also hampered where the subject is politically sensitive, since researchers may find it hard to gain free access to the affected communities.

## Impact

Cernea's research and that of his colleagues contributed to reforming compensation and resettlement policies at major international financing institutions (the World Bank and regional development banks). They subsequently required internationally funded projects to draw up more comprehensive "resettlement plans" that account for social rather than purely economic dimensions.

## Limitations

Despite these policy reforms, later studies show that the gap between announced policies and actual implementation on the ground remains wide in many contemporary dam projects, especially in contexts of weak institutional oversight and limited local political accountability.
