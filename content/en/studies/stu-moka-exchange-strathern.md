# Moka Exchange Among Mount Hagen Peoples: Strathern on Prestige Competition

Andrew Strathern's *The Rope of Moka: Big-Men and Ceremonial Exchange in Mount Hagen, New Guinea* (Cambridge University Press, 1971) is a classic study of ceremonial exchange in the Western Highlands of Papua New Guinea. It describes the moka, a system in which men give pigs and shell valuables to exchange partners in return for larger gifts later, and shows how such exchange is linked to the creation of leadership ("big-man" status) and to political relations among clans.

## Context

Strathern, trained at Cambridge, carried out fieldwork in the Mount Hagen area beginning in 1964-65, working with his wife and colleague Marilyn Strathern, and returned several times. His main field community was the Melpa-speaking Kawelka tribe. The Hagen area had been reached by Europeans only in the 1930s; Papua New Guinea was an Australian-administered territory until independence in 1975, and during the 1960s cash cropping (notably coffee) was expanding.

## What the Moka Is

Moka is a form of competitive, delayed gift exchange between partners, usually men of different clans, in which valuables (pigs and, historically, pearl shells and later cash and other items) are given with the expectation that a larger return gift will be made later. The increment gives the giver prestige and obliges the receiver; failure to repay damages a man's name. Exchanges are arranged in chains of partners (the "rope" of the title) which link individuals and groups. Women contribute essential labour in raising pigs and growing sweet potatoes, but the public transactions are conducted mainly by men.

## Findings

- **Leadership.** Men who can mobilize support from kin and followers, accumulate wealth and make large moka gifts become "big-men." Status is achieved rather than inherited, and is vulnerable to rivals. Strathern's case is often set against the hereditary rank of Polynesian chiefdoms, in line with Marshall Sahlins's well-known typology of Melanesian big-men and Polynesian chiefs (1963).
- **Competition and alliance.** Moka serves both as rivalry and as a means of creating alliances among groups; exchanges between clans could take the place of earlier warfare, and big-men negotiate to raise the contributions of their supporters.
- **Political economy.** Moka is a system for converting resources (labour, pigs, shells) into prestige and political influence, involving calculation about timing and partners, but also moral obligation.
- **Adaptation to change.** The system was adapted to new forms of wealth, and cash could be used to buy pigs and valuables, so that moka continued to be important well after colonial control and was not simply a "traditional" relic.

## Reception and Critique

The book became a standard source on Melanesian exchange and on the big-man model. Critics have noted that a focus on men's transactional rivalry may underplay women's roles and the moral and cosmological meanings of exchange; Marilyn Strathern's work (for example *Women in Between*, 1972, and later *The Gender of the Gift*, 1988) addressed those themes. Others have argued that the big-man model overgeneralizes from the Highlands and that colonial pacification and cash cropping changed the scale and form of moka, so that the system documented in the 1960s was not simply "traditional." Later Strathern studies, such as *A Line of Power* (1984), extended the analysis to the long-term history of Hagen politics.
