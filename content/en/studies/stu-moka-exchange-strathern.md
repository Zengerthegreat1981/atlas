# Moka Exchange Among Mount Hagen Peoples: Strathern on Prestige Competition

Andrew Strathern's ethnographic monograph documenting the Moka exchange system among the Kawelka and related peoples of Mount Hagen, Papua New Guinea, published in 1971. The Moka ring represents one of anthropology's best-documented prestige economies, in which participants accumulate and redistribute live pigs and indigenous shell valuables in competitive exchanges aimed at establishing and maintaining status hierarchies. Strathern's analysis provided sophisticated understanding of how prestige economies operated as mechanisms for wealth conversion, political domination, and alliance formation in societies without state institutions or market economies.

## Publication Context

*The Rope of Moka* was published in 1971 by Cambridge University Press, synthesizing Strathern's fieldwork in Mount Hagen conducted from 1964-1968. Strathern, trained at Oxford under Max Gluckman, had become fascinated with the Highland New Guinea political economies and their mechanisms of status competition and prestige accumulation. The monograph appeared at a time when anthropology was increasingly analyzing prestige economies and gift exchange systems, building on Malinowski's foundational work on the Kula and on subsequent theorizing by scholars like Mary Douglas and Marshall Sahlins. Strathern's work contributed sophisticated ethnographic documentation of how the Moka system actually operated—the mechanics of accumulation, the timing of exchanges, the strategies participants employed—rather than merely theoretical speculation. The book became influential in economic anthropology and in the study of Melanesian societies more broadly, and subsequent scholarship by Strathern (including his landmark work with his wife Marilyn on Mount Hagen societies) made him one of the twentieth century's most important Melanesian ethnographers. The monograph demonstrated how detailed ethnographic study of a single exchange system could generate insights into broader questions about wealth, status, political authority, and alliance in stateless societies.

## Research Question & Approach

Strathern's central research question was how prestige economies operated as mechanisms for creating and maintaining status hierarchies and political power in societies without formal political institutions. Specifically, how did the Moka system function? How did men accumulate pigs and shells, and what motivated their redistribution? How did Moka participation relate to political authority and alliance formation? His methodological approach combined extended fieldwork residence, participant observation of Moka preparations and exchanges, genealogical documentation of pigs as carefully tracked as human genealogies, and detailed interviews with participants about their strategic motivations. Strathern participated in daily economic activities, witnessed pig herding and maintenance, and observed the elaborate negotiations preceding major Moka exchanges. He documented the complete cycle of an exchange from initiation through preparation through the public distribution event, showing how each phase had particular social significance and how participants calculated advantages and risks. Crucially, Strathern attended to the Moka cycle's temporal dimension—exchanges typically occurred at multi-year intervals, requiring sustained accumulation and careful planning. His approach revealed that Moka participation involved long-term strategic calculation: a man had to maintain relationships with exchange partners, manage his herd, and time his distributions strategically to maximize prestige and establish or maintain political authority.

## Ethnographic Setting

Mount Hagen is a high-altitude (approximately 5,000-7,000 feet) region in the central highlands of Papua New Guinea, characterized by steep valleys, limited level ground, and subtropical climate. The Kawelka and neighboring peoples practiced mixed subsistence involving pig herding (pigs were particularly labor-intensive, requiring daily feeding and care) and cultivation of taro, sweet potato, and yams. Population density was relatively high (for the region), with villages of 100-300 persons typically organized into patrilineal clans and sub-clans. Strathern's fieldwork occurred during the 1960s, in the early post-colonial period; Papua New Guinea had only recently achieved independence, and Highland societies were experiencing rapid introduction of cash crops and wage labor, yet Moka exchanges continued to be central to political life. Strathern conducted fieldwork in the early period of contact with Australian colonial administration, when Australian colonial officials were actively attempting to "pacify" Highland societies and promote "development" (understood as market integration and cash cropping). Despite colonial pressure, Moka exchanges not only persisted but actually intensified during this period, as some men accumulated wealth from cash crops and converted that wealth into pigs and into enhanced Moka status. Strathern's access was facilitated by his established presence in the region and his previous work there; he lived in close quarters with community members and participated in daily life, developing relationships that permitted him to understand Moka motivations in their lived complexity.

## Key Findings

Strathern documented that the Moka system operated as a prestige economy in which pigs and indigenous shell valuables circulated in exchanges aimed at establishing status. The system's mechanics were complex: a man would accumulate pigs over years, sometimes acquiring pigs through purchase, theft, or capture in warfare, but more typically through steady herding and strategic exchanges. When he had accumulated sufficient pigs and shells (quantities varied, but major exchanges might involve 50+ pigs plus thousands of shells), he would arrange a Moka exchange—a public event in which he distributed his accumulated wealth to exchange partners and rivals. The distribution was not equal: he would give more to some partners than others, depending on political strategy and relationships. Recipients understood themselves as achieving enhanced status through their capacity to eventually reciprocate, though reciprocation might occur years later. Strathern discovered that Moka participation was linked directly to political authority: men who organized major Mokas were recognized as "big-men" with enhanced influence in community affairs. Leadership was achieved rather than inherited; a man's status depended on his demonstrated capacity to accumulate and redistribute wealth. Moka exchanges were not merely ceremonial; they involved real economic processes and wealth transfers that created political hierarchies and competitive relationships. The system also functioned to manage inter-group relations: exchanges between groups created alliances, and disputes between groups sometimes involved competitive Moka exchanges in which the group that displayed greater wealth established dominance. Strathern also found that the system was dynamic and responsive to external economic pressures; as cash crops were introduced, some men used cash wealth to purchase pigs and shells, enabling them to participate in and even intensify Moka exchanges. This suggested that prestige economies were not static traditional systems but flexible institutions that participants modified strategically.

## Ethnographic Examples

Strathern provides detailed documentation of specific Moka exchanges he observed. He describes preparations for a major Moka involving one prominent man's accumulation of pigs over a four-year period. The man had inherited some pigs, acquired others through trade and purchase, and bred his herds carefully. As the exchange approached, he negotiated with his wife (women's labor was essential for pig maintenance) and his kinsmen about the final numbers and strategic distribution. The exchange itself was a public event; the pigs were assembled, decorated, and distributed in a carefully orchestrated sequence. Strathern records the reactions of recipients: some accepted their allocations with satisfaction, others expressed disappointment if they received fewer pigs than expected or than they understood themselves to warrant. Strathern also documents his own role as an observer and the ways his presence complicated dynamics; at one point he was actually given pigs by exchange participants as a recognition of his presence and role, an unusual role for an anthropologist. He records conversations with participants about their motivations: men discussed their future plans for reciprocation, expressed concern about managing relationships with particular partners, and justified their distribution decisions by reference to political and kinship considerations. Strathern also documents a competitive Moka exchange between clans, in which rival big-men attempted to demonstrate superior status through the scale of their distributions. One big-man gave significantly more pigs than his rival, achieving a notable victory. Strathern notes how this exchange was discussed afterward, with observers assessing whether the loser could adequately reciprocate in future exchanges or whether his political dominance had been permanently shifted.

## Theoretical Contribution

Strathern's work contributed sophisticated economic anthropological understanding of prestige economies. His demonstration that Moka operated as a system of wealth conversion (converting consumable resources like agricultural surplus into pigs, then converting pigs into prestige and political authority) showed that prestige economies involved real economic processes and transformations, not merely ceremonial redistribution of surplus. His analysis of how Moka participation created and maintained status hierarchies and political authority demonstrated how leadership was achieved in stateless societies: through demonstrated capacity to mobilize resources and redistribute them in ways that established dominance. His documentation of the system's flexibility and responsiveness to external economic changes (the way participants incorporated cash crops into traditional exchange systems) challenged static anthropological conceptualizations of tradition and suggested that prestige economies were not museum pieces preserved unchanged but living institutions that participants modified to serve ongoing purposes. His analysis also contributed to understanding how wealth, status, and political authority were constituted in societies without hereditary rank: status had to be continuously achieved and asserted through exchanges, creating a more fluid and contentious political field than hierarchical inheritance systems. Strathern's work also influenced theorizing about gender and economics: while his analysis centered on male big-men, his documentation of women's essential roles in pig husbandry and his later collaboration with his wife on gender dimensions of Mount Hagen life contributed to understanding that gender was integral to economic and political organization.

## Methodological Legacy

Strathern pioneered methodological approaches to studying prestige economies that became influential in economic anthropology. His systematic documentation of pig genealogies—tracking pigs' origins, ownership changes, breeding, and exchange—as carefully as human genealogies became a model for understanding how prestige goods were valued and tracked. His method of following a complete Moka cycle from initiation through culmination revealed how exchange systems operated temporally and how different phases had distinct social significance. His detailed recording of participant motivations and strategic calculations revealed that prestige economies involved rational strategic action despite operating through gift exchange rather than market mechanisms. His attention to how external economic pressures and opportunities were incorporated into traditional exchange systems pioneered what would later become political economy approaches in anthropology. Strathern's work also influenced the integration of economic anthropology with political and social analysis, showing that economic systems had political consequences and that understanding political organization required understanding economic practices. His practice of detailed observation of actual exchanges rather than merely interviewing about exchanges demonstrated how anthropologists could witness and document economic processes as they unfolded, generating different insights than retrospective accounts alone.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has identified important critiques and extensions of Strathern's analysis. Feminist scholars have noted that while Strathern's analysis centers on male big-men, women's roles in pig husbandry, in productive labor, and in the social relationships that enabled Moka exchanges warrant greater analytical focus. Strathern himself (in collaboration with Marilyn Strathern) subsequently produced important work on gender dimensions of Mount Hagen societies that addressed this limitation. Postcolonial scholars have questioned whether Strathern's analysis adequately contextualized Moka exchanges within colonialism and whether the intensification of Mokas during the colonial period (which Strathern documents) represented genuine continuity with pre-colonial practices or reflected colonial-era transformations. Some scholars have argued that focusing on prestige economies risks romanticizing non-capitalist systems and obscuring the ways capitalism and colonial capitalism were reshaping these societies. Additionally, some Melanesianists have noted that Strathern's analysis, while sophisticated, represents a particular moment in Mount Hagen social organization and that subsequent decades saw further significant changes as cash cropping expanded and wage labor became more important. Despite these critiques, Strathern's monograph remains enormously valuable as a detailed ethnographic documentation of a prestige economy and as a sophisticated analysis of how status hierarchies are created and maintained in stateless societies. The work exemplifies how detailed study of exchange systems generates understanding of political organization, authority, and wealth. For contemporary anthropologists, the text remains valuable as a model of ethnographic analysis of economic systems and as a record of a historically significant exchange system.
