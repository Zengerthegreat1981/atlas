# Tally's Corner: A Study of Negro Streetcorner Men

An ethnographic study by the anthropologist Elliot Liebow, based on fieldwork carried out in 1962–1963 among low-income Black men who gathered at a carry-out shop on a street corner in a poor neighbourhood of Washington, D.C. It was published in 1967 and offered an ethnographic alternative to "culture of poverty" explanations of urban poverty.

## Method and Content

Liebow carried out the research as part of a broader study of low-income family life in Washington directed by Hylan Lewis. He spent many months in daily contact with a group of men, one of whom he named "Tally Jackson", documenting how they coped with unstable, low-paid, often menial work, unemployment, and strained relationships with women and children. The book's central argument is that behaviour that outsiders read as a rejection of mainstream values, such as unreliable work and failed marriages, was not the product of a separate, inherited value system. The men largely shared the values of the wider society, but they could not live up to them given the jobs and incomes available to them. Liebow described what he called a "shadow system of values" through which the men protected their self-esteem from the experience of repeated failure. Behaviour, he argued, was a response to present circumstances and would change if opportunities changed.

## Context

The study entered the American policy debate about poverty among Black Americans in the 1960s. Its fieldwork preceded the 1965 Moynihan Report, *The Negro Family: The Case for National Action*, which linked family structure to persistent poverty, but the book appeared in 1967 amid the controversy that report provoked and is often read as a counterweight to cultural explanations. It also stood against the "culture of poverty" idea associated with the anthropologist Oscar Lewis, whose *La Vida* appeared in 1966.

## Impact and Critique

*Tally's Corner* became one of the most widely taught ethnographies in courses on urban sociology and American poverty. Critics have observed that the study concerns a small group of men (a few dozen at most, with a core of about twenty) in a single neighbourhood and a particular moment, so that its conclusions cannot be statistically generalised to the wider Black poor, even though its insights into the relation between values and constraints have been influential. The book also reflects the language and gender perspective of its time: the men's world is described largely from the men's own point of view.
