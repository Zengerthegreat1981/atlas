# The Ritual Process: Turner on Ndembu Ritual and Social Conflict

Victor Turner's *The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure* (Aldine, 1969), based on his Lewis Henry Morgan lectures of 1966, draws on his earlier fieldwork among the Ndembu of north-western Zambia (then Northern Rhodesia) and on comparative material to develop a processual theory of ritual. Its best-known concepts are **liminality**, **communitas** and **structure/anti-structure**.

## Context

Turner (1920-1983) was trained at Manchester under Max Gluckman and worked for the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute. He lived among the Ndembu in two periods between 1950 and 1954, and published his early monographs *Schism and Continuity in an African Society* (1957) and *Chihamba, the White Spirit* (1962), followed by *The Forest of Symbols* (1967) and *The Drums of Affliction* (1968). The Ndembu are matrilineal, but residence after marriage is typically in the husband's village, which creates tensions between lineage and village loyalties; in *Schism and Continuity* he analyzed these in terms of "social dramas" (breach, crisis, redressive action, reintegration or schism), and he saw ritual as part of redressive action.

## Ndembu Ritual

Turner studied Ndembu rituals of life-crisis (such as the girls' puberty ceremony *Nkang'a*), rituals of affliction (such as *Isoma* and *Ihamba*, in which misfortune is attributed to ancestral spirits or the intrusion of an ancestor's tooth, and a doctor and the community work through grievances), and hunters' cults. His analysis of symbols distinguishes their *exegetical* meaning (what informants say), their *operational* meaning (how they are used) and their *positional* meaning (relations among symbols). In *Nkang'a*, the milk tree (*mudyi*) is a dominant symbol, standing for breast milk, motherhood and the unity of the matrilineage and, in another register, for the bond of women. Symbols can condense opposed meanings, such as social norms and bodily or emotional experience.

## Liminality and Communitas

Turner built on Arnold van Gennep's *Rites of Passage* (1909), which divided rites into separation, transition (limen, "threshold") and incorporation. Turner focused on the middle phase, in which the ritual subjects ("liminars" or "threshold people") are "betwixt and between" the positions assigned by law and custom: they are stripped of status markers, humbled and subject to authority, and often treated as a group of equals. In *The Ritual Process* he contrasted two "models" of human interrelatedness: **structure**, the differentiated and hierarchical system of social positions, and **communitas**, an unstructured and relatively undifferentiated community of equals, experienced in liminal conditions. He applied these concepts beyond the Ndembu to rites of status elevation, the position of "structurally inferior" groups, millenarian and religious movements, and figures such as the Franciscan friars and to the hippies of the 1960s counterculture. Turner argued that societies need both, and that liminality can generate creativity and critique.

## Reception and Critique

The concepts influenced religious studies, performance studies, literary theory and cultural studies, and "liminality" is widely used in studies of migration, pilgrimage and social transition. Critics have argued that Turner's account of communitas sometimes romanticized spontaneity and underestimated the political uses of ritual and of hierarchy within it; that the model works better for some rites than others and cannot be extended to all ritual; that his early work focused on Ndembu social conflict in a colonial setting that he did not fully treat; and that his later writing generalized beyond his ethnographic base. Some feminists noted that the model's treatment of gender and the liminal condition is limited. The book remains a core text in the anthropology of ritual.
