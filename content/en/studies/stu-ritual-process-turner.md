# The Ritual Process: Turner on Ndembu Ritual and Social Conflict

Victor Turner's groundbreaking theoretical work *The Ritual Process* (1967) synthesized ethnographic research among the Ndembu of Zambia conducted from 1950-1956. The monograph moved beyond structural analysis of ritual to develop a processual approach that examined how rituals generated transformation, resolved social conflicts, and created communitas (a sense of unbounded community transcending status distinctions). The work introduced concepts—liminality, anti-structure, communitas—that became foundational to the anthropology of ritual and to performance studies more broadly.

## Publication Context

*The Ritual Process* was published in 1967 by Aldine Publishing Company as the capstone to Turner's ethnographic work among the Ndembu, a Zambian people (then Northern Rhodesia, under British colonial administration) practicing swidden agriculture and hunting. Turner, trained at Manchester University under Max Gluckman and influenced by Gluckman's processual and dramatic approaches to social analysis, had conducted intensive fieldwork among the Ndembu from 1950-1956, studying their ritual practices, village organization, and the relationship between ritual and social conflict. His earlier monographs—*Schism and Continuity in an African Society* (1957) and *The Drums of Affliction* (1968)—provided detailed ethnographic documentation of Ndembu rituals and social organization. *The Ritual Process*, however, moved beyond ethnographic description to develop broader theoretical arguments about how rituals functioned in all human societies, arguing that rituals operated as mechanisms for resolving social contradictions and generating social transformation. The book emerged in the context of rising interest in symbolic anthropology and structural analysis, but Turner also pioneered what would become called performance anthropology by attending to ritual as a dramatic, processual event rather than as a static structural form. The work became profoundly influential, not only in anthropology but in religious studies, psychology, literature, and performance theory, contributing concepts that entered broader intellectual discourse.

## Research Question & Approach

Turner's central research question was deceptively complex: What do rituals do? How do rituals relate to social conflicts and social structures? Rather than analyzing ritual as expressing stable social meanings or reinforcing existing structures (the standard functionalist approach), Turner asked how rituals actually transformed social situations and participants. He sought to understand the temporal process of ritual—how rituals moved through phases (separation from ordinary time, a liminal period, and reincorporation into social structure) and how each phase generated specific effects. His methodological approach combined participant observation of actual rituals, detailed analysis of ritual symbolism (what ritual objects meant to the Ndembu), and attention to the social context of ritual events. Turner would observe a ritual, record what he saw, then interview participants afterward about what the ritual meant, why particular objects were used, and how the ritual related to broader social concerns. He systematized ritual analysis by examining repeated rituals (girls' puberty rituals, healing rituals, divination rituals) and comparing their structures, revealing underlying patterns across diverse ritual types. Crucially, Turner attended to the temporal unfolding of ritual—the duration, the sequence of events, the participation of different social actors, and the ways ritual events integrated participants into a community that transcended their everyday hierarchical status. His approach pioneered what became performance analysis: ritual understood as dramatic action unfolding through time rather than as a static structure to be decoded.

## Ethnographic Setting

The Ndembu occupied the Zambian savanna woodland region (then in Northern Rhodesia under British colonial administration), numbering approximately 6,000-8,000 persons distributed across numerous villages organized by matrilineal kinship. They practiced shifting cultivation supplemented by hunting, with villages typically containing 30-100 persons composed of a matrilineal core of related women and their husbands. Ndembu society was characterized by internal tensions: kinship rules traced descent matrilineally (property and status passed through the mother's line), but marriages were virilocal (women moved to their husbands' villages), creating conflicts between a man's interest in his sister's children (his matrilineal heirs) and his wife's children (whom he raised but who would inherit from their matrilineal uncles). Turner found that rituals were deeply engaged with these structural contradictions, serving as mechanisms for temporarily managing social conflicts. His fieldwork occurred during the 1950s, in the late colonial period; Northern Rhodesia was approaching independence, and Ndembu society was experiencing rapid change as Christianity was spreading, wage labor was expanding, and colonial rule was reshaping political authority. Turner witnessed village conflicts, fissions, and the ways rituals were being reformulated in response to these changes. His access was substantially facilitated by his extended residence in Ndembu villages, his learning of the Ndembu language, and his demonstrated interest in Ndembu concerns. He participated in rituals, funerals, healing ceremonies, and court proceedings, developing a detailed understanding of how social conflicts emerged and were addressed ritually.

## Key Findings

Turner demonstrated that Ndembu rituals were solutions to recurrent social contradictions, particularly the contradictions generated by their kinship system. The girls' puberty ritual (*Nkang'a*) celebrated the transition of a girl to womanhood while also managing anxieties about sexuality and fertility; the ritual involved an extended period (weeks) of instruction, public display, seclusion, and reincorporation. Turner showed that the ritual's duration and phases corresponded to the social work the ritual performed—gradual transformation of the girl's social status while initiating her into women's knowledge. The ritual involved symbolic actions whose meanings were understood differently by different participants: some symbols referred to fertility, others to proper sexual comportment, others to women's collective identity. Similarly, Ndembu healing rituals addressed illness understood as caused by vengeful spirits of deceased kinspeople, usually understood as expressing unresolved social conflicts. The healing ritual brought together the afflicted person, kinspeople, and healers, and through a process of divination, discussion, and ritual action, it identified the source of affliction (usually a kinship conflict), generated a public airing of grievances, and through ritual reconciliation, resolved the underlying social conflict. The affliction disappeared when the social conflict was addressed. Turner crucially identified the liminal phase of ritual (the ritual's middle period, separated from ordinary time and routine social structure) as a state in which normal status distinctions were dissolved. In the girls' puberty ritual, the initiate was separated from her ordinary status; in the liminal period, she existed outside normal social categories. Within that liminal space, participants experienced communitas—a sense of unbounded, egalitarian community that transcended their everyday hierarchical positions. This temporary collapse of status distinctions and emergence of communitas served important functions: it created solidarity among participants, it generated a sense of transcendent meaning, and it permitted the articulation of social conflicts in forms that could be addressed.

## Ethnographic Examples

Turner records in detail specific rituals he observed, documenting the sequence of events, symbolic objects, and participants' reactions. He describes a girls' puberty ritual beginning with the girl's seclusion in a hut, her mother and female relatives maintaining her in ritual seclusion for weeks. During this period, elder women taught the initiate about sexuality, reproduction, and her obligations as a wife and mother. Turner notes that the teaching involved both explicit instruction and the symbolic interpretation of ritual objects: a rope representing the tying of kinship bonds, particular plants associated with fertility and protection. The initiate bathed in ritual water, was anointed with ritual oils, and gradually was reintegrated into the community through phases of the ritual. Turner records that when the initiate was finally presented to the community, the assembled persons greeted her with jubilation; her status had been transformed, and she was now recognized as a woman capable of marriage and childbearing. Turner also documents healing rituals in which an afflicted person's illness prompted investigation into kinship conflicts. In one case he records, a man's sister was ill and attributed her illness to the irritation of their deceased mother's spirit, understood as being angry because the man had not properly cared for their brother during the brother's final illness. The healing ritual brought the man and his sister together with other kinspeople, and the healer (a respected elder) guided discussion of the conflict, eliciting public acknowledgment of the neglect, and through ritual action and the payment of compensation, resolved the conflict. Turner notes that the afflicted woman's health improved once the kinship conflict was publicly addressed and resolved. Turner also documents divination rituals in which participants consulted oracles or experts to determine the sources of illness or misfortune. These rituals involved a period of questioning, interpretation, and negotiation in which the diviner and participants collaboratively arrived at an explanation for the affliction. Turner emphasizes that the process of interpretation itself was socially significant—the discussion permitted airing of grievances, acknowledgment of conflicts, and collective problem-solving.

## Theoretical Contribution

Turner's theoretical contributions to anthropology were profound. His concept of liminality—the idea that rituals create a phase of separation from ordinary social structure in which participants exist in an ambiguous state outside normal categories—revolutionized anthropological understanding of ritual and became hugely influential in performance studies. His analysis of communitas—the sense of unbounded, egalitarian community created in liminal phases—provided a framework for understanding how rituals generated solidarity and social integration despite operating outside normal hierarchical structures. These concepts moved beyond structural-functional analysis (which explained ritual as reinforcing social structure) to analyze ritual as generative, creating transformations and experiences transcending ordinary social organization. Turner's processual approach to ritual (understanding ritual as unfolding through time rather than as a static form) influenced subsequent anthropology toward performance analysis and toward understanding cultural events as dramatic processes. His demonstration that ritual symbolism was multivalent—that a single ritual object had multiple meanings accessed by different participants—influenced approaches to symbol and meaning in anthropology, suggesting that rituals could generate diverse meanings and address multiple social concerns simultaneously. Additionally, Turner's integration of social conflict analysis with ritual analysis showed that rituals were not merely expressions of stable social structure but were mechanisms for addressing and temporarily resolving structural contradictions. This insight influenced social theories of ritual and contributed to understanding how societies managed internal tensions. Turner's work also importantly shifted anthropological attention to subjective experience: he asked not only what rituals meant in structural terms but what rituals felt like to participants, what emotional states they generated, and what transformations participants underwent.

## Methodological Legacy

Turner pioneered several methodological approaches that influenced anthropological research on ritual. His systematic documentation of ritual performances—recording the sequence of events, the participants involved, the duration, and the participants' verbal commentary—established standards for ethnographic description of ritual. His method of post-ritual interviews, in which he questioned participants about their understanding of ritual symbols and actions, revealed that ritual meanings were contested and variable among participants, suggesting that anthropologists needed to attend to multiple interpretive voices rather than assuming a single coherent meaning. His comparative analysis of different rituals performed by the same society (puberty rituals, healing rituals, initiation rituals, divination) revealed patterns that might not be visible in study of a single ritual type. Turner's development of the extended case method—following a social conflict through its initiation, crisis, and ritual resolution, treating ritual as the culminating phase of a longer social process—influenced how anthropologists studied ritual as part of broader social dynamics rather than as isolated events. His attention to ritual as a temporal process, involving distinct phases with distinct social effects, pioneered what later became called performance analysis, influencing ethnographic study of ritual globally. Turner's approach of combining detailed thick description of ritual action with theoretical analysis of what those actions accomplished influenced the integration of ethnographic description with theoretical interpretation that became standard in anthropological monography.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has identified important critiques of Turner's approach. Some scholars have questioned whether his focus on ritual's integrative and conflict-resolving functions adequately accounted for rituals' potential to reinforce inequality, to serve dominant interests, or to exclude certain participants. Feminist scholars have noted that Turner's analysis of communitas did not adequately address how gender hierarchies shaped participation in ritual communities—whether women's experiences of communitas differed from men's, and how rituals might reinforce gender inequalities even as they generated egalitarian experiences. Postcolonial scholars have questioned whether Turner adequately contextualized Ndembu rituals within colonialism and the broader political economy of Northern Rhodesia, whether colonial disruptions were sufficiently attended to, and whether his analysis of ritual as addressing social contradictions accounted for the ways colonialism itself generated the contradictions rituals addressed. Some have also argued that Turner's theoretical concepts (liminality, communitas, communitas) were sufficiently abstracted from Ndembu ethnographic specificity that they risked universalizing structures specific to particular historical moments and societies. Despite these critiques, *The Ritual Process* remains enormously influential and valuable. The concepts Turner developed have proven applicable across diverse ritual contexts globally, and the work established ritual as central to anthropological analysis. For contemporary anthropologists, the volume remains valuable as a model of how ethnographic detail can generate theoretical insights and how ritual can be analyzed as a complex social process addressing multiple domains simultaneously. The text's attention to subjective experience, emotional states, and transformation remains influential in contemporary anthropology's engagement with ritual and performance.
