# Evicted

*Evicted: Poverty and Profit in the American City* (2016), by the sociologist Matthew Desmond, is an ethnographic study of eviction in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, during and after the 2008 financial crisis. Desmond followed eight families — Black and white, living in a trailer park on the city's South Side and in a rooming house and apartment buildings in the predominantly Black inner city — as they struggled to pay rent, faced eviction in court, lost their homes, and tried to start again. His central argument is that eviction is not merely a consequence of poverty but a cause of it: losing a home triggers a cascade of losses — possessions, jobs, schooling, health, and community ties — that makes it harder to escape poverty, while at the same time eviction is a profitable business for landlords who rent substandard housing to people with nowhere else to go.

Desmond came to the subject through his doctoral research at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, where he earned his PhD in sociology in 2010. He was struck by how little sociological research existed on eviction, despite its scale: eviction was common among poor renters in Milwaukee, and nationally millions of eviction filings are recorded each year. The book's subtitle, *Poverty and Profit*, captures its double focus. On one side are tenants who spend most of their income on rent — in the book, several families pay most of their income to landlords — leaving almost nothing for food, medicine, or transport. On the other side are landlords, most vividly Sherrena Tarver, who owns dozens of units in the inner city, and Tobin Charney, who runs the trailer park, both of whom treat eviction as a routine management tool and a source of income.

## Method

Desmond's method was immersive participant observation. In 2008 and 2009 he lived in a trailer park and then in a rooming house in Milwaukee's inner city, moving with tenants through housing court, welfare offices, shelters, and new apartments. He recorded court proceedings, kept detailed field notes, and later linked his ethnographic material to court records and to the Milwaukee Area Renters Study, a survey he designed of more than a thousand renter households. This combination of ethnography and survey data allowed him to show that the experiences he witnessed up close were not exceptional: eviction was a regular, patterned feature of poor renters' lives, and it fell most heavily on Black women, who were evicted at higher rates than any other group.

## Key Findings

The first finding is that eviction is a common event, not a rare catastrophe. In the Milwaukee court records Desmond examined, eviction was a routine part of the housing market for the poor, and many evictions were for non-payment of rent by tenants who were already spending most of their income on housing. The second is that eviction deepens poverty in specific, measurable ways. Families who are evicted lose possessions that cannot be moved or stored, children change schools, adults miss work or lose jobs, and the eviction record itself becomes a black mark that makes the next landlord less willing to rent to them, pushing them toward worse housing on worse terms. Desmond's survey-based articles found that evicted mothers were more likely to experience material hardship, depression and subsequent housing instability than comparable mothers who were not evicted.

A third finding concerns the structure of the market. Because poor tenants have few alternatives, landlords in the cheapest segment of the market can charge rents that are high relative to the quality of the housing, and can profit from turnover: fees, forfeited deposits, and the re-renting of the same unit. Desmond argues that this is not primarily a story of individual villainy but of a market in which exploitation is built into the business model. A fourth finding is the gendered and racial character of eviction. Black women, particularly mothers, faced the highest risk, a pattern Desmond links to the intersection of racial discrimination in housing, the low wages of service work, and the demands of caring for children. Finally, the book shows how eviction interacts with other institutions: an eviction can trigger child welfare involvement, loss of benefits, and contact with the criminal justice system, so that a housing problem becomes a problem in every domain of life.

## Reception and Influence

*Evicted* was published in March 2016 and became a bestseller and a critical success. It won the 2017 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction, the National Book Critics Circle Award, the Robert F. Kennedy Book Award, and the PEN/John Kenneth Galbraith Award for Nonfiction. Reviewers praised its combination of rigorous research and narrative power, and it has been widely adopted in university courses on poverty, urban sociology, and housing policy. Desmond went on to found the Eviction Lab at Princeton University, which collects and publishes national data on eviction filings, and his later book *Poverty, by America* (2023) extends the argument that American poverty is sustained by policies and markets that benefit the affluent.

The study reshaped the sociological understanding of housing. Before *Evicted*, sociologists had treated housing mainly as a background condition of poverty; Desmond showed it to be a mechanism that produces poverty, comparable to job loss or illness. The book has influenced policy debates about rental assistance, eviction moratoria — which became a national issue during the COVID-19 pandemic — and the right to counsel in housing court. It also revived the tradition of immersive urban ethnography associated with the Chicago School and with William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943), demonstrating that long-term fieldwork in a single city could support claims of national significance when paired with systematic data.
