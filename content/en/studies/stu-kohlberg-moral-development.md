# Lawrence Kohlberg's Study of the Stages of Moral Development and the Heinz Dilemma

A landmark longitudinal study in cognitive-developmental moral psychology. Initiated in Lawrence Kohlberg's doctoral dissertation at the University of Chicago, the research presented children and adolescents with open-ended hypothetical moral dilemmas—most famously the Heinz dilemma—to track the developmental trajectory of structural moral reasoning.

## The Three Moral Levels and Six Stages

Kohlberg classified moral justifications not by whether a specific action was endorsed, but by the cognitive structure of the underlying rationale, organizing moral reasoning into three successive levels containing two stages each:

### Level I: Preconventional Morality
- **Stage 1 (Heteronomous Morality / Obedience and Punishment Orientation)**: Rules are viewed as fixed and external. Actions are evaluated strictly by their immediate physical consequences; an act is wrong simply because it brings punishment.
- **Stage 2 (Individualism, Instrumental Purpose, and Exchange)**: Right action consists of what satisfies one's personal needs and occasionally the needs of others. Reciprocity is understood instrumentally ("tit-for-tat") rather than out of loyalty or justice.

### Level II: Conventional Morality
- **Stage 3 (Mutual Interpersonal Expectations / Conformity)**: Morality is defined by living up to social roles, seeking approval, and maintaining mutual trust ("good boy / nice girl" orientation). Intentions become central to moral evaluation.
- **Stage 4 (Social System and Conscience Maintenance / Law and Order)**: Moral duty consists of upholding societal laws, maintaining authority, and preserving the functional social order.

### Level III: Postconventional or Principled Morality
- **Stage 5 (Social Contract or Utility and Individual Rights)**: Laws are recognized as flexible social contracts aimed at preserving fundamental rights and maximizing social welfare. Unjust laws may be democratically challenged.
- **Stage 6 (Universal Ethical Principles)**: Moral decisions are guided by self-chosen abstract ethical principles (such as universal human dignity, justice, and equality) that take precedence over contradictory statutory laws.

## Context, Dilemma Design, and Longitudinal Follow-Up

In his 1958 study, Kohlberg administered 10 hypothetical moral dilemmas via semi-structured interviews to 72 boys from Chicago aged 10, 13, and 16. In the classic "Heinz dilemma," a woman is dying from a rare form of cancer, and a druggist has invented a radium-based drug that could cure her, but charges ten times its production cost. Unable to raise the money, Heinz breaks into the store to steal the drug for his wife. Kohlberg evaluated the rationale behind participants' judgments through the Moral Judgment Interview (MJI) scoring system.

In a comprehensive 20-year longitudinal follow-up (Colby, Kohlberg et al., 1983), the research team tracked 58 of the original boys, retesting them every three to four years. The findings supported an invariant, non-regressive developmental progression through Stages 1 to 4. However, Stage 5 reasoning emerged only in adulthood among a minority of participants, and Stage 6 reasoning occurred so infrequently that Kohlberg eventually treated it as a theoretical ideal rather than an empirically verifiable stage.

## Methodological Critique and Alternative Models

Kohlberg's framework generated significant critical debate on multiple fronts:

1. **Carol Gilligan's Care Ethics Critique**: In *In a Different Voice* (1982), Gilligan argued that Kohlberg's initial all-male cohort biased his model toward an abstract, rights-oriented "ethics of justice," systematically undervaluing the relational, contextual "ethics of care" characteristic of female socialization. Subsequent meta-analyses (such as Jaffee and Hyde, 2000) indicated small gender differences in care and justice orientations, but Gilligan's work established care and connection as foundational moral domains.
2. **Cross-Cultural Validity**: Anthropologists and psychologists (notably Richard Shweder, 1987) demonstrated that Kohlberg's framework presupposes Western liberal individualism. In traditional and collectivist societies, moral frameworks rooted in community obligation, filial duty, and religious sanctification were often misclassified as "conventional" (Stage 4) rather than postconventional reasoning.
3. **Social Intuitionism**: Jonathan Haidt (2001) contested Kohlberg's rationalist premise, arguing that moral judgments arise rapidly and automatically from affective intuitions, while conscious moral reasoning primarily serves as post-hoc rationalization ("the emotional dog and its rational tail").
