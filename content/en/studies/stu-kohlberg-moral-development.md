# Lawrence Kohlberg's Study of the Stages of Moral Development and the Heinz Dilemma

A landmark longitudinal study in cognitive-developmental moral psychology; in it Kohlberg presented complex moral dilemmas (the most famous being the Heinz dilemma, the theft of a drug to save his wife), and classified the participants' logical justifications into 3 levels and 6 stages graded in moral reasoning.

## The Three Moral Levels

1. Preconventional (fear of punishment and self-interest). 2. Conventional (conforming to society's expectations and upholding the law). 3. Postconventional (universal moral principles and human rights).

## Gilligan's Critique and Haidt's Social Intuitionism

Kohlberg was criticized because his first sample was limited to males and because he neglected the emotional and intuitive dimension in moral judgments. Carol Gilligan, who worked as a research assistant to Kohlberg, argued that his scale is structurally biased toward an abstract "ethics of justice" favored by male upbringing, ignoring the relational "ethics of care" that emerges more in the moral thinking of girls and women. Jonathan Haidt, for his part, offered a different critique (his social intuitionist model, 2001), proposing that moral judgment is in reality primarily intuitive and rapidly emotional, and that the rational justification Kohlberg measures is largely a later attempt to justify a judgment already made intuitively, not the process that produces the judgment itself.

## Context and Experimental Design

For his doctoral dissertation at the University of Chicago, Lawrence Kohlberg interviewed 72 American boys aged 10 to 16 and presented them with a series of hypothetical moral dilemmas, the most famous of which is the "Heinz dilemma": a man cannot afford a drug that could save his dying wife; should he steal it from the pharmacist who is charging a price far beyond his means? Kohlberg was not interested in whether the child answered yes or no, but in the kind of logic and justification he used to defend his answer, and he classified these justifications into six successive stages distributed over three major levels, and followed the same participants at intervals of about three to four years for more than twenty years to track the development of their moral thinking.
