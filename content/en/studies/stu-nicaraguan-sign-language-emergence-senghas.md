# The Emergence of Nicaraguan Sign Language: Senghas's Studies

A series of studies led by the linguist Ann Senghas since the 1990s on **Nicaraguan Sign Language** (NSL), a sign language that arose within a few decades (two or three successive cohorts of signers), when isolated deaf Nicaraguan children were gathered in special schools beginning in the late 1970s. Other researchers, notably Judy Kegl, also documented the case. They developed among themselves a full-fledged sign language without receiving any mature sign-language model from adults.

## A unique natural laboratory

This case provides an exceptionally rare opportunity: observing the emergence of a complete new human language "from zero," in real and documented time, instead of relying on speculative historical reconstruction of the emergence of the first languages. Senghas observed that the first generation of children developed a relatively simple sign system, often compared to a "pidgin." However, later generations of children who learned this system at an early age developed it spontaneously into a more complex and regular grammatical system (including spatial inflection and a regular agreement grammar), without any deliberate teaching intervention from adults.

## Theoretical significance

These results have been used as supporting evidence for the generative "poverty of the stimulus" hypothesis: the children's ability to generate a grammatical system more complex than the limited linguistic input they received points to an innate linguistic predisposition that goes beyond mere imitation. The case also provided a living example for studying how a "pidgin turns into a creole" (pidgin-to-creole transition) in the signed rather than the spoken medium.

## Historical background of the case

Before the deaf children were gathered in special schools in the late 1970s, in special education programs that began in the late 1970s and expanded after the Sandinista revolution of 1979, deaf people in Nicaragua were largely isolated in the homes of their scattered families, using rudimentary domestic gestures (known as "home sign") that did not constitute a complete language, nor did they share any kinship among families. When they were first brought together at school, they had no shared mature sign-language model to imitate (their teachers were hearing people who taught speech and lip-reading, not sign language). They therefore had to communicate among themselves by mixing their diverse home gestures, which gradually produced an entirely new shared sign system.

## Method for tracing change across generations

Senghas took advantage of the fact that Nicaraguan Sign Language is still "alive" and in the process of forming. She was able to compare the signs of speakers from different generations (the first generation that entered school in the 1970s, versus later generations who learned the language from older peers at a younger age) on the same descriptive task. She found that each new generation increased the regularity and complexity of the spatial grammatical system relative to the generation before it, even though the linguistic input available to each new generation was not necessarily richer than that of the preceding generation itself.
