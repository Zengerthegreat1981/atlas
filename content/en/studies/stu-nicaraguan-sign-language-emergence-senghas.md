# The Emergence of Nicaraguan Sign Language: Senghas's Studies

Studies by Ann Senghas and colleagues, from the 1990s onward, of **Nicaraguan Sign Language** (Idioma de Señas de Nicaragua). Deaf children who had little contact with one another were brought into special schools in Managua from the late 1970s, with the school population growing after the 1979 Sandinista revolution. Judy Kegl, Senghas, Marie Coppola, and others documented the signing that developed there. The children did not receive a mature national sign language from their teachers, who at first emphasized speech and lip-reading. They did bring idiosyncratic home signs, so the language did not appear from a blank state.

## Cohorts

The first cohort built a shared lexicon and a relatively variable grammar out of contact among those home-sign systems. Later cohorts, who learned that signing from older peers while still young, produced more regular spatial grammar. Senghas and Coppola (2001) reported that signers who had entered the community younger were more likely to use spatially modulated signs for agreement than the older first-cohort signers whose signing was their input. Senghas, Kita, and Özyürek (2004) reported a related result for manner and path of motion: younger signers more often separated the two into consecutive signs, while older signers more often combined them in one gesture-like sign. Teachers did not design these patterns. The comparison with a pidgin becoming a creole is an analogy used in the literature; NSL is a sign language with its own history, not a relexified spoken creole.

## Theoretical significance

The cohort results are cited in arguments about poverty of the stimulus: children produced structure that was more regular than the signing they saw from the previous cohort. That use is contested. The same facts are also read as evidence about learning from peers, sequential cohorts, and biases that segment events, without a commitment to an innate grammar of the generative type. Senghas's own papers emphasize the sequence of child learners. Home sign, as Goldin-Meadow has described it in other settings, already has some structure; NSL is what happened when many such signers formed a community and younger children acquired the shared system early.

## Historical background of the case

Before the Managua programs expanded, most deaf Nicaraguans lived with hearing families and used home sign that was not shared across households. The new school and vocational settings created daily contact among deaf children and adolescents. Signing spread horizontally among peers. A national sign language with native users of successive ages is the result of that contact over roughly two decades, and it has continued to change since.

## Method for tracing change across generations

Because the community is recent, age of entry is observable. Senghas compared people who arrived in the late 1970s and early 1980s with those who acquired the developing language younger, using the same elicitation tasks (descriptions of motion and of spatial relations). The design does not claim that every later cohort received poorer input in every domain. It claims that, for the spatial devices under study, the younger signers' output was more systematic than the older signers' output that had been the model. Fieldwork and change in the language are ongoing.
