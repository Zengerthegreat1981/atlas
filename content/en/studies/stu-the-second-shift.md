# The Second Shift

*The Second Shift: Working Parents and the Revolution at Home* (1989), by the sociologist Arlie Russell Hochschild with Anne Machung, is an influential study of family life. Hochschild interviewed fifty dual-earner couples, mainly in the San Francisco Bay Area, and found that even in households where both partners worked full time, women still did the overwhelming share of the housework and childcare. She named this unpaid labour after the paid working day the "second shift," and the phrase entered the language of sociology, feminism, and everyday life.

## The Setting

By the late 1980s the transformation of American family life was well advanced: most mothers of young children were in paid work, and the two-earner household had become the norm. Yet the institutions of work and family had not caught up. Workplaces still assumed a worker with a wife at home, schools and childcare services were thin, and the division of labour inside the home had changed far more slowly than the division of labour outside it. Hochschild called this gap the "stalled revolution": women had entered the workforce, but the home and the workplace had not been reorganised to match.

## Method

Hochschild's method was intensive interviewing combined with home observation. She and her research team interviewed fifty couples, in most cases speaking to husband and wife separately and together, and, for a smaller number of couples, observing family life at home — who cooked, who cleaned, who bathed the children, and who kept track of the household's needs. The couples came from a range of occupations and backgrounds, and the interviews probed not only the division of tasks but the feelings around it: gratitude, resentment, guilt, and pride. Hochschild paid close attention to the stories couples told about themselves, because she believed that what people said about their marriages revealed the "gender strategies" they used to make sense of unequal arrangements.

## Key Findings

The central finding was stark: in the couples Hochschild studied, the second shift fell mainly on women. Drawing on earlier time-use studies, she estimated that women worked roughly fifteen hours a week more than men when paid work, housework, and childcare were added together — the equivalent of an extra month of twenty-four-hour days each year. Men had increased their share of housework only slightly since the 1960s, while women's paid hours had risen sharply. The result was a "leisure gap": women had less free time, less sleep, and more of the mental work of running a household — remembering birthdays, scheduling appointments, and noticing what needed doing.

Hochschild also identified the emotional machinery that kept the arrangement in place. Couples developed what she called an "economy of gratitude": each partner kept a mental account of what the other contributed, and gratitude flowed toward whoever was seen as doing more than their share. Many couples sustained a "family myth" — a shared story that the housework was divided evenly, or that the husband was unusually helpful — that papered over the real imbalance. Some couples adopted a "traditional" strategy, in which the wife accepted the second shift as her duty; others aimed for an egalitarian division but found it hard to sustain; still others reached a transitional compromise. In the couples she studied, it was more often the woman who did the emotional work of keeping the peace.

The book also showed how the second shift shaped women's lives beyond the home. Exhaustion limited the time women could give to careers, friendships, and politics, and it fed a sense of being perpetually behind — at work, at home, and as a parent. Hochschild described mothers who felt guilty at the office for not being with their children and guilty at home for not doing enough at work, a double bind that had no equivalent in their husbands' lives. This analysis of guilt and emotional labour connected the book to Hochschild's earlier work on the commercialisation of feeling, and it gave the second shift a psychological dimension that time-use statistics alone could never capture.

## Reception and Influence

*The Second Shift* became widely read and a landmark of feminist sociology. Its central concept gave a name to an experience millions of women recognised, and it reshaped research on housework, time use, and the family. Later studies confirmed the basic pattern even as the numbers changed: the gender gap in housework narrowed over the following decades, but it did not disappear, and women continued to do more of the unpaid work. Hochschild returned to the theme in *The Time Bind* (1997), which examined how work and home had come to trade places in people's emotional lives, and a new edition of *The Second Shift* appeared in 2012. The book remains a standard text in courses on gender, family, and the sociology of work, and its argument — that equality at work requires equality at home — continues to frame debates about childcare, flexible working, and the division of labour.
