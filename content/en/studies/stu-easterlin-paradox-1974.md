# The Easterlin Paradox (1974)

Richard Easterlin set out the observation in "Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot? Some Empirical Evidence", a chapter in the 1974 festschrift for Moses Abramovitz edited by Paul A. David and Melvin W. Reder. The chapter posed a contrast that later writers called the Easterlin paradox: within a country at one date, people with higher incomes report higher happiness on average, yet long-run growth in average income does not appear to raise average reported happiness by a similar amount.

## The Dual Observation: Comparison Within a Country and Comparison Over Time

Easterlin used the happiness surveys then available, which were sparse by later standards. Within a country at a given date, higher income went with higher average reported happiness. Across countries, he did not find a clear positive relationship between average income and average happiness. Over time, especially in the United States, average reported happiness did not rise in step with real national income. The time-series and cross-country parts of the claim are what make the result a paradox relative to the within-country gradient. The samples were small, the questions were not identical across surveys, and Easterlin presented the pattern as empirical evidence, not as a finished law.

## The Explanation: The Importance of Relative Income and Adaptation

Easterlin explained this apparent contradiction with a central psychosocial argument. Subjective life satisfaction depends largely on social comparison (how an individual compares their situation with that of those around them and their social reference points), rather than on the absolute level of income. When everyone's income rises roughly in the same proportion over time, the relative ranking among individuals remains roughly the same, so the feeling of relative satisfaction does not improve overall despite the growing material wealth of everyone. Easterlin also added the mechanism of "hedonic adaptation": after a period of material improvement, individuals adjust psychologically to their new living standard, and their expectations and aspirations rise in parallel with their new income. This keeps their sense of relative satisfaction relatively stable despite the objective material improvement.

## The Impact on Public Policy Debates and the Measurement of Well-Being

The chapter became a standard reference in happiness economics, a line of work that treats survey answers about life satisfaction as data. It is often cited in arguments that national income growth is not, by itself, a complete measure of how people say their lives are going. Bhutan's Gross National Happiness framework and later proposals such as Kate Raworth's doughnut are separate policy projects. They share a scepticism about using GDP alone. They were not results of Easterlin's 1974 chapter.

## A Later Challenge: The Stevenson and Wolfers Study

Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers (2008) reassembled a much larger set of surveys. They reported a positive, roughly logarithmic link between income and subjective well-being both within countries and across countries, including at high incomes, and they argued that the time-series evidence did not support a general claim that growth leaves average happiness unchanged. Easterlin and co-authors replied that short-run movements can be positive while the long-run relationship in a country remains flat. The disagreement turns on which surveys are comparable, how long a "long run" is, and whether a log relationship that is hard to see in short national series counts as a refutation. The 1974 claim in its strongest form, that growth and average happiness are unrelated, is not a settled finding. The within-country fact, that richer people report higher satisfaction at a given date, is much less disputed.
