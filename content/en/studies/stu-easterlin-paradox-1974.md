# The Easterlin Paradox (1974)

An influential empirical observation was presented by the economist Richard Easterlin in a 1974 essay titled "Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot? Some Empirical Evidence", published in a festschrift volume for Moses Abramovitz It posed an apparent contradiction that puzzled economists for decades: why does average national happiness not appear to rise with national income over the long run, even though the wealthy within a given society seem happier than the poor at the same moment?

## The Dual Observation: Comparison Within a Country and Comparison Over Time

Easterlin drew on two different types of data available to him at the time. First, survey data on subjective happiness within a single country at a given point in time, which clearly showed that individuals with higher incomes reported on average higher levels of happiness or life satisfaction than those with lower incomes. This was an entirely expected result, consistent with the intuitive assumption that money buys well-being. Second, time-series data tracking average national happiness in a single country (mainly the United States) over several decades of continuous economic growth. Easterlin noticed that this average had not risen noticeably despite the large increase in average real national income over the same period.

## The Explanation: The Importance of Relative Income and Adaptation

Easterlin explained this apparent contradiction with a central psychosocial argument. Subjective life satisfaction depends largely on social comparison (how an individual compares their situation with that of those around them and their social reference points), rather than on the absolute level of income. When everyone's income rises roughly in the same proportion over time, the relative ranking among individuals remains roughly the same, so the feeling of relative satisfaction does not improve overall despite the growing material wealth of everyone. Easterlin also added the mechanism of "hedonic adaptation": after a period of material improvement, individuals adjust psychologically to their new living standard, and their expectations and aspirations rise in parallel with their new income. This keeps their sense of relative satisfaction relatively stable despite the objective material improvement.

## The Impact on Public Policy Debates and the Measurement of Well-Being

The Easterlin paradox has been widely used as an argument supporting a reconsideration of "economic growth" as the sole goal sufficient for improving the well-being of societies. It influenced the development of the later field of "happiness economics," and fed into wider debates about alternatives to GDP, such as Bhutan's "Gross National Happiness" policy framework and Kate Raworth's "doughnut" model, which share the scepticism the Easterlin paradox raised about the assumed automatic link between pure economic growth and actual human well-being.

## A Later Challenge: The Stevenson and Wolfers Study

Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers challenged the original paradox in a 2008 study. Using more recent and broader data across a larger number of countries over a longer period, they found a stable, positive, logarithmic relationship between income and happiness that continues even at very high income levels, with no clear sign of "saturation" or a sharp break in the relationship. This cast doubt on the general validity of the original paradox in its categorical form. The empirical debate remains open among researchers as to the validity of Easterlin's original formulation of the paradox, in comparison with the later Stevenson-Wolfers challenge.
