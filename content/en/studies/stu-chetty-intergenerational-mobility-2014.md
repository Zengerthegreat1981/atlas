# Chetty et al.'s Study of Intergenerational Mobility (2014)

This large-scale research project was led by **Raj Chetty** with **Nathaniel Hendren**, **Patrick Kline** and **Emmanuel Saez**. It used anonymized US federal tax records for tens of millions of individuals to map, in unprecedented detail, the variation in intergenerational social mobility across different regions of the United States. It is one of the most influential studies in contemporary economics of inequality.

## Methodology: exploiting vast administrative tax data

The researchers were able to access, under strict privacy safeguards, anonymized US federal individual income tax records. These allowed them to link the declared income of parents while their children were young to the income of those same children after they reached their early thirties, across tens of millions of individual records. This scale of data allowed researchers, for the first time with this precision, to calculate measures of social mobility for each individual county and urban area in the United States, rather than relying on a national average that conceals important local variation.

## The central result: enormous geographic variation within one country

The resulting maps revealed a stark variation in opportunities for upward social mobility between different American regions. Children born into families in the lowest income bracket (the bottom fifth) in some areas, such as San Jose and Salt Lake City, had a much higher probability of reaching the top income quintile in adulthood. By contrast, children who grew up in similar circumstances in other areas, such as Charlotte, Atlanta and several other cities in the Southeast and industrial Midwest, had a much lower probability of upward mobility.

## Factors statistically associated with low mobility

The researchers statistically linked this geographic variation to several local factors common to areas with low mobility. These include the degree of residential separation between neighbourhoods by income and race, which isolates poor children from better social networks and schools. They also include weak quality of local education available to the poor specifically, weak local social ties and social capital (measured by indicators such as the rate of participation in local civic associations), and a high share of single-parent families in the area (an association that also held for children from two-parent families in those areas, pointing to a community-level rather than purely family-level pattern). The authors also noted that income inequality, rather than average income, was associated with lower mobility.

## Correlation versus causation

The 2014 study is correlational at the level of areas, so it does not by itself establish that any one factor causes differences in mobility. Later work by Chetty and Hendren (2018), using families who moved between areas at different ages, provided quasi-experimental evidence that childhood exposure to a place has causal effects on adult outcomes.

## Impact on policy and subsequent research

The 2014 paper was the centrepiece of what was then called the Equality of Opportunity Project. That project was later renamed Opportunity Insights. Subsequent work has kept producing local mobility statistics and has studied housing programmes that move families, including a reanalysis of Moving to Opportunity. The 2014 maps are descriptive. They are widely used in policy discussion. They do not by themselves show which local policy would raise mobility.
