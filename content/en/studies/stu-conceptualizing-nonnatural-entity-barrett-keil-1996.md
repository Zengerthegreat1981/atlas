# Barrett & Keil (1996): Conceptualizing a Nonnatural Entity, Anthropomorphism in God Concepts

Justin Barrett (`thk-justin-barrett`) and Frank C. Keil conducted an experimental study published in *Cognitive Psychology* in 1996, titled *"Conceptualizing a Nonnatural Entity: Anthropomorphism in God Concepts"*. It is an early experimental study of anthropomorphism in god concepts, related to the broader argument by Stewart Guthrie (`con-perceptual-anthropomorphism-guthrie`) that religion is a form of anthropomorphism.

## Experimental Design: A Gap Between Professed Belief and Actual Inference

The researchers asked mostly Christian participants, who were university students in the United States, to rate God's attributes in explicit questions. The ratings matched doctrine: God is omniscient, present everywhere at once, not bounded by time and space, and can attend to many things simultaneously. They then presented participants with short stories involving God's actions and tested comprehension and recall of them. The result: in these tasks participants tended to reason about God as a limited agent who attends to one thing at a time, and they distorted or reconstructed story details in line with anthropomorphic assumptions. This contradicts their explicit statements that God is simultaneously present and all-knowing. Barrett later replicated the pattern with Hindu participants in India (1998).

## The Gap Between the Two Levels: Theological Theory vs. Spontaneous Cognitive Representation

Barrett and Keil named this gap between the professed level (theological correctness) and the level actually used in online reasoning (the spontaneous anthropomorphic level) as evidence that religious conceptions are built on innate cognitive templates for understanding agents, such as those the mind develops to understand other humans. This holds even when the individual rejects these templates theologically and consciously. The study supports Guthrie's thesis that belief in personal gods is not the product of purely doctrinal teaching, but an extension of a more general cognitive tendency to attribute intentionality and agency to anything that might be an agent.

## Its Impact on Cognitive Science of Religion

This study became a methodological model that other researchers in the cognitive science of religion (such as Boyer and Atran) followed to test theoretical hypotheses through controlled experimental designs (such as narrative comprehension and recall). They did so rather than relying on philosophical reflection or ethnographic description alone. The approach thus complements, rather than replaces, the long-term fieldwork approach, as in Harvey Whitehouse's work (`stu-pomio-kivung-fieldwork-whitehouse-1995`).
