# Dream Hoarders

*Dream Hoarders: How the American Upper Middle Class Is Leaving Everyone Else in the Dust, Why That Is a Problem, and What to Do About It* (2017), by Richard V. Reeves, is a study of the class that sits just below the very rich — and, Reeves argues, does the most to keep everyone else down. Reeves, a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution, argues that the American conversation about inequality has been distracted by the top 1 percent, while a larger and more consequential divide has opened between the upper middle class and the rest of society.

## The Argument

Reeves defines the upper middle class as households in the top fifth of the income distribution, roughly those earning above $112,000 a year at the time he wrote. This group, about a fifth of the population, is largely made up of educated professionals: two-income families in which both partners hold degrees, live in expensive neighbourhoods, and invest heavily in their children. The book's central claim is that this class is engaged in "opportunity hoarding" — using its resources to secure advantages for its own children in ways that make it harder for others to climb. America, Reeves writes, is becoming the kind of class-bound society its founders rebelled against.

## Method

The book is a work of policy-oriented social science rather than fieldwork. Reeves synthesises census and survey data, research on income, wealth, education, health, and family structure, and the findings of other scholars. He traces how the top fifth has pulled away from the middle and the bottom over recent decades: in 2013 the group held 52 percent of the country's wealth, up from 48 percent in 1983, while its share of income also grew. He pays close attention to the mechanisms of advantage, moving step by step through the life course — from birth and early childhood, through schooling and college, and into the labour market — to show where the gaps open and how they widen.

## Key Findings

Three mechanisms of opportunity hoarding stand at the centre of the book. The first is exclusionary zoning: wealthy suburbs use land-use rules to keep out cheaper housing, so that access to good schools and safe streets is effectively rationed by house price. The second is unfairness in college admissions, especially legacy preferences for the children of alumni, which give an edge to families already inside elite institutions. The third is the allocation of unpaid internships, which allow affluent young people to build the contacts and experience that launch careers, while poorer graduates cannot afford to work for free.

Around these mechanisms Reeves builds a picture of compounding advantage. Upper-middle-class children are more likely to be born to married, college-educated parents, to attend well-funded schools, and to expect not just a bachelor's degree but a graduate degree. By age twenty-five, about 60 percent of them hold a bachelor's degree, compared with 33 percent of the middle group and 10 percent of the poorest. Assortative mating — highly educated people marrying one another — reinforces the pattern, as does family stability. The result, Reeves argues, is declining mobility: poor children are more likely to remain poor, and affluent children to remain affluent.

Reeves is careful to say that the upper middle class is not made up of villains. Most of its members believe sincerely in fairness and opportunity, and many are uneasy about the advantages they enjoy. That, for Reeves, is precisely the problem: the hoarding happens through ordinary, respectable decisions — buying a house in a good school district, paying for test preparation, arranging an internship through a family friend — rather than through conspiracy or corruption. Because each decision seems reasonable on its own, the class as a whole rarely recognises the cumulative effect. The book's moral argument is therefore addressed to its own readers: those who benefit from the system are the ones with the power to change it, and the first step is to admit that the playing field is tilted in their favour.

## Reception and Influence

*Dream Hoarders* was widely discussed on publication, reviewed in major newspapers and policy journals, and taken up in debates about inequality, education, and housing. Supporters praised Reeves for shifting attention from the super-rich to the class that actually shapes opportunity in America, and for naming concrete policies — zoning reform, ending legacy admissions, paying interns — that could be changed. Critics argued that the book underplayed race, since Black and Hispanic families are sharply under-represented in the top fifth, and that some of its remedies were modest compared with the scale of the problem. Others questioned whether the top fifth is really a coherent class. Whatever the disagreements, the book's phrase "opportunity hoarding" entered the vocabulary of inequality research, and its argument continues to frame discussions of how the American dream is distributed — and who gets to keep it.
