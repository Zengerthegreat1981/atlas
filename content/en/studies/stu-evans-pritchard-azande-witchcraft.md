# Witchcraft Among the Azande

## Definition

A foundational ethnographic study by the British anthropologist E. E. Evans-Pritchard (1902–1973), published under its full title *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande* in 1937 by the Clarendon Press in Oxford, following fieldwork he carried out among the Azande people, chiefly in the southern Anglo-Egyptian Sudan (now South Sudan) with some work in the north-eastern Belgian Congo, in three expeditions between 1926 and 1930.

## Historical Context

Evans-Pritchard's expeditions were supported by the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan government, whose administrative network gave him access to Azande areas. The Azande are an agricultural people who speak a Ubangian language, and whose communities are spread across three different colonial states (Sudan, Belgian Congo and French Equatorial Africa).

## Core Ideas and Method

The central argument of the book is that the Azande belief in witchcraft (*mangu*) is not "irrational", as early evolutionary anthropology (Frazer, Tylor) assumed, but an internally coherent explanatory system that gives meaning to chance events. When a granary collapses on a particular person because termites have eaten its supports (a natural and accepted cause), the Azande ask, "Why this particular person at this particular moment?", and answer with witchcraft as a "social" cause that complements the natural cause rather than replacing it. Evans-Pritchard distinguished between witchcraft (*mangu*), an innate substance in the body that is inherited from parent to child of the same sex (from father to son and from mother to daughter) and is regarded as acting without ritual, and sorcery, which involves deliberate use of harmful medicines, and he studied the poison oracle (*benge*) as a means of identifying responsibility and settling disputes.

## Oracles, Doctors and Politics

The Azande consulted several oracles of differing authority. In the poison oracle, *benge* was given to fowls and a question was put so that the death or survival of the bird supplied the answer, often checked by a second test framed the opposite way. Cheaper and more readily available techniques included the termite oracle and the rubbing-board oracle. Specialist witch-doctors detected witchcraft in public séances. The poison oracle also had a political side: princes of the ruling Avongara clan controlled the verdicts that carried legal weight, and accusations of witchcraft were not openly directed at nobles. Evans-Pritchard further described how apparent failures or contradictions were explained within the system itself, for instance by bad poison, a broken taboo or interference by witchcraft, so that a single failed prediction did not call the whole system into question.

## Notable Works and Figures

Evans-Pritchard later returned to the Azande in *The Azande: History and Political Institutions* (1971). The book became a standard reference in the philosophy of the social sciences, as the philosopher of science Peter Winch drew on it in his well-known discussion of the "rationality" of different systems of belief; Winch argued that standards of rationality must be understood within the practices of each society.

## Impact and Criticism

The book deeply influenced the anthropology of religion and knowledge, and it provoked a long philosophical debate about the possibility of judging the "rationality" of a system of beliefs from outside it. It has been criticised for describing a coherent logical system more than the lived reality, which is sometimes contradictory among the Azande themselves.
