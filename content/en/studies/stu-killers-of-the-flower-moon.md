# Killers of the Flower Moon

In the 1920s, the Osage Nation of Oklahoma was, per capita, the richest people in the world. Oil had been discovered beneath their reservation land, and under federal law each enrolled Osage held a "headright" — a share of the oil royalties that could not be sold but could be inherited. Then members of the Osage began to die: shot, poisoned, blown up in their homes, one after another, in a string of murders the press called the Reign of Terror. David Grann's Killers of the Flower Moon: The Osage Murders and the Birth of the FBI, published in 2017, reconstructs the conspiracy behind the killings, the investigation that exposed it, and the much larger pattern of violence it concealed.

## The Author and the Question

David Grann, a staff writer at The New Yorker since 2003, came to the story through a historian's passing mention of the murders and a visit to the Osage Tribal Museum in Pawhuska, Oklahoma. He spent several years researching the book, drawing on FBI and court records, trial transcripts and private papers, and interviewing descendants of the victims and of people connected to the killers. His question was simple and, as it turned out, very large: who killed the Osage, and how many? The official answer, settled in the 1920s, named a single mastermind — the cattleman William K. Hale, the self-styled "King of the Osage Hills" — and the convictions of Hale and his accomplice John Ramsey for the murder of Henry Roan. Grann's investigation showed that the official answer was incomplete: the murders were far more numerous than the two dozen the FBI investigated, and the conspiracy was wider than any single man.

The book is organised in three parts. The first follows Mollie Burkhart, an Osage woman whose family was systematically targeted: her sister Anna Brown was shot in a ravine in 1921, her mother Lizzie died in 1921, her cousin Henry Roan was shot in 1923, and her sister Rita and brother-in-law Bill Smith were killed when their house was bombed in 1923. The second part follows the FBI investigation led by former Texas Ranger Tom White, sent by the young director J. Edgar Hoover in 1925. The third part follows Grann's own investigation in the 2010s, as he uncovers murders the FBI never pursued.

## Method: Archival Investigation

Grann's method was archival and forensic rather than ethnographic. He worked through thousands of pages of Bureau of Investigation files, court records, coroners' reports and newspaper accounts, cross-checking names, dates and headright transfers to build a picture of who benefited from each death. He interviewed Osage descendants, including members of Mollie Burkhart's family, and he consulted historians of the Osage and of federal Indian policy. Where the record was ambiguous — and it often was, because evidence had been destroyed and witnesses intimidated — he said so, distinguishing carefully between what could be proved and what could only be inferred.

A crucial part of the method was attention to the legal structure that made the murders profitable. Under the Osage Allotment Act of 1906, the tribe's mineral estate was held communally, with each of the 2,229 enrolled members receiving a headright. Headrights could be inherited, including by non-Osage spouses and relatives, and a 1921 act subjected Osage deemed "incompetent" to court-appointed guardians — usually local white men, some of whom stood to benefit — who controlled their money. The result was a system in which marrying an Osage, and then arranging for that Osage to die, was a rational way to acquire wealth.

## Key Findings

The first finding is the scale of the killing. The Bureau's investigation covered roughly two dozen murders in the early 1920s. Grann argues that the death toll was considerably higher, and that many deaths recorded as suicide, accident or natural causes may in fact have been murders. The conspiracy was not a single plot but a culture of killing: doctors who falsified death certificates, lawyers who manipulated guardianships, lawmen who looked away, and neighbours who profited.

Second, the study exposes the FBI's role as both heroic and self-serving. Tom White's undercover team — including the Ponca agent John Wren, one of the Bureau's few Native American agents — did genuine and dangerous investigative work, and the convictions of Hale and Ramsey were real achievements. But Hoover was eager to use the convictions to mythologise the young Bureau, and the investigation stopped once a satisfying story had been told. The deeper pattern — the guardianship system, the complicity of local officials, the uninvestigated deaths — was left in place.

## Reception and Influence

Killers of the Flower Moon was a number-one New York Times bestseller, a finalist for the National Book Award for nonfiction in 2017, and the winner of the Edgar Award for Best Fact Crime. The book brought the Osage murders — long known to specialists and to the Osage themselves — to a mass audience, though some commentators noted that its centre of gravity remained the white investigators. In 2023 Martin Scorsese directed a film adaptation starring Leonardo DiCaprio, Robert De Niro and Lily Gladstone, whose performance as Mollie Burkhart earned her an Academy Award nomination for Best Actress.

## Why It Matters

Killers of the Flower Moon matters because it shows how murder can be organised by law. The Osage were not killed in spite of the legal system but through it: through headrights, guardianships and inheritance rules that made an Osage death profitable to the people closest to the victim. That insight travels well beyond Oklahoma in the 1920s. It explains how institutions can produce violence without any single person ordering it, and why the exposure of one villain rarely repairs the system that made the villain possible.
