# Bowling Alone Studies

Americans were bowling in large numbers in the 1990s — and joining bowling leagues far less. That paradox, noticed by the political scientist Robert D. Putnam, became the emblem of one of the most influential research programmes in modern social science: the study of declining social capital in the United States. In a 1995 essay and then in the book Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community (2000), Putnam assembled decades of survey data to argue that Americans had withdrawn, since the 1960s, from the clubs, associations and informal gatherings that once held civic life together — and that this withdrawal was eroding trust, political participation and even public health. This entry treats the research programme itself: its data, its argument, its critics and its legacy.

## The Researcher and the Question

Robert D. Putnam, professor of public policy at Harvard University, had spent the 1970s and 1980s studying Italian regional government, work that produced Making Democracy Work (1993) and the concept of social capital: the networks, norms of reciprocity and trust that arise from connections among people and that make collective action possible. Returning to the United States, he asked whether the same resource was in decline at home. The question was prompted by a simple observation from the bowling industry: league bowling, a classic form of sociable, organised leisure, had collapsed even as the total number of bowlers rose.

The 1995 essay "Bowling Alone: America's Declining Social Capital," published in the Journal of Democracy, set out the thesis in brief. The 2000 book expanded it into a much longer study drawing on the General Social Survey, the Roper Social and Political Trends data, the DDB Needham Life Style surveys, and membership records from dozens of organisations.

## Method: Measuring Civic Life

Putnam's method was quantitative and historical. He tracked, over decades, membership and volunteering in civic organisations — the League of Women Voters, the Parent-Teacher Association, labour unions, fraternal orders such as the Elks and the Lions, veterans' groups, the Red Cross and the Boy Scouts — and he tracked informal sociability: dinner parties, card games, visiting friends, and participation in sports leagues. He tracked political engagement: voter turnout, attendance at public meetings, service on committees, work for political parties. And he tracked measures of trust: whether Americans believed most people could be trusted and whether they trusted government.

The pattern he found was striking in its consistency. Among the declines he reported were falling membership in the League of Women Voters and the Parent-Teacher Association from the 1960s, a drop in union density from its mid-century peak, a steep fall in league bowling during the 1980s and early 1990s even as the number of individual bowlers rose, and a decline in informal socialising such as entertaining friends at home. Voter turnout, volunteering and trust moved in the same general direction, though not all indicators declined to the same degree.

Putnam then asked what explained the decline. He apportioned the causes, with careful caveats: about 10 per cent to the pressures of work and two-career families, about 10 per cent to suburbanisation and commuting, about 25 per cent to electronic entertainment and above all television, and about 50 per cent to generational change — the passing of the unusually civic-minded generation that came of age during the Second World War, replaced by less engaged cohorts. 

## Key Findings

The first finding is the decline itself, documented across many indicators of civic and social life: across a wide range of independent indicators, American civic engagement fell from its peak in the 1960s and early 1970s. The decline was not uniform — some large advocacy groups grew — but Putnam argued that these were "tertiary" associations whose members rarely met one another.

The second finding is that the decline matters: Putnam linked social capital to trust, to political participation, to the performance of schools and neighborhoods, and to health and well-being, drawing on a wide range of correlational evidence. The third finding is the generational account. The decline, Putnam argued, was driven less by any single policy or technology than by the replacement of one generation by another: the long civic generation, socialised by war and depression into habits of joining, was dying, and the generations that followed had never acquired those habits. This is why the decline was so broad and so steady: it was a cohort effect, unfolding over decades.

## Reception and Influence

Bowling Alone was one of the most discussed social science books of its decade. It was praised for the scale of its evidence and for reintroducing the concept of social capital into public debate, and it was taken up by policymakers, foundations and community organisers across the political spectrum. Putnam was invited to meet political leaders, including U.S. President Bill Clinton, and the book's argument shaped a generation of research on civic life, volunteering and community.

It also drew sustained criticism. Theda Skocpol argued that the decline was driven less by individual withdrawal than by institutional change: national organisations had professionalised, replacing local chapters with centralised management. Pamela Paxton, using different statistical methods, questioned whether the decline in social capital was as uniform as Putnam claimed. Others pointed out that Putnam's measures missed new forms of association — online communities, immigrant organisations, self-help groups — and that his account of television as a cause was speculative. Putnam's later research programme included the Social Capital Community Benchmark Survey of 2000, which interviewed roughly 30,000 Americans, many of them in samples drawn from about forty communities.

## Why It Matters

The Bowling Alone studies matter because they made a private behaviour — whether you join a club — into a public question. Putnam's claim was that democracy is not sustained by constitutions and elections alone, but by the daily habits of association: the meeting, the committee, the league, the dinner party. When those habits fade, trust fades with them, and a society becomes harder to govern and lonelier to live in.
