# Ainsworth's Strange Situation Paradigm and Attachment Classifications

A standardized research and observational procedure for children aged 12-18 months that exposes them to a series of short episodes of separation from the mother and meeting a stranger and then reunion, in order to classify the quality of the attachment bond.

## Context and Experimental Design

Ainsworth developed the Strange Situation as a standardized laboratory procedure to observe the balance between exploratory behavior and attachment behavior under increasing, mild stress. Designed for infants aged 12 to 18 months, the protocol consists of eight fixed, three-minute episodes (except the brief introductory episode) conducted in a playroom equipped with a one-way observation mirror:
1. **Episode 1 (30 seconds):** Experimenter introduces parent and infant to the experimental room.
2. **Episode 2 (3 minutes):** Parent sits quietly while infant explores toys, testing the parent as a secure base.
3. **Episode 3 (3 minutes):** A friendly stranger enters, converses with the parent, and then approaches the infant with a toy. Parent leaves unobtrusively.
4. **Episode 4 (3 minutes or curtailed):** First separation episode. Infant is left alone with the stranger, assessing stranger anxiety and coping.
5. **Episode 5 (3 minutes or more):** First reunion episode. Parent returns and greets/comforts the infant while the stranger leaves. Parent then leaves again.
6. **Episode 6 (3 minutes or curtailed):** Second separation episode. Infant is left entirely alone in the room, assessing separation distress.
7. **Episode 7 (3 minutes or curtailed):** Stranger enters and interacts with the infant, testing whether stranger presence soothes separation distress.
8. **Episode 8 (3 minutes):** Second reunion episode. Parent returns, greets and picks up infant; stranger leaves quietly.

## Results and Conclusions

Coding focused primarily on the infant's behavior toward the mother during the reunion episodes (episodes 5 and 8), specifically evaluating proximity seeking, contact maintenance, avoidance, and resistance. Ainsworth established three primary attachment classifications:
- **Type B (Secure Attachment, ~65% in normative samples):** The infant actively explores in the mother's presence, shows visible distress or diminished exploration during separation, greets the mother enthusiastically upon reunion, accepts comfort, is readily soothed, and returns promptly to exploratory play.
- **Type A (Insecure-Avoidant, ~20%):** The infant shows apparent indifference during separation, avoids or ignores the mother upon reunion (averting gaze or turning away), treats the stranger similarly to the mother, and focuses defensively on toys.
- **Type C (Insecure-Resistant/Ambivalent, ~10–15%):** The infant remains wary and clings before separation, displays intense distress when the mother departs, and upon reunion demonstrates angry resistance (hitting, kicking, writhing away) mixed with desperate clinging, unable to settle or return to play.

In Ainsworth's Baltimore longitudinal cohort (26 families followed across the infant's first year, with 23 evaluated in the Strange Situation), these patterns correlated significantly with maternal sensitivity: mothers of securely attached infants responded promptly, appropriately, and warmly to their infants' distress signals at home, whereas mothers of avoidant infants showed rejection or aversion to physical contact, and mothers of resistant infants exhibited inconsistent responsiveness. These findings were expanded to 106 mother-infant dyads in the landmark 1978 monograph *Patterns of Attachment*.

## Criticism and methodological controversy

The Strange Situation became the standard laboratory measure of infant attachment and its classifications show reasonable stability and predictive validity. Critics, however, note that it samples behavior in one stressful context, that infant temperament may influence reactions to separation (a view associated with Jerome Kagan), and that its categories may fit some cultural settings less well. A meta-analysis by van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988) found secure attachment to be the most common classification in all countries studied, with avoidant patterns relatively more frequent in Western European samples and resistant patterns relatively more frequent in Japanese and Israeli samples; differences within countries were larger than differences between them.

## Later development: the fourth pattern and its intergenerational effect

Mary Main and her colleague Judith Solomon added in 1986 a fourth pattern, "disorganized/disoriented attachment" (Type D), as they observed that some infants display contradictory and frozen behaviors (approach followed by sudden freezing, or expressions of fear of the very parent who is supposed to be the source of safety), and research has since linked this pattern to risk factors such as child maltreatment and unresolved loss or trauma in the caregiver. Main and colleagues also developed the Adult Attachment Interview (George, Kaplan, & Main, 1985) to assess adults' "state of mind" regarding their own childhood attachment, and studies have found an association between parents' interview classifications and their infants' Strange Situation classifications (intergenerational transmission of attachment), though the strength of this link varies across studies.
