# Ainsworth's Strange Situation Paradigm and Attachment Classifications

A standardized research and observational procedure for children aged 12-18 months that exposes them to a series of short episodes of separation from the mother and meeting a stranger and then reunion, in order to classify the quality of the attachment bond.

## Context and experimental design

Ainsworth designed an observational procedure of 8 successive episodes, each lasting about 3 minutes (the first, a brief introduction, is shorter), in a playroom equipped with a one-way mirror: the mother and child enter, exploration of the toys, a stranger enters and interacts, the mother leaves and the child stays with the stranger, the mother returns and the stranger leaves (the first reunion), the mother leaves and the child stays completely alone, the stranger returns, and finally the mother returns and the second reunion takes place.

## Results and conclusions

The analysis focused on the child's behavior during reunion and divided the children into three main patterns: secure attachment (Type B: calms quickly when the mother returns and resumes play), insecure avoidant attachment (Type A: ignores the mother and avoids eye contact when she returns), and insecure resistant/anxious attachment (Type C: shows intense anger and clings to the mother while pushing her away and refusing to be soothed). In Ainsworth's Baltimore sample (26 families observed at home during the first year), these patterns were linked to the mother's sensitivity and responsiveness to the infant's signals at home; the 1978 book *Patterns of Attachment* drew on this and on several other samples, and the strength of this association has been debated in later research.

## Criticism and methodological controversy

The Strange Situation became the standard laboratory measure of infant attachment and its classifications show reasonable stability and predictive validity. Critics, however, note that it samples behavior in one stressful context, that infant temperament may influence reactions to separation (a view associated with Jerome Kagan), and that its categories may fit some cultural settings less well. A meta-analysis by van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988) found secure attachment to be the most common classification in all countries studied, with avoidant patterns relatively more frequent in Western European samples and resistant patterns relatively more frequent in Japanese and Israeli samples; differences within countries were larger than differences between them.

## Later development: the fourth pattern and its intergenerational effect

Mary Main and her colleague Judith Solomon added in 1986 a fourth pattern, "disorganized/disoriented attachment" (Type D), as they observed that some infants display contradictory and frozen behaviors (approach followed by sudden freezing, or expressions of fear of the very parent who is supposed to be the source of safety), and research has since linked this pattern to risk factors such as child maltreatment and unresolved loss or trauma in the caregiver. Main and colleagues also developed the Adult Attachment Interview (George, Kaplan, & Main, 1985) to assess adults' "state of mind" regarding their own childhood attachment, and studies have found an association between parents' interview classifications and their infants' Strange Situation classifications (intergenerational transmission of attachment), though the strength of this link varies across studies.
