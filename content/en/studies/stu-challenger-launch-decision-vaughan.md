# The Challenger Launch Decision

An in-depth organizational study by Diane Vaughan (published 1996), based on extensive analysis of NASA and Rogers Commission documents and interviews, to explain how the decision to launch the Challenger shuttle in 1986 was made despite earlier known engineering warnings of defects in the rubber O-ring seals, the shuttle broke apart 73 seconds after liftoff, killing all seven crew members.

## Method and Content

Vaughan rejected the common superficial explanations (managerial corruption, deliberate negligence, moral cover-up) in favor of a deeper sociological explanation she called the "normalization of deviance" (Normalization of Deviance). Each earlier launch decision in which the shuttle survived despite slight signs of erosion in the O-rings redefined this level of erosion as "acceptable" within the team's technical standards, rather than as a real danger signal for which the launch should be halted. This was a slow cumulative process across dozens of earlier successful launches that gradually "normalized" what had originally been regarded as a dangerous deviation from safe technical standards, until it became part of the accepted institutional routine, without clear awareness among the engineers themselves that they were taking increasing risks. Vaughan emphasized that the launch decision followed the agency's established rules and norms, so that the disaster was a "mistake" rather than "misconduct". The normalization was not the product of individual corruption or deliberate negligence, but the product of ordinary structural institutional pressures (tight schedules, budget pressures, an institutional culture that rewards adherence to the schedule) interacting with accumulated confidence in repeated earlier success. She linked these to a "culture of production" at NASA and to "structural secrecy", the way organizational structure and procedures limited what information reached decision-makers.

## Influence

The concept of the "normalization of deviance" offered a highly influential analytical tool for understanding major organizational disasters (the Columbia Accident Investigation Board, after the 2003 Columbia shuttle disaster, drew explicitly on Vaughan's analysis and identified similar organizational patterns). It became a widely cited concept in the sociology of organizations and in safety and risk management in fields such as aviation and medicine, showing how "good" institutions with good intentions can produce major disasters through the accumulation of small, gradual, unintended deviations.

## Methodological Context

Diane Vaughan, later a professor of sociology at Columbia University (she was at Boston College when the book appeared), relied on a large archive of NASA internal documents (engineering reports, meeting minutes, internal memoranda) collected mainly for the official investigation of the disaster (the Rogers Commission), but she re-read it through a sociological lens radically different from that of the official investigation, which leaned toward individual explanations (the errors of particular persons). Vaughan spent years analyzing these documents to reconstruct the chronology of the earlier launch decisions, showing how each step taken separately seemed reasonable and technically justified at the time within its own context, even though the sum of these steps cumulatively produced a major disaster.
