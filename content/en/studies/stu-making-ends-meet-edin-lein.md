# Making Ends Meet

*Making Ends Meet: How Single Mothers Survive Welfare and Low-Wage Work* (1997), by Kathryn Edin and Laura Lein, is a qualitative study based on in-depth interviews with 379 low-income single mothers in four American cities. It examines how these women actually balanced their household budgets, and it documented a stark gap between the income they could legally report and the expenses of basic survival.

## Method and Content

Edin and Lein interviewed mothers in Chicago, Boston, San Antonio and Charleston, South Carolina, in the late 1980s and early 1990s, choosing cities and neighbourhoods to give some regional, ethnic and economic variety. Their sample was made up of two groups: mothers who relied on welfare (Aid to Families with Dependent Children, together with food stamps and sometimes housing assistance) and mothers who held low-wage jobs. Rather than asking only about income, the researchers went through each household's monthly spending and sources of money in detail, and then compared the two.

Their central finding was that welfare benefits alone fell well short of what the welfare-reliant mothers actually spent on housing, food, clothing, transport and children's needs. To close the gap, nearly all of them relied on income that they did not report to the welfare agency: informal or off-the-books work, help from family and friends, contributions from the children's fathers and boyfriends, and help from churches and charities. They did so at the risk of losing benefits if the extra income was discovered. Mothers who worked in low-wage jobs also struggled: although their incomes were somewhat higher, work brought new costs such as child care, transportation and the loss of some benefits, and their budgets were also often short. The authors concluded that, for these mothers, low-wage work did not by itself provide a secure way out of hardship, and that the "hidden economy" of unreported income was a survival necessity rather than an opportunistic choice.

## Impact

The book was published in the same period as the debate that led to the 1996 welfare reform law, which replaced AFDC with time-limited assistance and stressed work requirements. It gave empirical weight to the argument that simple assumptions about work incentives overlook the real complexity of poor mothers' budgets, and it became a standard reference in the sociology of poverty, the family and social policy.

## Limits

The study is based on interviews and self-reports from a sample that was not statistically representative of all single mothers, and the account of unreported income depended on respondents' willingness to disclose it. The authors' conclusions concern the circumstances of the early 1990s and the benefit rules then in force; they do not describe the post-reform welfare system.
