# Children and Society

For most of the twentieth century, sociology treated children as unfinished adults: beings to be socialised, measured against developmental milestones, and understood mainly through what adults did to them. William A. Corsaro's long programme of ethnographic research on children's peer cultures turned that picture upside down. Working in preschools in the United States and Italy from the 1970s onwards, Corsaro showed that children do not merely absorb adult culture; they collectively produce their own culture — a stable set of routines, values, jokes, games and rules — and in doing so they actively interpret, resist and reshape the adult world around them.

## The Researcher and the Question

William A. Corsaro, professor of sociology at Indiana University Bloomington, began his fieldwork with a deliberately naive question: what do children do together when adults leave them to their own devices? Rather than testing children in laboratories or asking them to fill in questionnaires, he entered their world as a participant observer. His first major study, published as Friendship and Peer Culture in the Early Years (1985), grew out of extended observation in American preschools. In 1983–1984 he took up a Fulbright fellowship in Bologna, Italy, where he observed children in public preschools in Bologna and Modena, and he returned to Italy repeatedly over the following decades. The comparison between American and Italian settings became central to his argument: peer culture looks different where the surrounding institutions treat children differently.

Corsaro's theoretical frame was what he called interpretive reproduction. Against the older socialisation model, in which children internalise adult norms in a one-way process, interpretive reproduction holds that children appropriate elements of adult culture, reinterpret them in peer interaction, and then feed the results back into the wider society. Children are therefore cultural producers, not only cultural consumers.

## Method: Entering the Children's World

The method was long-term ethnography, sustained over months and years rather than days. Corsaro spent whole school days sitting on the floor with three- and four-year-olds, taking field notes, recording conversations, and gradually earning the children's trust to the point where they treated him as an unusual, non-authoritative adult who did not give orders or report them to teachers (he describes the children coming to treat him as a kind of "Big Bill"). He observed in private and middle-class preschools in California and Indiana, in government-supported Head Start programmes in Indiana, and in public preschools in Bologna and Modena. He later followed Italian children from preschool into elementary school with his colleague Luisa Molinari, tracing how peer cultures changed across that transition.

This approach carried obvious difficulties. An adult male researcher sitting among small children is conspicuous, and Corsaro had to negotiate access with teachers, parents and administrators, and to learn the children's own rules about who may join a game and who may not. His solution was patience: he entered slowly, accepted the children's rejections, and learned their routines — including their elaborate strategies for protecting interactive space, the physical and social territory of an ongoing game, from adult interruption and from other children.

## Key Findings

The first finding was that peer culture is organised, not chaotic. Children develop shared routines — greeting rituals, chanting games, pretend play scripts, disputes over toys — that persist across days and weeks and are passed on to newcomers. These routines are the infrastructure of children's social life, and they carry real moral weight: knowing the rules of a game, and knowing who is allowed to change them, is a central skill of childhood.

Second, friendship in early childhood is a serious social achievement. Corsaro documented how three- and four-year-olds negotiate access to play ("Can I play?"), defend fragile friendships, and use sharing as a political act. Disputes over a toy are rarely about the toy; they are about membership, status and the right to belong. The children's talk about "being friends" is laced with rules, tests and reconciliations that mirror adult social life without copying it.

Third, children actively resist adult control through what Corsaro, borrowing Goffman's term, called secondary adjustments: small, creative evasions of institutional rules. Children whisper when they are supposed to be silent, turn a tidy-up routine into a game, or use the toilet break as a social occasion. These are not acts of disobedience in the adult sense; they are collective assertions that the peer group has its own agenda.

Fourth, the cross-cultural comparison mattered. Italian preschools, more fully integrated into community life and less dominated by formal instruction than their American counterparts, were associated, in his observations, with distinctive peer cultures that differed from the American ones. The lesson was that peer culture is not a fixed stage of development: it is shaped by the institutions children inhabit.

## Reception and Influence

Corsaro's work helped found the sociology of childhood as a recognised field. His textbook The Sociology of Childhood, first published in 1997 and revised through several editions, became the standard introduction to the subject, and his 2003 book "We're Friends, Right?": Inside Kids' Culture brought the ethnography to a wider readership. The concept of peer culture is now used across early childhood education, developmental psychology and the sociology of education, and the interpretive reproduction framework is routinely cited alongside the work of Allison James, Alan Prout and Jens Qvortrup in the "new sociology of childhood."

## Why It Matters

The study of children and society matters because childhood is where culture is renewed. Every generation of children takes the adult world apart and puts it back together in play, and in doing so they reveal what that world means to the people who will inherit it. Society is not handed down from adults to children, but continually remade by both.
