# Middletown: A Study in Modern American Culture

A pioneering study by Robert Lynd and Helen Merrell Lynd, in which they applied a field-based, anthropological approach to a medium-sized American industrial city, helping to establish the "community study" as a form of sociological research in the United States.

## Method and Content

The Lynds chose Muncie, Indiana (about 35,000 to 38,000 people in the 1920s), which they gave the pseudonym "Middletown" to stress its supposedly typical character. They spent about eighteen months from January 1924 collecting data through observation, interviews, official documents, surveys and local newspapers. They organised their analysis around six main categories of activity: getting a living, making a home, training the young, using leisure, engaging in religious practices, and engaging in community activities. The study, published in 1929, showed how modern industry (including the local glass factories) was reshaping the town and described a sharp division between the "working class" and the "business class", with deep contrasts in the daily lives of the two groups within a single, relatively small community. Black residents were deliberately left out of the study.

## Influence

*Middletown* became an influential model of the "community study" as a way of understanding national social change (industrialisation, consumption, class) through the lens of a single community, and it paved the way for later studies such as W. Lloyd Warner's "Yankee City" series. The Lynds returned in 1935 and published *Middletown in Transition* (1937), which examined how the Great Depression had affected the town and its business-class leadership.

## Background of the Project

The study was commissioned by the Institute of Social and Religious Research, a body financed by John D. Rockefeller Jr., which wanted a study of religious life in a typical American town. The Lynds widened the scope well beyond religion to cover the whole structure of social life, and the resulting book was published commercially by Harcourt, Brace.

## Methodology and Class Classification

The Lynds distinguished between the "working class", whose members sold their time and physical effort for wages, and the "business class", whose members managed, owned, or sold goods, ideas and services. They argued that this division affected nearly every detail of daily life, from work schedules and social clubs to child-rearing practices, even though many residents thought of their community as essentially classless.
