# Kahneman and Tversky's Study of Risk Evaluation and Prospect Theory (1979)

One of the most influential and most cited papers in the social sciences and behavioral economics; it challenged the normative expected utility model of decision-making under risk and demonstrated that people evaluate prospects in terms of gains and losses relative to a neutral reference point, with losses looming larger than equivalent gains. Daniel Kahneman was awarded the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this line of research.

## Core Theoretical Components

Prospect theory replaces the global utility function of expected utility theory with two central functions:

1. **The S-Shaped Value Function**: Defined on deviations from an adaptable reference point (gains and losses) rather than total assets. The function is concave in the domain of gains (reflecting risk aversion for gains) and convex in the domain of losses (reflecting risk-seeking behavior to avoid a certain loss). Crucially, the function is steeper for losses than for gains, formalizing *loss aversion*—the psychological finding that the pain of losing an amount is roughly twice as intense as the pleasure of gaining that same amount.
2. **The Non-Linear Probability Weighting Function**: Decision makers do not weight outcomes by their objective probabilities $p$, but by subjective decision weights $\pi(p)$. This weighting function overweights low probabilities (explaining both lottery participation and the purchase of insurance against rare catastrophes) and underweights moderate to high probabilities, leading to the "certainty effect" where outcomes that are certain are heavily favored over merely probable ones.

## Experimental Samples and Problem Structure

The empirical findings reported in the 1979 *Econometrica* paper were obtained through a series of structured choice questionnaires administered to university students and faculty across several institutions (the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, the University of Stockholm, and the University of British Columbia). 

Rather than exposing a single subject pool to the entire battery, Kahneman and Tversky presented distinct choice problems to separate groups of participants, with per-problem sample sizes typically ranging from 66 to 95 respondents (for example, $N = 72$ for Problems 1 and 2, $N = 95$ for Problems 3 and 4, and $N = 72$ for Problem 14). By comparing between-group responses across mirror-image pairs of gambles, the authors isolated the "reflection effect" (the systematic reversal of risk preferences when shifting from positive to negative payoffs) and demonstrated violations of expected utility axioms such as the substitution axiom (the Allais paradox).

## Impact on Financial Markets and Policy

The paper became the bedrock of behavioral economics and behavioral finance, providing explanatory frameworks for phenomena such as the equity premium puzzle, the disposition effect (investors holding losing assets too long while selling winning assets too quickly), and consumer reaction to price changes. Behavioral insights derived from prospect theory later underpinned the "nudge" framework developed by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein, which employs choice architecture—such as automatic enrollment defaults and loss-framed disclosures—to encourage welfare-improving financial and health choices without restricting individual freedom.

## Replication Status and Methodological Debate

Prospect theory's central phenomena—framing effects, the reflection effect, and loss aversion—have been replicated in hundreds of laboratory and field studies across economics, psychology, and neuroscience. In 1992, Tversky and Kahneman refined the model into Cumulative Prospect Theory to handle continuous distributions and satisfy first-order stochastic dominance. 

Methodological debates persist regarding the magnitude and universality of loss aversion: aggregate reviews (such as Yechiam, 2019) have argued that loss aversion is attenuated in small-stakes gambles, routine market transactions, or environments where outcomes are learned through repeated feedback rather than described in hypothetical vignettes. Nonetheless, prospect theory remains the dominant descriptive model of individual decision-making under risk.
