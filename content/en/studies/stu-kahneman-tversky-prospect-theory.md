# Kahneman and Tversky's Study of Risk Evaluation and Prospect Theory (1979)

One of the most influential and most cited papers in the social sciences and behavioral economics; it challenged the "rational expected utility" model and showed that people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point, with losses looming larger than equivalent gains. Kahneman shared the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this line of work.

## The S-Shaped Value Function (Value Function)

Concave in the region of gains (risk avoidance) and convex in the region of losses (risk seeking to escape a certain loss).

## Impact on Financial Markets and Policy

The paper became a foundation of behavioral economics, applied to topics such as investor behavior and insurance choices. Behavioral insights of this kind informed the "nudge" approach to policy developed by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein, which uses knowledge of human cognitive biases (such as the default option and loss framing) to design choice environments that encourage better health and financial decisions without abolishing individual freedom of choice, as in automatic enrollment systems for retirement savings programs.


## Replication Status and Methodological Criticism

Prospect theory and the loss aversion effect have been replicated in hundreds of later experiments across the fields of finance, insurance and negotiation, and are among the most replicated results in behavioral economics. Nevertheless, a number of later reviews and aggregate analyses (such as Yechiam's 2019 review) have questioned how general loss aversion is, pointing out that its size varies with context and type of decision and that some studies found little or no loss aversion in certain settings. That is: the general direction of the theory is stable and replicated, but the effect size and its generality remain a matter of continuing debate with no full consensus.

## Context and Experimental Design

The study relied on questionnaires given to groups of university students and faculty, who were asked to choose between pairs of hypothetical "gambles" (for example: a sure gain of 3,000 versus an 80% chance of winning 4,000 and a 20% chance of winning nothing). The same questions were repeated with the wording of loss instead of gain, which revealed a systematic reversal in individuals' preferences between the gain and loss conditions, something the classical expected utility model does not explain. The two researchers observed that the majority of participants prefer to avoid risk when it concerns possible gains (choosing the sure amount of lower expected value), whereas the preference reverses when the same numbers are framed as losses, so that most choose the gamble over a sure loss (the "reflection effect").
