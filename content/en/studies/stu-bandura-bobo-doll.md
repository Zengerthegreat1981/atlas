# Bandura's Bobo Doll Experiments on Observational Learning and Aggression

Pioneering experiments in developmental and social psychology showing that nursery-school children who watched an adult model hitting and verbally abusing an inflatable plastic doll ('Bobo') imitated the same aggressive behaviors precisely when they were later exposed to frustration.

## Context and Experimental Design

Seventy-two children (36 boys and 36 girls, aged roughly 3 to 6) from the Stanford University nursery school were divided into three groups: one group watched an adult model carry out distinctive verbal and physical aggression against the Bobo doll (hitting it with a mallet, kicking it, and shouting phrases such as 'Pow, Boom'); one group watched a non-aggressive model playing quietly; and one was a control group. Afterward, all the children were exposed to a mild frustration situation (being prevented from playing with attractive toys) and were placed in a room containing a Bobo doll and other toys so that their behavior could be observed.

## Results and Conclusions

The children who had been exposed to the aggressive model showed precise and immediate imitation of the same aggressive acts and phrases, in addition to inventing new forms of aggression and using weapons the model had not used (such as toy guns). The other groups, by contrast, showed very little aggression. Boys were on average more physically aggressive than girls, and children tended to imitate same-sex models more than opposite-sex ones, with the clearest effect for male models. Bandura concluded that learning can occur through observation and modeling (Modeling) without the observer receiving direct external reinforcement.

## Criticism and Methodological Controversy

Critics argued that the Bobo doll was designed in the first place to be hit and to bounce back (Demand Characteristics) and that the experiment does not reflect real aggression against living human beings; however, Bandura's later experiments using filmed human and cartoon models showed that aggression was also imitated from film.

## The Theoretical Significance of the Result: Going Beyond Classical Behaviorism

This experiment represented a pivotal turning point in the history of psychology because it undermined the strict behaviorist assumption (Skinner) that learning necessarily requires direct experience and personal reinforcement; the children acquired complex aggressive behavior merely through passive viewing, without any direct interaction with the situation or receipt of a reward, which led Bandura to formulate "Social Cognitive Theory" later, stressing that internal cognitive processes (attention, retention, motor reproduction and motivation) are a necessary mediator between observation and behavior.

## The Subsequent Serial Designs

In 1963 Bandura, Ross, and Ross compared a live model, a filmed human model, and a filmed cartoon-like model (a woman dressed as a cat), and found that children in all three aggressive-model conditions showed more aggression than the control group and at broadly similar levels, which raised an early and long-lasting concern about the influence of film and television violence on children.
