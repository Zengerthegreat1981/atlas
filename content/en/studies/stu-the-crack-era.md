# The Crack Era

This entry, listed under the title "The Crack Era", presents Philippe Bourgois's ethnography of East Harlem during the crack cocaine epidemic, published as *In Search of Respect: Selling Crack in El Barrio* (Cambridge University Press, 1995). The book draws on about five years of fieldwork (1985–1990) among Puerto Rican crack dealers, drug users and local residents in El Barrio — Spanish Harlem — in New York City, during the early and most violent years of the epidemic. Bourgois, an anthropologist, moved with his family into a tenement apartment in El Barrio and spent years in the neighbourhood, including in crackhouses and on street corners. His central argument is that the crack trade and the street culture around it cannot be understood only as individual pathology or criminal choice but must be seen in the context of structural conditions: the deindustrialisation of New York, the loss of the manufacturing jobs that had once employed Puerto Rican migrants, racial and ethnic segregation, and the weakening of public services. The political response to crack was a war on drugs that sent large numbers of young Black and Latino men to prison. The book was among the first sustained ethnographic accounts of that world from the inside, and it received the C. Wright Mills Award of the Society for the Study of Social Problems in 1996.

## Method

The method was long-term participant observation. Bourgois's access depended on personal relationships, above all with Ray, who ran a local crack-selling operation, and with workers in his network such as Primo and Caesar at a crackhouse he calls the Game Room. Bourgois spent many hours in conversation with dealers, users and their families, recorded their words, observed their work and followed their lives over time. The book is built from transcribed conversations and observation, and it includes a history of El Barrio, an account of how a crackhouse was organised, and an analysis of street culture.

Bourgois's method raised questions that he addresses in the book. He was a white outsider living among people who were poor, mostly Puerto Rican and involved in an illegal trade; his presence depended on the trust of a small number of friends, and he writes frankly about the limits of that trust and about the ethics of writing on people who could be harmed by exposure. A second edition appeared in 2003 with additional material.

## Key Findings

The first finding is that crack dealing was, for many of these men, a rational economic strategy in a neighbourhood where legal work had become scarce and the available jobs were low-paid, insecure and often humiliating. Bourgois argues that the trade offered not wealth for most participants but a way of earning a living and gaining respect, a form of recognition that the formal economy did not offer them. The title, *In Search of Respect*, captures this argument.

A second finding concerns the organisation of the crackhouse. Bourgois shows that it was run like a business with hierarchy, rules and discipline, and that violence was tied in part to the need to enforce contracts and discipline in an illegal market without recourse to the courts. A third finding concerns the interaction of street culture and the wider society. Bourgois argues that the dealers' culture, with its emphasis on toughness, loyalty and conspicuous consumption, was both a response to marginalisation and a cause of further marginalisation, because the same traits that earned respect on the street made it difficult to keep a job in the formal economy. The book describes a cycle in which structural exclusion produces a street culture and the street culture deepens the exclusion.

## Reception and Influence

*In Search of Respect* was widely praised and became a classic of urban anthropology and sociology, noted for its depth of fieldwork, its candid ethnographic voice and its attempt to avoid both romanticising and condemning its subjects. It is widely assigned in courses on urban poverty, race, drugs and ethnographic method. Bourgois's later work with Jeff Schonberg, *Righteous Dopefiend* (2009), a study of homeless heroin users in San Francisco, took a similar approach, combining long immersion, photographs and attention to what Bourgois calls structural and symbolic violence. In developing that framework he built on the earlier concept of structural violence, associated with Johan Galtung, and on work by Nancy Scheper-Hughes. Critics have asked about the representativeness of a small circle of informants, about the book's heavy use of transcribed speech and its portrayal of its subjects, and about how far structural explanations can account for individual violence.
