# Among Schoolchildren

What does a year in an American public school actually feel like — for the teacher, for the children, and for the families waiting at home? Tracy Kidder answered that question by sitting for nine months at a small desk beside Christine Zajac, a fifth-grade teacher at the Kelly School in Holyoke, Massachusetts, watching twenty children learn, fight, fail and occasionally triumph. Among Schoolchildren, published in 1989, is a work of narrative journalism, drawing on extended observation, that sociologists of education have often read alongside formal ethnographies: a close, unsentimental account of how poverty, bureaucracy and human stubbornness meet inside one classroom.

## The Author and the Setting

Tracy Kidder, who had won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction for The Soul of a New Machine (1981), came to Holyoke after the success of his earlier books. Holyoke was a declining industrial city in western Massachusetts — a place of abandoned mills, burned-out tenements and a largely poor, racially mixed population of Irish, Polish and Puerto Rican families. The Kelly School stood in the Flats, the poorest section of town. Its fifth-grade classroom, taught by Chris Zajac, held twenty children of widely different backgrounds, abilities and needs: some reading far above grade level, some barely able to read at all, some carrying the effects of violence and instability at home.

Kidder's approach was total immersion. For one school year in the late 1980s he attended class nearly every day, sitting at a desk next to Zajac's, observing lessons, recess, staff meetings, parent conferences and home visits. The book reads like a novel but is presented as nonfiction, based on what Kidder observed.

## Method: A Year of Watching

The method is best described as participant observation by a journalist: Kidder did not teach, grade or discipline, but his constant presence made him part of the classroom's furniture, and children and teacher alike gradually behaved as if he were not there. He supplemented observation with conversations with Zajac and others connected with the school, and he paid attention to the institutional pressures bearing on the classroom.

## Key Findings

The book's central finding is that teaching in a poor urban school is an intensely personal and moral enterprise, not a technical one. Zajac is strict, funny, exhausted and idealistic by turns. She believes that her children — many of them written off by the system — can learn, and she structures her classroom around that belief: clear rules, relentless repetition, public praise, private correction. Kidder shows how much of her work is invisible to outsiders: the hours of preparation, the emotional labour of managing twenty children with different traumas, the negotiations with parents who are themselves struggling, and the constant awareness that a single bad week can undo months of progress.

A second finding concerns the children themselves. Kidder gives each child a distinct presence: Clarence, a troubled and defiant boy; Judith, an able and thoughtful girl; and Puerto Rican children moving between languages. Their problems are not abstract "educational disadvantage" but concrete and specific, rooted in family circumstances, health and emotional needs, and uneven preparation. The book shows how poverty enters the classroom not as a statistic but as hunger, fatigue, anger and shame.

Third, the study exposes the institutional context. The school serves a poor district with limited resources, and its teachers are caught between demands from above — standardised tests, curriculum mandates, paperwork — and the realities of the children in front of them. Kidder's account makes clear that the problems of American public education are not mainly problems of individual teachers, but of a system that asks teachers to compensate for inequality without giving them the resources to do so.

## Reception and Influence

Among Schoolchildren was a bestseller and a critical success, praised for its precision and its refusal to sentimentalise. It is widely used in teacher-education courses as a portrait of what teaching demands. Sociologists of education may read it alongside works such as Philip Jackson's Life in Classrooms (1968) and Jean Anyon's 1980 study of social class and the hidden curriculum, because it shows — rather than merely asserting — how social class shapes what happens between a teacher and her pupils.

The book also shaped public debate. Published at the end of the 1980s, as American anxiety about failing schools was rising, it complicated the simple narratives of blame: it showed a teacher working heroically within a system that was failing her children, and it made the case that improving schools would require attending to the lives children lead outside them. 

## Why It Matters

Among Schoolchildren matters because it answers a question that policy debates usually dodge: what is it actually like to be in the room where education happens? Kidder's year in Zajac's classroom shows that schooling is a relationship before it is a system, and that the quality of that relationship — its patience, its fairness, its refusal to give up on a child — is the real measure of a school.
