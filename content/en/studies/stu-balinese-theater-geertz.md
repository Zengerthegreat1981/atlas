# Balinese Theater and Aesthetic Performance: Geertz on Ritual Drama and Meaning

Clifford Geertz's monograph *Negara: The Theatre State in Nineteenth-Century Bali* (1980), analyzing the pre-colonial Balinese state through its theatrical performances and aesthetic practices. Rather than analyzing Balinese political organization through institutional structures or power relationships, Geertz approached the Balinese state as fundamentally theatrical—a state constituted through elaborate ritual performances, ceremonial display, and aesthetic representation. This innovative approach, combining ethnographic study of Balinese theater (cremation ceremonies, court dances, ritual processions) with historical analysis of the pre-colonial Balinese kingdom, demonstrated how anthropology could understand political systems through their aesthetic dimensions.

## Publication Context

*Negara* was published in 1980 by Princeton University Press, synthesizing Geertz's ethnographic research conducted in Bali from 1957-1970 and his engagement with historical sources concerning the pre-colonial Balinese state. The monograph appeared as the culmination of Geertz's work on Balinese culture and represented his mature theoretical approach to analyzing culture as systems of meaning requiring interpretive understanding. The book was somewhat controversial; some scholars questioned whether Geertz's emphasis on theater and aesthetics adequately accounted for economic organization, military power, and material bases of political authority. However, the work became widely recognized as an innovative approach to understanding non-Western states through their own aesthetic and ceremonial frameworks rather than through European-derived categories of political structure and power. The monograph has had significant influence on performance studies, on the anthropology of aesthetics, and on approaches to understanding pre-colonial and non-Western states. The work also influenced how anthropologists understood ritual and ceremony as not merely expressive of social structure but as productive of political authority and meaning.

## Research Question & Approach

Geertz's central research questions were: How was the pre-colonial Balinese state organized? How did the Balinese themselves understand their state and their position within it? Rather than analyzing the state through institutions or military structures (approaches influenced by European political theory), Geertz asked how the state constituted itself through ceremonial and theatrical performances. What was the significance of the elaborate state rituals and ceremonies? How did aesthetic performance relate to political authority? His methodological approach involved studying contemporary Balinese ceremonial life (cremation ceremonies, court dances, ritual processions) in the 1960s and 1970s, interpreting these as texts revealing pre-colonial state organization. He also engaged historical sources and worked with Balinese intellectuals to understand how pre-colonial states had operated. His approach treated Balinese aesthetic and ceremonial forms as windows into understanding how power and authority were constituted and displayed. Rather than assuming that state authority was based in military force or economic control (as European-derived theories suggested), he examined how Balinese political authority was performed and displayed through elaborate ceremonial and aesthetic practices.

## Ethnographic Setting

Bali, one of the Indonesian islands, is located east of Java and was home to Hindu-Buddhist populations who maintained distinctive cultural practices despite Muslim dominance of surrounding islands. The Balinese had developed a sophisticated pre-colonial state system and maintained complex ceremonial and artistic traditions. Geertz's fieldwork was conducted in the 1960s-1970s, after Balinese kingdoms had been incorporated into the Indonesian nation-state and had lost their previous political autonomy. However, Balinese ceremonial and artistic traditions remained vibrant, and Geertz was able to observe elaborate ceremonies (particularly cremations of high-ranking individuals) that provided windows into pre-colonial ceremonial practices. He worked with Balinese intellectuals, historians, and cultural experts who helped him understand how pre-colonial states had organized themselves. Geertz lived in a village setting and participated in ceremonial life to the extent possible for an outsider, though his access to certain ceremonies was necessarily limited by gender, kinship, and outsider status. His approach involved combining observation of contemporary ceremonial life with historical and textual analysis of pre-colonial practices.

## Key Findings

Geertz argued that the pre-colonial Balinese state was fundamentally a "theatre state"—a state organized around the performance of power rather than around the accumulation and deployment of military or economic force. The state's authority was constituted and displayed through elaborate ceremonial performances, particularly the great cremation ceremonies of high-ranking individuals. These ceremonies involved vast assemblies of people, elaborate architectural constructions, intricate ritual sequences, and artistic performances of great sophistication. Geertz showed that the cremation ceremonies demonstrated the power and prestige of the royal house not through military display but through the state's demonstrated capacity to organize elaborate ceremonial events that brought together thousands of people and involved elaborate artistic expression. The state demonstrated its power through ceremonial magnificence. Geertz further argued that the Balinese understood themselves as part of a ritual cosmos centered on the state: participation in state ceremonies was understood as participation in a cosmos-ordering enterprise, and the state's authority derived from its role in maintaining cosmic order through proper performance of rituals and ceremonies. The state's legitimacy derived not from rational-legal principles or from military domination but from its demonstrated capacity to perform properly the ceremonies that maintained cosmic order. Geertz also analyzed the aesthetic dimensions of state ceremonies: the elaborate dances, the intricate architectural compositions, the coordinated movements of thousands of people, all expressed aesthetic principles and demonstrated the state's sophistication and power.

## Ethnographic Examples

Geertz provides detailed descriptions of Balinese ceremonies he observed and of pre-colonial ceremonies recorded in historical sources. He describes cremation ceremonies involving the cremation of a high-ranking individual, the construction of a great tower (bade) for the corpse, the assembly of crowds from across regions, and the performance of dances and ceremonial sequences over days or weeks. He records that the ceremony involved intense physical labor and coordination—hundreds of men were required to construct the cremation tower and accompanying structures, the food provisioning required careful planning, the ceremonial sequences had to be precisely ordered. Geertz notes that individuals who participated in the ceremony gained prestige and status association with the ruler who organized it. He also analyzes the symbolism of the cremation ceremony: the tower represented the cosmos, the cremation released the soul for resurrection, the elaborate artistic display demonstrated the ruler's connection to cosmic forces. Geertz further describes court dances and theatrical performances, analyzing how the dancers' movements, their costumes, and the music coordinated to express aesthetic principles and political meanings. He notes that certain dances were restricted to royal courts and could only be performed by high-ranking individuals, making the dances themselves markers of rank and authority. Geertz also provides historical examples from pre-colonial times, describing how rulers had staged elaborate ceremonies and how contemporary Balinese understood those ceremonies as displays of state power.

## Theoretical Contribution

Geertz's theoretical contribution was to establish that the Balinese state could be understood as fundamentally aesthetic and theatrical—that political authority was constituted and demonstrated through ceremonial and artistic performance rather than through military force or institutional control. This argument challenged Eurocentric assumptions that all states operated through similar institutional and power mechanisms, and suggested that non-Western states might be organized according to fundamentally different principles. His work contributed to developing an anthropology of aesthetics and to understanding how aesthetic practices could be politically significant. His demonstration that political authority could be understood through analysis of ceremonial and theatrical performance influenced subsequent scholarship on ritual and performance as politically productive. His approach also contributed to developing theories of the state that emphasized the performative dimensions of state power—how states constitute authority through theatrical display rather than (or in addition to) institutional structures or coercive force. His work influenced the development of performance anthropology and contributed to understanding how culture and aesthetics were connected to political organization.

## Methodological Legacy

Geertz pioneered methodological approaches to understanding states through their ceremonial and aesthetic dimensions. His practice of using contemporary ceremonial life as a window into understanding pre-colonial political organization showed how ethnographic study could inform historical understanding. His close analysis of ritual forms and their meanings influenced methodologies for analyzing ritual performances. His integration of textual analysis (of historical sources) with ethnographic observation showed how anthropology could combine historical and ethnographic methods. His attention to aesthetic dimensions—the design of structures, the choreography of movements, the artistic coordination—influenced methodologies for analyzing aesthetics ethnographically. His approach of treating ceremonies as texts that could be "read" for their political and cosmological meanings influenced performance analysis and textual approaches to understanding cultural phenomena.

## Critique & Contemporary Value

Subsequent scholarship has identified important critiques of Geertz's approach. Some scholars have questioned whether Geertz's emphasis on theater and aesthetics adequately accounts for material bases of political authority—economic organization, control of land, military capacity. Postcolonial historians have noted that Geertz's analysis of the pre-colonial Balinese state is somewhat speculative, based partly on fragmentary historical sources and partly on contemporary ceremonial practices that may have changed significantly from pre-colonial times. Some have also questioned whether Geertz's interpretation of Balinese ceremonial meanings accurately represents Balinese perspectives or reflects Geertz's own interpretive frameworks. Balinese intellectuals have sometimes engaged with Geertz's analysis, appreciating aspects while questioning others. Despite these critiques, the work remains valuable as a sophisticated analysis of how ceremonial and aesthetic practices relate to political authority and as a demonstration that non-Western states operated according to different organizing principles than European nation-states. The work's influence on performance studies and on the anthropology of aesthetics has been substantial. For contemporary students, the text exemplifies how anthropologists can understand political systems through their own cultural categories and aesthetic frameworks rather than through Eurocentric concepts.
