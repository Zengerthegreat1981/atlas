# Balinese Theater and Aesthetic Performance: Geertz on Ritual Drama and Meaning

Clifford Geertz's *Negara: The Theatre State in Nineteenth-Century Bali* (Princeton University Press, 1980) is a historical-interpretive study of the pre-colonial Balinese polities. Rather than explaining them through institutions, coercion or economic control, Geertz argued that the Balinese negara (a word meaning both "state" and "capital/court centre") was constituted through ceremony: royal rites, cremations and temple festivals were not decoration for a state that really ran on power but were what the state was for. His memorable formulation is that power served pomp, not pomp power.

## Context

Geertz carried out fieldwork in Bali in 1957-58 (with Hildred Geertz), but *Negara* rests chiefly on historical sources: Dutch colonial reports, Balinese chronicles and court literature, and earlier ethnography, together with the author's own observations. The study concerns the Balinese kingdoms of roughly the nineteenth century, before Dutch conquest of the south of the island in the early twentieth century. It is a companion to his essays on Balinese culture in *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), including the essays on person, time and conduct and on the Balinese cockfight, but it differs in addressing the structure of the polity.

## Argument

Geertz described the Balinese state as a "theatre state." Several claims are central to the book.

First, the court was an exemplary centre: the royal palace and its ritual life were understood as a microcosm of the cosmos (in Hindu-Buddhist terms associated with Mount Meru), and the king's task was to embody and display the order of the universe so that others would imitate it. Order in the realm was thought to flow from the centre by example rather than by administration.

Second, the rulers' resources were spent on large-scale ritual: royal cremations, tooth-filing ceremonies and temple celebrations, in which thousands of people contributed labour, food and offerings. Geertz argued that status competition among lords was fought out through such spectacles rather than through the direct administrative control of territory.

Third, political structure in Bali was highly segmentary and fluid: lords had followers through shifting ties, irrigation societies (subak) and village associations were largely self-governing, and the central courts did not control them directly. The state was thus weak in routine administration but strong in symbolic claims.

## Ceremonial Examples

Geertz draws on accounts of royal cremations, in which the corpse of a ruler was carried to the burning ground in a tall tower (bade), with large processions and elaborate offerings, as occasions on which a lord's rank was displayed and made real. Such ceremonies required coordinated labour from many dependants and showed the ruler's capacity to mobilize. Aesthetic forms, including dance, music and architecture, are treated as part of this political expression.

## Reception and Critique

The book was influential for the anthropology of the state, for performance studies and for arguments that political authority has expressive and symbolic dimensions. It has also been criticized. Historians and anthropologists, including Dutch and Balinese-studies specialists, argued that Geertz played down material, military and economic aspects of Balinese rule, such as warfare, taxation in kind, labour obligations and control of land and people, and that the evidence for some generalizations was thin. Others argued that the Balinese court's concern with power was more instrumental than the "theatre" model allows. The "theatre state" idea has also been applied to other Southeast Asian polities, with similar debate.
