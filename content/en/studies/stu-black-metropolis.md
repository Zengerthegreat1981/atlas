# Black Metropolis

*Black Metropolis: A Study of Negro Life in a Northern City* (1945), by the anthropologist St. Clair Drake and the sociologist Horace R. Cayton Jr., is one of the great classics of American urban sociology. Based on years of research in Chicago's South Side, it presented Black Chicago as a "city within a city" — a metropolis with its own institutions, class structure, and politics, built inside a white city that confined it by segregation. The book remains among the most detailed portraits of a Black urban community in the first half of the twentieth century.

## The Setting

The Black Belt of Chicago's South Side, known to its residents as Bronzeville, had grown explosively during the Great Migration, as hundreds of thousands of Black Southerners moved north for industrial work. By the 1930s it was one of the largest Black urban communities in the United States, stretching for miles along State Street. It was also a segregated world: restrictive covenants, discriminatory lending, and white hostility confined Black Chicagoans to an overcrowded district, even as the community built churches, newspapers, businesses, and political organisations of its own.

## Method

The study grew out of research conducted in the 1930s under the Works Progress Administration, on which Drake and Cayton worked together. The authors combined the methods of anthropology and sociology: participant observation in churches, clubs, bars, and political meetings; hundreds of interviews; life histories; census data; and the analysis of newspapers, church records, and business directories. Drake, trained in anthropology at the University of Chicago, brought an ethnographer's attention to everyday life; Cayton, a sociologist who had grown up in Seattle and written for Black newspapers, brought political and institutional knowledge. The authors also drew on the "color-caste" framework associated with W. Lloyd Warner's work, treating race as a caste-like system that cut across class. The result ran to some eight hundred pages, moving from the history of the migration through the economy, the family, the church, and the political machine.

## Key Findings

Drake and Cayton's central finding was that the Black Metropolis was not a disorganised slum but a complex, stratified society. It had an upper class of professionals and businesspeople, a middle class of teachers, clerks, and skilled workers, and a large lower class of labourers and the unemployed — and each class had its own institutions, values, and ways of life. The community's churches were its most powerful institutions, providing not only worship but welfare, social life, and political organisation. Its newspapers, above all the *Chicago Defender*, shaped opinion across the country. Its politics were dominated by a machine that delivered jobs and services in exchange for votes, while also limiting independent Black political power.

The authors also showed how segregation structured everything. The Black Belt's economy depended on white-owned businesses and on jobs controlled by white employers; its housing was overcrowded because white neighbourhoods were closed to Black buyers; and its residents lived with the daily knowledge that their opportunities were limited by race. Yet the book refuses a picture of passive victimhood. It documents ambition, organisation, and cultural vitality, and it argues that the Black Metropolis was both a product of oppression and a base from which Black Chicagoans fought for a fuller place in American life.

The book's portrait of class is especially detailed. Drake and Cayton describe how the upper class — doctors, lawyers, publishers, and successful business owners — set the tone for the community's institutions, while the middle class staffed its schools and churches and the lower class, the largest group, lived closest to the edge of poverty. Each class had its own churches, clubs, and social codes, and movement between them was possible but difficult. The authors also trace the community's internal debates: over respectability, over the role of the church, over whether to pursue integration or to build Black institutions. These tensions, they argue, were signs of a living society rather than a broken one, and they gave the Black Metropolis a political and cultural complexity that outsiders rarely saw.

## Reception and Influence

*Black Metropolis* was recognised immediately as a landmark, and it became a foundational text of urban sociology and African American studies. It shaped the Chicago School's understanding of race and the city, influenced generations of ethnographers, and provided a model for the community study as a whole. Its analysis of segregation, class structure, and institutional life informed later work on the urban ghetto, including William Julius Wilson's work on Chicago's inner city in the 1980s. The book has been reissued by the University of Chicago Press, and it continues to be read as both a historical document of Bronzeville at its height and a living source of ideas about race, community, and the city.
