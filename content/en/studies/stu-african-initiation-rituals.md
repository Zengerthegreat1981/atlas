# African Initiation Rituals and Life Transitions: Ethnographies of Rites of Passage

Initiation rituals have been among the most intensively studied institutions in African ethnography. They mark the passage of young people, and in some societies adults, into a new social status, often through a sequence of separation, instruction, ordeal, and return. This entry reviews the main lines of ethnographic work on them, from early twentieth-century theory to late twentieth-century studies of performance, gender, and change.

## Theoretical Background

The comparative framework was set by Arnold van Gennep in *Les rites de passage* (1909), which described rituals of transition as having three phases: separation, margin (or liminality), and incorporation. Victor Turner developed the middle phase in his studies of the Ndembu of Zambia, collected in *The Forest of Symbols* (1967), emphasizing the ambiguity of initiates, who are "betwixt and between" social categories, and the symbolic richness of ritual objects and actions. Later, Turner and others spoke of the solidarity of initiates, which Turner called communitas.

## Classic Ethnographies

Several ethnographies became reference points. Jomo Kenyatta's *Facing Mount Kenya* (1938) gave an account of Gikuyu initiation, including the circumcision of boys and girls, as the foundation of age-sets and of moral education; it was also a political text written against colonial and missionary attacks on these practices. Audrey Richards's *Chisungu* (1956) recorded in detail a girls' initiation ceremony among the Bemba of what was then Northern Rhodesia, recording songs, sacred emblems, and the teaching given to girls, and interpreting the ceremony as a preparation for marriage and as a means of transmitting knowledge. Maurice Bloch's *From Blessing to Violence* (1986) analyzed Merina circumcision in Madagascar as a ritual in which the ancestral blessing of the elders is joined to a symbolic violence, and he related its changing meanings to Merina history. Corinne Kratz's *Affecting Performance* (1994) studied Okiek women's initiation in Kenya, attending to how participants experience and shape meaning through song, movement, and dress. Jean La Fontaine's *Initiation* (1985) offered a comparative synthesis that treated initiation as a means of creating social persons and, in many cases, of transmitting secret knowledge.

## Findings

Across these studies, several themes recur. Initiation often marks the transition from childhood to adulthood and incorporates initiates into age-sets, associations, or gender categories. It frequently involves instruction in sexuality, marriage, and social obligations, and in ritual knowledge that is restricted to initiates. The typical sequence includes seclusion from ordinary life, a period of teaching, sometimes physical ordeal such as circumcision or other bodily modification, and a return to society with a new status. Anthropologists have also stressed that initiation is not only expressive of social structure but productive of it: through ritual, gender, age, and authority are created and made visible.

## Methods and Limits

Because of restrictions on access, ethnographers, who are often outsiders of different gender or age, have usually observed only some phases of initiation, supplementing this with interviews with initiates and ritual specialists. Richards, a woman, was able to attend the Bemba ceremony in unusual detail, but male ethnographers in many societies could not have done so. Studies of the performances of initiation have since attended to song, dance, and the sensory and emotional experience of participants, not just to the symbolism of ritual objects.

## Change, Critique, and Contemporary Value

Initiation practices have been reshaped by colonial rule, missionary Christianity, schooling, urbanization, and national and international policy. In some places the ritual has been shortened or moved to school holidays, or elements have been dropped. Female genital cutting, which is part of some initiation traditions, has become the subject of intense debate among activists, governments, health agencies, and scholars, who disagree about the weight to be given to cultural meaning, health, bodily integrity, and women's agency. Postcolonial critics have also noted that early ethnographies sometimes presented initiation as timeless tradition, although communities have continued to adapt it. Despite these debates, the ethnography of initiation remains a central source for understanding how societies make persons, transmit knowledge, and mark social change.
