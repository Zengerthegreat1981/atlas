# Boyer and Ramble (2001): Cognitive Templates for Religious Concepts — Cross-Cultural Evidence

An experimental study published by Pascal Boyer with Charles Ramble in the journal *Cognitive Science* in 2001, under the title "Cognitive Templates for Religious Concepts: Cross-Cultural Evidence for Recall of Counter-Intuitive Representations." It extends laboratory work on memory for counterintuitive concepts, conducted mainly with Western participants, to several cultural settings, testing the hypothesis that "minimally counterintuitive" concepts are preserved and recalled from memory with higher accuracy than ordinary intuitive concepts or excessively counterintuitive ones.

## The Cross-Cultural Design

The two researchers tested the hypothesis in three culturally distant contexts, geographically and religiously: France (a Western secular/Catholic background), Gabon (a traditional African context, with Fang participants), and Nepal (a Himalayan context, with data gathered by Ramble, a scholar of Tibetan culture). At each site, participants were presented with lists of concepts (beings and objects) that varied in the degree to which they violated innate ontological expectations, and their recall of them was then measured.

## The Result

The same result recurred across the three cultures despite the complete difference in local religious content. Concepts that violate expectations of their ontological category in a limited way were, on the authors' analysis, better recalled than ordinary intuitive concepts; the authors also argued that the effect depends on the kind of violation and that items with many violations are not recalled better. The authors took this as evidence that the presumed cognitive mechanism is not a peculiar Western cultural feature, but a general human memory bias that partly explains why the structure of religious beings is similar across traditions that have no direct historical contact with each other.

## Methodological Importance

Together with related experimental work such as Barrett and Nyhof (2001), this study formed part of the empirical background to Boyer's central argument in *Religion Explained* (2001): that the spread of religious concepts is a by-product of a general cognitive structure, not a specific evolutionary adaptation for religion.

## Methodological Caution

Cross-cultural experiments in cognitive psychology face the risk of imposing material of Western origin on participants from other cultures, using language or images that do not carry the same meaning for them. The authors addressed this by adapting the test material to each setting; readers should consult the original article for the exact stimulus sets, sample sizes and statistics.

## Later Tests and Critiques

Later research has qualified the recall effect. Gonce and colleagues (2006) found that it depends heavily on context: when counterintuitive items were presented in lists without a supporting narrative frame, they were not reliably remembered better, and could be remembered worse, than intuitive ones. Norenzayan, Atran, Faulkner and Schaller (2006) reported that intuitive items had the advantage in immediate recall, although minimally counterintuitive ones were forgotten less over time; they also found that folktales combining mostly intuitive elements with a few counterintuitive ones were more likely to be culturally successful. Their proposal shifts attention from single concepts to the overall mix within a story. Upal and others have argued that expectations created by context, and the effort to make sense of an item, explain recall better than violations of ontological categories as such. In a critical discussion (2016), Purzycki and Willard argued that the theory's key notions have been defined and operationalized inconsistently, particularly the difference between what violates an innate category expectation and what is merely unusual or bizarre, which makes studies hard to compare. The cross-cultural findings of 2001 are therefore best read as one important result within an unsettled literature.
