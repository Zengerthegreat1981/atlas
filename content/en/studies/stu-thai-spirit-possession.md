# Thai Spirit Possession and Buddhist Practice: Ethnographies of Cosmological Experience

Spirit possession and spirit mediumship are well documented in Thailand, where they exist alongside Theravada Buddhism. Ethnographers have used them to discuss how Thai religion combines elements that earlier scholars classified separately as "Buddhism" and "animism." This entry gives an overview of the main studies and arguments.

## Background

Thai religious life includes the veneration of spirits (*phi*) of ancestors, places and the dead, as well as Buddhist merit-making, monks and amulets. Earlier discussions asked whether Thai religion was a layered or "syncretic" system, with Buddhism overlying older spirit beliefs. Scholars such as Stanley Tambiah, A. Thomas Kirsch and B. J. Terwiel argued instead that Buddhism, Brahmanical ritual and spirit practices form a single, if complex, religious field in which each has its domain.

## Key Studies

- **Tambiah (1970)** studied a village in north-east Thailand and showed that Buddhist monks and spirit specialists (such as spirit doctors and mediums) play complementary roles: monks deal with merit and death, and spirit cults with illness and misfortune in the village. He interpreted them as parts of a single "total field" of religion. He later studied the cult of amulets and forest monks in *The Buddhist Saints of the Forest and the Cult of Amulets* (1984).
- **Terwiel (1975)** described central Thai rural ceremonies and argued that Buddhism, Brahmanical ritual and spirit-related rites form a unified system.
- **Kirsch (1977)** argued that the Thai religious system should be understood as a complex of Buddhist, Brahmanical and spirit elements with different functions, challenging the "syncretism" model.
- **Rosalind Morris (2000)** studied spirit mediums in Chiang Mai in northern Thailand, interpreting the practice as a way in which people respond to modernity, the nation-state and mass media, including mediums who channel spirits associated with the region's past.
- **Justin McDaniel (2011)** studied everyday Buddhist practice, including amulets, ghosts and magical monks, which complicated the contrast between "textual Buddhism" and "popular religion."

## Themes

Possession is generally understood by participants as an encounter with a spirit that temporarily speaks through a medium. Mediums often provide healing, divination and counsel, and many are women. Scholars have interpreted the practice in terms of healing and social support, gender and authority, local history and memory, and responses to economic and political change. Monks and some reformist Buddhists have criticized spirit cults as incompatible with doctrine, while many lay people combine both.

## Critique

Critics have noted that the "syncretism" and "layer" models can impose scholarly categories on lived practice; that functional explanations (for example, possession as therapy or as compensation for women's low status) can reduce the experiences to social functions; and that the scholarly focus on mediums may underrepresent the many Thais who engage with spirits without being possessed.
