# The Meritocracy Trap

*The Meritocracy Trap: How America's Foundational Myth Feeds Inequality, Dismantles the Middle Class, and Devours the Elite* (2019), by the Yale law professor Daniel Markovits, is a sweeping attack on one of the most cherished ideas in American life: that success should go to the talented and hard-working, and that a fair society is one in which merit decides who rises. Markovits argues that meritocracy has not delivered on that promise. Instead, it has become a system that harms almost everyone it touches — the poor, the middle class, and even the winners.

## The Argument

Meritocracy was originally offered as an alternative to aristocracy: where birth once decided a person's fate, talent and effort would decide it instead. Markovits argues that the system has now produced a new kind of aristocracy, one that passes its advantages down through intensive training rather than through titles. Elite families invest enormous resources — in tutoring, test preparation, extracurricular coaching, and private schooling — to give their children the skills that elite universities and elite employers reward. The result is a self-perpetuating caste: children of the affluent dominate the best colleges and the best jobs, while everyone else is told that their failure is their own fault.

## Method

The book is a work of synthesis rather than a single field study. Markovits draws on economic data on income and wealth, research on education and admissions, studies of working hours and occupational structure, and the history of the idea of merit itself. He shows how the returns to education have grown, how the competition for places at elite institutions has intensified, and how the labour market now rewards a narrow set of highly trained skills. He also draws on his own experience teaching at Yale Law School, where he has watched the machinery of meritocratic selection operate at close range.

## Key Findings

Three findings carry the argument. First, meritocratic inequality is driven by education rather than by inherited wealth alone. At the most selective colleges, Markovits notes, there are now more students from the richest 1 percent of families than from the entire bottom half of the income distribution. Second, the middle class is being squeezed out of the competition. Middle-class families cannot match the scale of investment that affluent families pour into their children's training, and their children are increasingly shut out of the institutions that lead to secure professional careers. Third, the winners suffer too. Elite workers now work extraordinarily long hours in high-pressure jobs, and elite children endure a childhood of relentless preparation — what Markovits calls a "meritocratic arms race" that leaves the affluent exhausted, anxious, and estranged from the lives they were supposedly winning.

Markovits calls this the trap: meritocracy justifies inequality by telling the losers they deserve to lose and the winners they deserve to win, while in practice the contest is rigged by unequal starting points. The ideology is more damaging than the old aristocratic order, he argues, because it makes inequality look legitimate.

The book closes with a programme for escaping the trap. Markovits proposes expanding the number of places at elite universities so that admission stops being a lottery for the already privileged, reforming admissions to reduce the weight of expensive preparation, and restructuring work so that mid-skilled jobs once again offer security and dignity. He also argues for changing the tax and labour rules that reward the most intensive forms of elite work. These proposals are deliberately practical: Markovits does not call for abolishing merit, but for rebuilding the institutions that are supposed to reward it. Whether his remedies match the scale of his diagnosis became one of the main points of debate after publication, but the proposals themselves have fed into real discussions about university expansion, admissions reform, and the future of professional work.

## Reception and Influence

*The Meritocracy Trap* was widely reviewed and debated on publication, praised for the force of its argument and for the breadth of evidence it assembles. Supporters welcomed its challenge to a consensus that spans the political spectrum, and its insistence that the problem is not a failure of meritocracy but meritocracy working as designed. Critics argued that Markovits overstates the role of education and understates the role of capital, inheritance, and political power, and that his remedies — expanding elite university places, reforming admissions, and restructuring work — are too modest for the diagnosis. The book appeared alongside Michael Sandel's *The Tyranny of Merit* (2020), and together the two works reopened a public debate about whether merit is a fair basis for distributing rewards at all. Markovits's central term, the "meritocracy trap," has since become a standard reference point in discussions of education, inequality, and the future of work.
